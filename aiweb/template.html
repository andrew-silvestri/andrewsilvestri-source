People still search, but fewer searches end on a website

On 19 February 2024 Alan Antin, an analyst at Gartner, a research firm, predicted that the volume of searches on traditional search engines would fall {{gartner_drop}} percent by 2026, as people put their questions to chatbots instead. In October 2026 Sundar Pichai, Alphabet's chief executive, told investors that Google's “year-over-year query growth rate is the highest it’s been in the last five years”, and search revenue had grown {{goog_growth}} percent in a year (to ${{goog_rev}} billion a quarter). The forecast missed. This page sets five futures commonly predicted for the web against what has been measured so far. The change Gartner was reaching for is still happening, in a different place: fewer searches end with a visit to a website.

How is the map drawn?

Most predictions about the web are claims about two shares: how much of its traffic is software rather than people, and how much of what readers see was written by machines. The map puts the first across and the second up. Four futures sit on it. In the first, the web adapts to AI as it adapted to search engines and phones. In a hollowed web, AI answers stand in for the visit. In an agentic web, software agents browse and buy for their owners. In a synthetic flood, machines write most of what is published and most of what is shown. The fifth future, a closing web, is about access rather than authorship, so it is drawn as a strip below the map. Where each future sits is this page's judgment, placed against today, since none of them has machine traffic or machine writing falling. The markers for today are measurements.

Today sits just past the middle across and low on the map, though how low depends on the measure. Automated software made {{bot_share}} percent of the traffic that Imperva, a security firm, saw on its customers' sites in 2025; Cloudflare, a network company, counted a similar {{cf_machine_html}} percent of requests for web pages as non-human on 2 December 2025, by its own definition (most of either is old-fashioned automation, not AI). AI wrote {{graphite_articles}} percent of new English-language articles in early 2026, in a study by one company using three commercial detectors, but only {{graphite_serp}} percent of the pages Google showed in mid-2025. The arithmetic is short: {{graphite_articles}} divided by {{graphite_serp}} is {{pub_shown_ratio}}, so the AI share of new articles is {{pub_shown_words}} its share of Google's results. Part of that gap is age rather than filtering, since Google's results include pages written before chatbots existed, and the two samples are three quarters apart. The map's vertical axis uses what Google shows, because that is what readers see.

Do searches still end on websites?

The hollowed web is the future news publishers have put a number on: their executives expect search traffic to fall {{ri_pub_drop}} percent within three years. Its mechanism is simple. An AI answer at the top of the results page settles the question, so the reader never visits the pages it was drawn from. Search engines once crawled pages in exchange for sending readers to them. In July 2025 Cloudflare counted about {{crawl_google}} crawls by Google for every visit Google sent back; for OpenAI the figure was {{crawl_openai}}, and for Anthropic {{crawl_anthropic}}. Whatever the old ratio was, a crawler that takes a thousand pages for each reader it returns is not keeping the bargain search engines struck.

The click data point the same way, as a rate. The Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of American adults in March 2025 ({{pew_n}} people, {{pew_searches}} searches). They clicked a result on {{pew_click_ai}} percent of searches that showed an AI summary and on {{pew_click_no}} percent of those that did not, and clicked a link inside the summary on {{pew_click_in}} percent. SparkToro, which studies search behavior, estimates that {{zero_2026}} percent of American Google searches in early 2026 ended with no click at all, up from {{zero_2024}} percent in 2024; that is the high end of the published range. Fewer clicks per search can still mean as many visits, if there are more searches. For news, they do not: Chartbeat, which measures traffic for news sites (more than {{chartbeat_sites}} of them), found Google search traffic to them down {{chartbeat_drop}} percent worldwide in the year to November 2025.

Outside news, the counts disagree. Google's head of search, Liz Reid, wrote in August 2025 that total organic click volume “has been relatively stable year-over-year”, without publishing the data. Graphite, a search-marketing agency, estimated that organic Google traffic to American sites (more than {{graphite_sites}} of them) fell only {{graphite_traffic}} percent in 2025, with the ten largest up {{graphite_top10}} percent on average. In Graphite's data the losses gather where an answer can replace the visit: news, health and recipes. In Chartbeat's count they also gather on small publishers, which lost {{chartbeat_small}} percent of their search traffic against {{chartbeat_large}} percent for large ones. Wikipedia, which relies on visits for its donors and volunteers, counted about {{wiki_drop}} percent fewer human page views in recent months of 2025 than in the same months of 2024, after it reclassified some traffic as bots. The visit is disappearing first from the sites whose pages make the best answers.

Evidence: strong that fewer searches end in a visit, and that news sites get fewer visits; moderate on how much of the drop AI answers cause. Most of the data come from browsing panels and from firms that sell traffic services, and other changes to search happened at the same time.

Are machines becoming the web's main readers?

The agentic web goes one step further: software agents, not people, do the browsing. In 2025 Microsoft announced what it called the open agentic web, Google published a protocol for agents to make payments, and OpenAI published one for agents to buy inside ChatGPT. The Model Context Protocol, Anthropic's standard for connecting AI models to outside tools, had more than {{mcp_servers}} public servers by December 2025. McKinsey, a consultancy, projects that agents could arrange ${{mck_lo}} trillion to ${{mck_hi}} trillion of commerce by 2030, a forecast with nothing yet measured against it.

Software already makes most of the web's requests. Automated software made {{bot_share}} percent of the traffic Imperva saw in 2025, up from {{bot_share_prev}} percent a year before. Most of it is old-fashioned automation: scrapers, scanners, attack tools and search crawlers. Declared AI crawlers made {{cf_ai_bots}} percent of requests for web pages on Cloudflare's network in 2025, about as many as Google's own crawler at {{cf_googlebot}} percent. Agents working for a person are a small slice of that traffic, and no two vendors yet count them the same way. In October 2026 HUMAN Security, which tracks them, found that {{agent_browse}} percent of agent requests went to product and search pages and {{agent_checkout}} percent reached a checkout or payment page, a share of requests rather than of tasks.

Buying is the weak link. OpenAI launched Instant Checkout, purchases inside ChatGPT, in September 2025, and removed it in March 2026, sending checkout to merchants' own apps and sites in a change it called temporary. In a 2025 Forrester survey, {{forrester_trust}} percent of American online adults said they would trust an agent to make routine purchases for them. Agents browse. People still pay.

Evidence: strong that software makes most requests; weak that agents act for people at scale. Every traffic share comes from a company that sells bot management, and each defines a bot its own way.

Is the web closing its doors?

The closing web is a reaction to the other two futures. A site that loses visitors to AI answers has less reason to let AI companies read it. In 2024 Shayne Longpre and colleagues at the Data Provenance Initiative audited the websites behind three large AI training sets. In a single year, instructions to crawlers in robots.txt files had come to restrict {{dpi_after_lo}} to {{dpi_after_hi}} percent of the text, up from about {{dpi_before}} percent; for the most important sources the restricted share was {{dpi_head_lo}} to {{dpi_head_hi}} percent.

News publishers moved fastest. By late 2025 about {{news_block}} percent of the top news sites in the United States and Britain blocked at least one AI training crawler, according to BuzzStream, a marketing firm. Across the million most-visited websites, about {{top1m_block}} percent fully blocked OpenAI's crawler in September 2025, a narrower measure that explains part of the gap. Closing came with a price list. By one count, {{deals}} licensing deals between publishers and AI companies were announced or reported between January 2023 and June 2026. News Corp's deal with OpenAI is reported at about ${{newscorp}} million over five years; Amazon's with The New York Times at ${{nyt_lo}} million to ${{nyt_hi}} million a year, about {{nyt_share}} percent of the paper's revenue.

The gates leak. TollBit, which sells licensing tools to publishers, found that about {{tollbit}} percent of AI bot visits in late 2025 ignored a site's robots.txt. By October 2026 there were {{lawsuits}} copyright suits against AI companies in the United States. The largest result so far, Anthropic's ${{bartz}} billion settlement with book authors, turned on pirated books rather than crawled web pages, so the courts have yet to say what a robots.txt file is worth.

Evidence: strong for news sites, weak for the web as a whole. Most of the million largest sites still let AI crawlers in, and a robots.txt file is a request, not a lock.

How much of the web do machines now write?

The best-known number about the synthetic flood is a guess. So where did it come from? A 2022 report by Europol's Innovation Lab said that experts estimated as much as {{europol}} percent of online content may be synthetically generated by 2026. Its only source was a 2020 book by Nina Schick, a writer on deepfakes, who said in a 2023 interview that the figure “might” be reached by 2025. No survey, model or measurement stood behind it. It is still often quoted as a Europol forecast.

The measured share is lower, and more interesting. Graphite, the agency that studied search traffic, ran a sample of new English-language articles through three commercial AI detectors. It found that {{graphite_articles}} percent were mostly machine-written in early 2026, a share that has stayed near half for {{graphite_flat_words}} quarters. In mid-2025 such pages made up only {{graphite_serp}} percent of Google's results and {{graphite_top}} percent of its first results. NewsGuard, which rates news sites, counted {{ng_2023}} websites publishing AI-generated news with little human oversight in May 2023 and {{ng_2026}} by June 2026, about {{ng_ratio}} times as many (a lower bound, since it counts only the sites it has found).

The flood also runs back into the machines. In 2024 Ilia Shumailov and colleagues showed in Nature that models trained again and again on their own output lose the rare cases first, and then degrade. In the same paper, keeping some of the original human-written data in each round prevented most of the damage. A web that keeps its record of human writing is worth more to AI companies, not less.

Evidence: moderate. Both measured shares come from one agency's samples of Common Crawl, which over-represents low-value pages, and from commercial detectors that misread a small fraction of human text as machine-written.

Or does the web adapt, as it did before?

The quietest forecast is that the web absorbs AI as it absorbed search engines, social networks and phones. Google's business supports it. Search revenue reached ${{goog_rev}} billion in the third quarter of 2026, and google.com received {{g_visits}} billion visits in October 2026 against {{c_visits}} billion for chatgpt.com, about {{g_c_ratio}} times as many, on Similarweb's estimates for the web alone (chatbot apps, where much chatbot use happens, are left out). Across six countries surveyed by the Reuters Institute, weekly use of standalone AI tools rose from {{ri_ai_2025}} to {{ri_ai_2026}} percent of online adults in a year, and across nine, {{ri_saw}} percent had seen AI answers inside a search engine in the previous week. On these figures, people use chatbots in addition to search, not instead of it.

But adaptation describes the gatekeepers better than the web behind them. Google keeps its users by answering more of their questions itself. The sites that supplied the answers see fewer of those users. AI tools have not filled the gap: they sent under {{ai_ref_share}} percent of publishers' page views in Chartbeat's data to early 2026.

Evidence: strong that search remains the web's front door; weak that the sites behind it keep their traffic.

What do forecasters expect?

Experts asked about AI in general are divided, and neither survey below asked about the web. Elon University canvassed {{elon_n}} experts at the turn of 2025, not a random sample, on how AI would change being human by 2035: {{elon_better}} percent expected mostly better, {{elon_worse}} percent mostly worse, and {{elon_mixed}} percent an equal measure of both. In a 2024 Pew survey, {{pew_exp}} percent of AI experts expected AI to have a positive effect on the United States over the next 20 years, against {{pew_pub}} percent of the public. The two loud forecasts, a collapse in search and a web of synthetic text, named years that have now arrived, or nearly, without them. The quieter shift, from visits to answers, was forecast mainly by the publishers losing the visits. Their forecast is the one still open.

Evidence: weak for the surveys, which asked about AI rather than the web; strong for the table's verdicts on Gartner and Europol.

What would settle it?

Three numbers will show which future is arriving. If AI tools start to send publishers visits on the scale search does, against under {{ai_ref_share}} percent of page views now, the hollowed web is being repaired. If agents move from browsing to buying, with well over the {{agent_checkout}} percent of their requests that now reach a checkout ending in a purchase, the agentic web has begun. And if the AI share of what Google shows climbs from {{graphite_serp}} percent toward the {{graphite_articles}} percent of what is published, the flood has reached the front page. So far the web has kept its searchers, and fewer of their searches end on it.

Method

Figures. Every number on this page is copied from the report, filing or survey cited beside it, as read on {{as_of_words}}, and listed with its source in the download. Where sources disagree, the page gives the measure closest to the claim and names it. Zero-click shares, for example, run from about a quarter of searches ({{zero_datos}} percent on a desktop-only panel in March 2025) to about two-thirds (SparkToro's {{zero_2026}} percent), depending on which devices and which searches are counted. Ratios and growth rates are computed from the published values by the build script, and rounded half up.

The map. The axes run from 0 to 100 percent. Across is the share of web requests made by software, which today's marker takes from Imperva's 2025 data; Cloudflare's share of non-human requests for web pages, on one day in December 2025 and by a different definition, is similar. Up is the share of what readers are shown that machines wrote, which today's marker takes from Graphite's sample of Google results in mid-2025; the second, smaller square is the AI share of new English articles in early 2026, from the same agency. The placement of each future is this page's judgment of where its predictors put the end state, and each is placed at or beyond today on both axes. The strip is the share of sites that block AI training crawlers in two samples that measure different things.

Limits

Almost every measure of web traffic comes from a company with something to sell: bot protection, search marketing, licensing or analytics. Their samples are their own customers, and their definitions of a bot, a click or an AI-written article differ. AI detectors misclassify some human writing and miss some machine writing, and none of them measures text that a person drafted with AI and then edited. Requests are not readers: one person's question can send an agent to dozens of pages, so the map's horizontal axis overstates how much of the web machines read. The forecasts in the table were picked because each named a number and a date; most predictions about the web name neither.
