3733	
3734	It being our determination to discourse of Natural Philosophy, we
3735	judge it necessary, in the first place and chiefly, to divide the body
3736	of philosophy into its proper members, that we may know what is that
3737	which is called philosophy, and what part of it is physical, or the
3738	explanation of natural things. The Stoics affirm that wisdom is the
3739	knowledge of things human and divine; that philosophy is the exercise
3740	of that art which is expedient to this knowledge; that virtue is the
3741	sole and sovereign art which is thus expedient; and this distributes
3742	itself into three general parts,—natural, moral, and logical. By
3743	which just reason (they say) philosophy is tripartite; of which one
3744	is natural, the other moral, the third logical. The natural is when
3745	our enquiries are concerning the world and all things contained in
3746	it; the ethical is the employment of our minds in those things which
3747	concern the manners of man’s life; the logical (which they also call
3748	dialectical) regulates our conversation with others in speaking.
3749	Aristotle, Theophrastus, and after them almost all the Peripatetics
3750	give the following division of philosophy. It is absolutely requisite
3751	that the complete person be contemplator of things which have a being,
3752	and the practiser of those things which are decent; and this easily
3753	appears by the following instances. If the question be proposed,
3754	whether the sun, which is so conspicuous to us, be informed with a
3755	soul or inanimate, he that makes this disquisition is the thinking
3756	man; for he proceeds no farther than to consider the nature of that
3757	thing which is proposed. Likewise, if the question be proposed,
3758	whether the world be infinite, or whether beyond the system of this
3759	world there is any real being, all these things are the objects about
3760	which the understanding of man is conversant. But if these be the
3761	questions,—what measures must be taken to compose the well ordered
3762	life of man, what are the best methods to govern and educate children,
3763	or what are the exact rules whereby sovereigns may command and
3764	establish laws,—all these queries are proposed for the sole end of
3765	action, and the man conversant therein is the moral and practical man.

3770	CHAPTER I.
3771	
3772	WHAT IS NATURE?
3773	
3774	
3775	Since we have undertaken to make a diligent search into Nature, I
3776	cannot but conclude it necessary to declare what Nature is. It is very
3777	absurd to attempt a discourse of the essence of natural things, and
3778	not to understand what is the power and sphere of Nature. If Aristotle
3779	be credited, Nature is the principle of motion and rest, in that thing
3780	in which it exists principally and not by accident. For all things
3781	that are conspicuous to our eyes, which are neither fortuitous nor
3782	necessary, nor have a divine original, nor acknowledge any such like
3783	cause, are called natural and enjoy their proper nature. Of this sort
3784	are earth, fire, water, air, plants, animals; to these may be added all
3785	things produced from them, such as showers, hail, thunders, hurricanes,
3786	and winds. All these confess they had a beginning, none of these were
3787	from eternity, but had something as the origin of them; and likewise
3788	animals and plants have a principle whence they are produced. But
3789	Nature, which in all these things hath the priority, is the principle
3790	not only of motion but of repose; whatsoever enjoys the principle of
3791	motion, the same has a possibility to find a dissolution. Therefore on
3792	this account it is that Nature is the principle of motion and rest.

3797	CHAPTER II.
3798	
3799	WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A PRINCIPLE AND AN ELEMENT?
3800	
3801	
3802	The followers of Aristotle and Plato conclude that the elements are
3803	discriminated from a principle. Thales the Milesian supposeth that
3804	a principle and the elements are one and the same thing, but it is
3805	evident that they vastly differ one from another. For the elements
3806	are things compounded; but we do pronounce that principles admit not
3807	of a composition, nor are the effects of any other being. Those which
3808	we call elements are earth, water, air, and fire. But we term those
3809	principles which have nothing precedent to them out of which they are
3810	produced; for otherwise not these themselves, but rather those things
3811	whereof they are produced, would be the principles. Now there are
3812	some things which have a pre-existence to earth and water, from which
3813	they are begotten; to wit, matter, which is without form or shape;
3814	then form, which we call ἐντελέχεια (_actuality_); and lastly,
3815	privation. Thales therefore is very peccant, by affirming that water is
3816	both an element and a principle.

3821	CHAPTER III.
3822	
3823	OF PRINCIPLES, AND WHAT THEY ARE.
3824	
3825	
3826	Thales the Milesian doth affirm that water is the principle whence all
3827	things in the universe spring. This person appears to be the first of
3828	philosophers; from him the Ionic sect took its denomination, for there
3829	are many families and successions amongst philosophers. After he had
3830	professed philosophy in Egypt, when he was very old, he returned to
3831	Miletus. He pronounced, that all things had their original from water,
3832	and into water all things are resolved. His first reason was, that
3833	whatsoever was the prolific seed of all animals was a principle, and
3834	that is moist; so that it is probable that all things receive their
3835	original from humidity. His second reason was, that all plants are
3836	nourished and fructified by that thing which is moist, of which being
3837	deprived they wither away. Thirdly, that that fire of which the sun and
3838	stars are made is nourished by watery exhalations,—yea, and the world
3839	itself; which moved Homer to sing that the generation of it was from
3840	water:—
3841	
3842	                        The ocean is
3843	  Of all things the kind genesis.[36]
3844	
3845	Anaximander, who himself was a Milesian, assigns the principle of all
3846	things to the Infinite, from whence all things flow, and into the same
3847	are corrupted; hence it is that infinite worlds are framed, and those
3848	vanish again into that whence they have their original. And thus he
3849	farther proceeds, For what other reason is there of an Infinite but
3850	this, that there may be nothing deficient as to the generation or
3851	subsistence of what is in nature? There is his error, that he doth not
3852	acquaint us what this Infinite is, whether it be air, or water, or
3853	earth, or any other such like body. Besides he is peccant, in that,
3854	giving us the material cause, he is silent as to the efficient cause of
3855	beings; for this thing which he makes his Infinite can be nothing but
3856	matter; but operation cannot take place in the sphere of matter, except
3857	an efficient cause be annexed.
3858	
3859	Anaximenes his fellow-citizen pronounceth, that air is the principle
3860	of all beings; from it all receive their original, and into it all
3861	return. He affirms that our soul is nothing but air; it is that which
3862	constitutes and preserves; the whole world is invested with spirit
3863	and air. For spirit and air are synonymous. This person is in this
3864	deficient, that he concludes that of pure air, which is a simple body
3865	and is made of one only form, all animals are composed. It is not
3866	possible to think that a single principle should be the matter of all
3867	things, from whence they receive their subsistence; besides this there
3868	must be an operating cause. Silver (for example) is not of itself
3869	sufficient to frame a drinking cup; an operator also is required, which
3870	is the silversmith. The like may be applied to vessels made of wood,
3871	brass, or any other material.
3872	
3873	Anaxagoras the Clazomenian asserted Homoeomeries (or parts similar or
3874	homogeneous) to be the original cause of all beings; it seemed to him
3875	impossible that any thing could arise of nothing or be resolved into
3876	nothing. Let us therefore instance in nourishment, which appears simple
3877	and uniform, such as bread which we owe to Ceres, and water which we
3878	drink. Of this very nutriment, our hair, our veins, our arteries,
3879	nerves, bones, and all our other parts are nourished. These things
3880	thus being performed, it must be granted that the nourishment which
3881	is received by us contains all those things by which these parts of
3882	us are increased. In it there are those particles which are producers
3883	of blood, bones, nerves, and all other parts; which particles (as he
3884	thought) reason discovers for us. For it is not necessary that we
3885	should reduce all things under the objects of sense; for bread and
3886	water are fitted to the senses, yet in them there are those particles
3887	latent which are discoverable only by reason. It being therefore
3888	evident that there are particles in the nourishment similar to what is
3889	produced thereby, he terms these homogeneous parts, averring that they
3890	are the principles of beings. Matter is according to him these similar
3891	parts, and the efficient cause is a Mind, which orders all things
3892	that have an existence. Thus he begins his discourse: “All things
3893	were confused one among another; but Mind divided and reduced them to
3894	order.” In this he is to be commended, that he yokes together matter
3895	and an intellectual agent.
3896	
3897	Archelaus the son of Apollodorus, the Athenian, pronounceth, that the
3898	principles of all things have their original from an infinite air
3899	rarefied or condensed. Air rarefied is fire, condensed is water.
3900	
3901	These philosophers, the followers of Thales, succeeding one another,
3902	made up that sect which takes to itself the denomination of the Ionic.
3903	
3904	       *       *       *       *       *
3905	
3906	Pythagoras the Samian, the son of Mnesarchus, from another origin
3907	deduces the principles of all things; it was he who first gave
3908	philosophy its name. He assigns the first principles to be numbers,
3909	and those symmetries resulting from them which he styles harmonies;
3910	and the result of both combined he terms elements, called geometrical.
3911	Again, he enumerates unity and the indefinite binary number amongst the
3912	principles. One of these principles tends to an efficient and forming
3913	cause, which is Mind, and that is God; the other to the passive and
3914	material part, and that is the visible world. Moreover the nature of
3915	number (he saith) consists in the ten; for all people, whether Grecians
3916	or barbarians, reckon from one to ten, and thence return to one
3917	again. Farther he avers the virtue of ten consists in the quaternion;
3918	the reason whereof is this,—if any person reckon from one, and by
3919	addition place his numbers so as to take in the quaternary, he shall
3920	complete the number ten; if he exceed the four, he shall go beyond
3921	the ten; for one, two, three, and four being cast up together make
3922	up ten. The nature of numbers, therefore, if we regard the units,
3923	resteth in the ten; but if we regard its power, in the four. Therefore
3924	the Pythagoreans say that their most sacred oath is by that God who
3925	delivered to them the quaternary.
3926	
3927	  By th’ founder of the sacred number four,
3928	  Eternal Nature’s font and root, they swore.
3929	
3930	Of this number the soul of man is composed; for mind, knowledge,
3931	opinion, and sense are the four that complete the soul, from which all
3932	sciences, all arts, all rational faculties derive themselves. For what
3933	our mind perceives, it perceives after the manner of a thing that is
3934	one, the soul itself being a unity; as for instance, a multitude of
3935	persons are not the object of our sense nor are comprehended by us, for
3936	they are infinite; our understanding gives the general notion of _a
3937	man_, in which all individuals agree. The number of individuals is
3938	infinite; the generic or specific nature of all being is a unit, or
3939	to be apprehended as one only thing; from this one conception we give
3940	the genuine measures of all existence, and therefore we affirm that
3941	a certain class of beings are rational and discoursive beings. But
3942	when we come to give the nature of a horse, it is that animal which
3943	neighs; and this being common to all horses, it is manifest that the
3944	understanding, which hath such like conceptions, is in its nature
3945	unity. The number which is called the infinite binary must needs be
3946	science; in every demonstration or belief belonging to science, and in
3947	every syllogism, we draw that conclusion which is the question doubted
3948	of, from those propositions which are by all granted, by which means
3949	another proposition is demonstrated. The comprehension of these we call
3950	knowledge; for which reason science is the binary number. But opinion
3951	is the ternary; for that rationally follows from comprehension. The
3952	objects of opinion are many things, and the ternary number denotes a
3953	multitude, as “Thrice happy Grecians;” for which reason Pythagoras
3954	admits the ternary. This sect of philosophers is called the Italic, by
3955	reason Pythagoras opened his school in Italy; his hatred of the tyranny
3956	of Polycrates enforced him to leave his native country Samos.
3957	
3958	Heraclitus and Hippasus of Metapontum suppose that fire gives the
3959	origination to all beings, that they all flow from fire, and in fire
3960	they all conclude; for of fire when first quenched the world was
3961	constituted. The first part of the world, being most condensed and
3962	contracted within itself, made the earth; but part of that earth being
3963	loosened and made thin by fire, water was produced; afterwards this
3964	water being exhaled and rarefied into vapors became air; after all this
3965	the world itself, and all other corporeal beings, shall be dissolved by
3966	fire in the universal conflagration. By them therefore it appears that
3967	fire is what gives beginning to all things, and is that in which all
3968	things receive their period.
3969	
3970	Epicurus the son of Neocles, the Athenian, his philosophical sentiments
3971	being the same with those of Democritus, affirms that the principles of
3972	all being are bodies which are perceptible only by reason; they admit
3973	not of a vacuity, nor of any original, but being of a self-existence
3974	are eternal and incorruptible; they are not liable to any diminution,
3975	they are indestructible, nor is it possible for them to receive any
3976	transformation of parts, or admit of any alterations; of these reason
3977	only is the discoverer; they are in a perpetual motion in vacuity,
3978	and by means of the empty space; for the vacuum itself is infinite,
3979	and the bodies that move in it are infinite. Those bodies acknowledge
3980	these three accidents, figure, magnitude, and gravity. Democritus
3981	acknowledged but two, magnitude and figure. Epicurus added the third,
3982	to wit, gravity; for he pronounced that it is necessary that bodies
3983	receive their motion from that impression which springs from gravity,
3984	otherwise they could not be moved. The figures of atoms cannot be
3985	apprehended by our senses, but they are not infinite. These figures
3986	are neither hooked nor trident-shaped nor ring-shaped, such figures as
3987	these being easily broken; but the atoms are impassible, impenetrable;
3988	they have indeed figures proper to themselves, which are discovered
3989	only by reason. It is called an atom, by reason not of its smallness
3990	but of its indivisibility; in it no vacuity, no passible affection is
3991	to be found. And that there is an atom is perfectly clear; for there
3992	are elements which have a perpetual duration, and there are animals
3993	which admit of a vacuity, and there is a unity.
3994	
3995	Empedocles the Agrigentine, the son of Meton, affirms that there are
3996	four elements, fire, air, earth, and water, and two powers which bear
3997	the greatest command in nature, concord and discord, of which one is
3998	the union, the other the division of beings. Thus he sings,
3999	
4000	  Mark the four roots of all created things:—
4001	  Bright shining Jove, Juno that giveth life,
4002	  Pluto beneath the earth, and Nestis who
4003	  Doth with her tears supply the mortal fount.
4004	
4005	By Jupiter he means fire and aether, by Juno that gives life he means
4006	the air, by Pluto the earth, by Nestis and the fountain of all mortals
4007	(as it were) seed and water.
4008	
4009	Socrates the son of Sophroniscus, and Plato son of Ariston, both
4010	natives of Athens, entertain the same opinion concerning the universe;
4011	for they suppose three principles, God, matter, and the idea. God
4012	is the universal understanding; matter is that which is the first
4013	substratum, accommodated for the generation and corruption of beings;
4014	the idea is an incorporeal essence, existing in the cogitations and
4015	apprehensions of God; for God is the soul and mind of the world.
4016	
4017	Aristotle the son of Nichomachus, the Stagirite, constitutes three
4018	principles; Entelecheia (which is the same with form), matter, and
4019	privation. He acknowledges four elements, and adds a certain fifth
4020	body, which is ethereal and not obnoxious to mutation.
4021	
4022	Zeno son of Mnaseas, the native of Citium, avers these principles to be
4023	God and matter, the first of which is the efficient cause, the other
4024	the passible and receptive. Four elements he likewise confesses.

4029	CHAPTER IV.
4030	
4031	HOW WAS THIS WORLD COMPOSED IN THAT ORDER AND AFTER THAT MANNER IT IS?
4032	
4033	
4034	The world being broken and confused, after this manner it was reduced
4035	into figure and composure as now it is. The insectible bodies or
4036	atoms, by a wild and fortuitous motion, without any governing power,
4037	incessantly and swiftly were hurried one amongst another, many bodies
4038	being jumbled together; upon this account they have a diversity in
4039	the figures and magnitude. These therefore being so jumbled together,
4040	those bodies which were the greatest and heaviest sank into the lowest
4041	place; they that were of a lesser magnitude, being round, smooth,
4042	and slippery, meeting with those heavier bodies were easily broken
4043	into pieces, and were carried into higher places. But when that force
4044	whereby these variously figured particles fought with and struck one
4045	another, and forced the lighter upwards, did cease, and there was no
4046	farther power left to drive them into superior regions, yet they were
4047	wholly hindered from descending downwards, and were compelled to reside
4048	in those places capable to receive them; and these were the heavenly
4049	spaces, unto which a multitude of these little bodies were whirled,
4050	and these being thus shivered fell into coherence and mutual embraces,
4051	and by this means the heaven was produced. Then a various and great
4052	multitude of atoms enjoying the same nature, as it is before asserted,
4053	being hurried aloft, did form the stars. The multitude of these
4054	exhaled bodies, having struck and broke the air in shivers, forced a
4055	passage through it; this being converted into wind invested the stars,
4056	as it moved, and whirled them about, by which means to this present
4057	time that circulary motion which these stars have in the heavens is
4058	maintained. Much after the same manner the earth was made; for by those
4059	little particles whose gravity made them to reside in the lower places
4060	the earth was formed. The heaven, fire, and air were constituted of
4061	those particles which were carried aloft. But a great deal of matter
4062	remaining in the earth, this being condensed by the forcible driving
4063	of the winds and the breathings from the stars, every little part and
4064	form of it was broken in pieces, which produced the element of water;
4065	but this being fluidly disposed did run into those places which were
4066	hollow, and these places were those that were capable to receive and
4067	protect it; or else the water, subsisting by itself, did make the lower
4068	places hollow. After this manner the principal parts of the world were
4069	constituted.

4074	CHAPTER V.
4075	
4076	WHETHER THE UNIVERSE IS ONE.
4077	
4078	
4079	The Stoics pronounce that the world is one thing, and this they say is
4080	the universe and is corporeal.
4081	
4082	Empedocles’s opinion is, that the world is one; yet by no means the
4083	system of this world must be styled the universe, but that it is a
4084	small part of it, and the remainder is idle matter.
4085	
4086	What to Plato seems the truest he thus declares, that there is one
4087	world, and that world is the universe; and this he endeavors to evince
4088	by three arguments. First, that the world could not be complete and
4089	perfect, if it did not within itself include all beings. Secondly,
4090	nor could it give the true resemblance of its original and exemplar,
4091	if it were not the one only begotten thing. Thirdly, it could not be
4092	incorruptible, if there were any being out of its compass to whose
4093	power it might be obnoxious. But to Plato it may be thus returned.
4094	First, that the world is not complete and perfect, nor doth it contain
4095	all things within itself. And if man is a perfect being, yet he doth
4096	not encompass all things. Secondly, that there are many exemplars and
4097	originals of statues, houses, and pictures. Thirdly, how is the world
4098	perfect, if any thing beyond it is possible to be moved about it? But
4099	the world is not incorruptible, nor can it be so conceived, because it
4100	had an original.
4101	
4102	To Metrodorus it seems absurd, that in a large field one only stalk
4103	should grow, and in an infinite space one only world exist; and that
4104	this universe is infinite is manifest by this, that there are causes
4105	infinite. Now if this world were finite and the causes which produced
4106	it infinite, it is necessary that the worlds likewise be infinite; for
4107	where all causes do concur, there the effects also must appear, let the
4108	causes be what they will, either atoms or elements.

4113	CHAPTER VI.
4114	
4115	WHENCE DID MEN OBTAIN THE KNOWLEDGE OF THE EXISTENCE AND ESSENCE OF A
4116	DEITY?
4117	
4118	
4119	The Stoics thus define the essence of a God. It is a spirit
4120	intellectual and fiery, which acknowledges no shape, but is continually
4121	changed into what it pleases, and assimilates itself to all things. The
4122	knowledge of this Deity they first received from the pulchritude of
4123	those things which so visibly appeared to us; for they concluded that
4124	nothing beauteous could casually or fortuitously be formed, but that
4125	it was framed from the art of a great understanding that produced the
4126	world. That the world is very resplendent is made perspicuous from the
4127	figure, the color, the magnitude of it, and likewise from the wonderful
4128	variety of those stars which adorn this world. The world is spherical;
4129	the orbicular hath the pre-eminence above all other figures, for being
4130	round itself it hath its parts likewise round. (On this account,
4131	according to Plato, the understanding, which is the most sacred part
4132	of man, is in the head.) The color of it is most beauteous; for it is
4133	painted with blue; which, though little blacker than purple, yet hath
4134	such a shining quality, that by reason of the vehement efficacy of its
4135	color it cuts through such an interval of air; whence it is that at so
4136	great a distance the heavens are to be contemplated. And in this very
4137	greatness of the world the beauty of it appears. View all things: that
4138	which contains the rest carries a beauty with it, as an animal or a
4139	tree. Also all things which are visible to us accomplish the beauty of
4140	the world. The oblique circle called the Zodiac in the heaven is with
4141	different images painted and distinguished:
4142	
4143	  There’s Cancer, Leo, Virgo, and the Claws;
4144	  Scorpio, Arcitenens, and Capricorn;
4145	  Amphora, Pisces, then the Ram, and Bull;
4146	  The lovely pair of Brothers next succeed.[37]
4147	
4148	There are a thousand others that give us the suitable reflections of
4149	the beauty of the world. Thus Euripides:
4150	
4151	  The starry splendor of the skies,
4152	  The wondrous work of that most wise
4153	  Creator, Time.[38]
4154	
4155	From this the knowledge of a God is conveyed to man; that the sun, the
4156	moon, and the rest of the stars, being carried under the earth, rise
4157	again in their proper color, magnitude, place, and times. Therefore
4158	they who by tradition delivered to us the knowledge and veneration of
4159	the Gods did it by these three manner of ways:—first, from Nature;
4160	secondly, from fables; thirdly, from the testimony given by the laws
4161	of commonwealths. Philosophers taught the natural way; poets, the
4162	fabulous; and the political way is received from the constitutions of
4163	each commonwealth. All sorts of this learning are distinguished into
4164	these seven parts. The first is from things that are conspicuous, and
4165	the observation of those bodies which are in places superior to us. To
4166	men the heavenly bodies that are so visible did give the knowledge of
4167	the Deity; when they contemplated that they are the causes of so great
4168	an harmony, that they regulate day and night, winter and summer, by
4169	their rising and setting, and likewise considered those things which
4170	by their influences in the earth do receive a being and do likewise
4171	fructify. It was manifest to men that the Heaven was the father of
4172	those things, and the Earth the mother; that the Heaven was the father
4173	is clear, since from the heavens there is the pouring down of waters,
4174	which have their spermatic faculty; the Earth the mother, because she
4175	receives them and brings forth. Likewise men considering that the stars
4176	are running (θέοντες) in a perpetual motion, that the sun and moon give
4177	us the power to view and contemplate (θεωρεῖν), they call them all Gods
4178	(θεούς).
4179	
4180	In the second and third place, they thus distinguished the Deities into
4181	those which are beneficial and those that are injurious to mankind.
4182	Those which are beneficial they call Jupiter, Juno, Mercury, Ceres;
4183	those who are mischievous the Dirae, Furies, and Mars. These, which
4184	threaten dangers and violence, men endeavor to appease and conciliate
4185	by sacred rites. The fourth and the fifth order of Gods they assign to
4186	things and passions; to passions, Love, Venus, and Desire; the Deities
4187	that preside over things, Hope, Justice, and Eunomia.
4188	
4189	The sixth order of deities are those made by the poets; Hesiod, willing
4190	to find out a father for those Gods that acknowledge an original,
4191	invented their progenitors,
4192	
4193	  Hyperion, Coeus, and Iapetus,
4194	  With Creius:[39]
4195	
4196	upon which account this is called the fabulous. The seventh rank of
4197	the deities added to the rest are those which, by their beneficence
4198	to mankind, were honored with a divine worship, though they were
4199	born of mortal race; of this sort were Hercules, Castor and Pollux,
4200	and Bacchus. These are reputed to be of a human species; for of all
4201	beings that which is divine is most excellent, and man amongst all
4202	animals is adorned with the greatest beauty, and is also the best,
4203	being distinguished by virtue above the rest because of his intellect:
4204	therefore it was thought that those who were admirable for goodness
4205	should resemble that which is the best and most beautiful.
4206	
4207	
4208	
4209	
4210	CHAPTER VII.
4211	
4212	WHAT IS GOD?
4213	
4214	
4215	Some of the philosophers, such as Diagoras the Melian, Theodorus the
4216	Cyrenean, and Euemerus the Tegeatan, did unanimously deny there were
4217	any Gods; and Callimachus the Cyrenean discovered his mind touching
4218	Euemerus in these Iambic verses, thus writing:
4219	
4220	  To th’ ante-mural temple flock apace,
4221	  Where he that long ago composed of brass[40]
4222	  Great Jupiter, Thrasonic old bald pate,
4223	  Now writes his impious books,—a boastful ass!
4224	
4225	meaning books which denote there are no Gods. Euripides the tragedian
4226	durst not openly declare his sentiment; the court of Areopagus
4227	terrified him. Yet he sufficiently manifested his thoughts by this
4228	method. He presented in his tragedy Sisyphus, the first and great
4229	patron of this opinion, and introduced himself as one agreeing with him:
4230	
4231	  Disorder in those days did domineer,
4232	  And brutal power kept the world in fear.
4233	
4234	Afterwards by the sanction of laws wickedness was suppressed; but by
4235	reason that laws could prohibit only public villanies, yet could not
4236	hinder many persons from acting secret impieties, some wise persons
4237	gave this advice, that we ought to blind truth with lying disguises,
4238	and to persuade men that there is a God:
4239	
4240	  There’s an eternal God does hear and see
4241	  And understand every impiety;
4242	  Though it in dark recess or thought committed be.
4243	
4244	But this poetical fable ought to be rejected, he thought, together with
4245	Callimachus, who thus saith:
4246	
4247	  If you believe a God, it must be meant
4248	  That you conceive this God omnipotent.
4249	
4250	But God cannot do every thing; for, if it were so, then God could
4251	make snow black, and the fire cold, and him that is in a posture of
4252	sitting to be upright, and so on the contrary. The brave-speaking Plato
4253	pronounceth that God formed the world after his own image; but this
4254	smells rank of the old dotages, old comic poets would say; for how did
4255	God, casting his eye upon himself, frame this universe? Or how can God
4256	be spherical, and not be inferior to man?
4257	
4258	Anaxagoras avers that bodies did consist from all eternity, but the
4259	divine intellect did reduce them into their proper orders, and effected
4260	the origination of all beings. Plato did not suppose that the primary
4261	bodies had their consistence and repose, but that they were moved
4262	confusedly and in disorder; but God, knowing that order was better
4263	than confusion, did digest them into the best methods. Both these were
4264	equally peccant; for both suppose God to be the great moderator of
4265	human affairs, and for that cause to have formed this present world;
4266	when it is apparent that an immortal and blessed being, replenished
4267	with all his glorious excellencies, and not at all obnoxious to any
4268	sort of evil, but being wholly occupied with his own felicity and
4269	immortality, would not employ himself with the concerns of men; for
4270	certainly miserable is the being which, like a laborer or artificer,
4271	is molested by the troubles and cares which the forming and governing
4272	of this world must give him. Add to this, that the God whom these men
4273	profess was either not at all existing previous to this present world
4274	(when bodies were either reposed or in a disordered motion), or that
4275	then God did either sleep, or else was in a perpetual watchfulness, or
4276	that he did neither of these. Now neither the first nor the second can
4277	be entertained, because they suppose God to be eternal; if God from
4278	eternity was in a continual sleep, he was in an eternal death,—and
4279	what is death but an eternal sleep?—but no sleep can affect a Deity,
4280	for the immortality of God and alliance to death are vastly different.
4281	But if God was in a continual vigilance, either there was something
4282	wanting to make him happy, or else his beatitude was perfectly
4283	complete; but according to neither of these can God be said to be
4284	blessed; not according to the first, for if there be any deficiency
4285	there is no perfect bliss; not according to the second, for, if there
4286	be nothing wanting to the felicity of God, it must be a useless
4287	enterprise for him to busy himself in human affairs. And how can it be
4288	supposed that God administers by his own providence human concerns,
4289	when to vain and trifling persons prosperous things happen, to great
4290	and high adverse? Agamemnon was both
4291	
4292	  A virtuous prince, for warlike acts renowned,[41]
4293	
4294	and by an adulterer and adulteress was vanquished and perfidiously
4295	slain. Hercules, after he had freed the life of man from many things
4296	that were pernicious to it, perished by the witchcraft and poison of
4297	Deianira.
4298	
4299	Thales said that the intelligence of the world was God.
4300	
4301	Anaximander concluded that the stars were heavenly Deities.
4302	
4303	Democritus said that God, being a globe of fire, is intelligence and
4304	the soul of the world.
4305	
4306	Pythagoras says that, of his principles, unity is God; and the perfect
4307	good, which is indeed the nature of a unity, is mind itself; but the
4308	binary number, which is infinite, is a devil, and in its own nature
4309	evil,—about which the multitude of material beings, and this world
4310	which is the object of our eyes, are conversant.
4311	
4312	Socrates and Plato agree that God is that which is one, hath its
4313	original from its own self, is of a singular subsistence, is one only
4314	being perfectly good; all these various names signifying goodness do
4315	all centre in mind; hence God is to be understood as that mind and
4316	intellect, which is a separate idea, that is to say, pure and unmixed
4317	of all matter, and not twisted with any thing obnoxious to passions.
4318	
4319	Aristotle’s sentiment is, that God hath his residence in superior
4320	regions, and hath placed his throne in the sphere of the universe, and
4321	is a separate idea; which sphere is an ethereal body, which is by him
4322	styled the fifth essence or quintessence. For there is a division of
4323	the universe into spheres, which are contiguous by their nature but
4324	appear to reason to be separated; and he concludes that each of the
4325	spheres is an animal, composed of a body and soul; the body of them is
4326	ethereal, moved orbicularly, the soul is the rational form, which is
4327	unmoved, and yet is the cause that the sphere is actually in motion.
4328	
4329	The Stoics affirm that God is a thing more common and obvious, and is
4330	a mechanic fire which every way spreads itself to produce the world; it
4331	contains in itself all seminal virtues, and by this means all things by
4332	a fatal necessity were produced. This spirit, passing through the whole
4333	world, received various names from the mutations in the matter through
4334	which it ran in its journey. God therefore is the world, the stars, the
4335	earth, and (highest of all) the supreme mind in the heavens.
4336	
4337	In the judgment of Epicurus all the Gods are anthropomorphites, or have
4338	the shape of men; but they are perceptible only by reason, for their
4339	nature admits of no other manner of being apprehended, their parts
4340	being so small and fine that they give no corporeal representations.
4341	The same Epicurus asserts that there are four other natural beings
4342	which are immortal: of this sort are atoms, the vacuum, the infinite,
4343	and the similar parts; and these last are called Homoeomeries and
4344	likewise elements.
4345	
4346	
4347	
4348	
4349	CHAPTER VIII.
4350	
4351	OF THOSE THAT ARE CALLED GENIUSES AND HEROES.
4352	
4353	
4354	Having treated of the essence of the deities in a just order, it
4355	follows that we discourse of daemons and heroes. Thales, Pythagoras,
4356	Plato, and the Stoics do conclude that daemons are essences which are
4357	endowed with souls; that the heroes are the souls separated from their
4358	bodies, some are good, some are bad; the good are those whose souls are
4359	good, the evil those whose souls are wicked. All this is rejected by
4360	Epicurus.
4361	
4362	
4363	
4364	
4365	CHAPTER IX.
4366	
4367	OF MATTER.
4368	
4369	
4370	Matter is that first being which is substrate for generation,
4371	corruption, and all other alterations.
4372	
4373	The disciples of Thales and Pythagoras, with the Stoics, are of opinion
4374	that matter is changeable, mutable, convertible, and sliding through
4375	all things.
4376	
4377	The followers of Democritus aver that the vacuum, the atom, and the
4378	incorporeal substance are the first beings, and not obnoxious to
4379	passions.
4380	
4381	Aristotle and Plato affirm that matter is of that species which is
4382	corporeal, void of any form, species, figure, and quality, but apt to
4383	receive all forms, that she may be the nurse, the mother, and origin
4384	of all other beings. But they that do say that water, earth, air, and
4385	fire are matter do likewise say that matter cannot be without form, but
4386	conclude it is a body; but they that say that individual particles and
4387	atoms are matter do say that matter is without form.
4388	
4389	
4390	
4391	
4392	CHAPTER X.
4393	
4394	OF IDEAS.
4395	
4396	
4397	An idea is a being incorporeal, which has no subsistence by itself, but
4398	gives figure and form unto shapeless matter, and becomes the cause of
4399	its manifestation.
4400	
4401	Socrates and Plato conjecture that these ideas are essences separate
4402	from matter, having their existence in the understanding and fancy of
4403	the Deity, that is, of mind.
4404	
4405	Aristotle objected not to forms and ideas; but he doth not believe them
4406	separated from matter, or patterns of what God has made.
4407	
4408	Those Stoics, that are of the school of Zeno, profess that ideas are
4409	nothing else but the conceptions of our own mind.
4410	
4411	
4412	
4413	
4414	CHAPTER XI.
4415	
4416	OF CAUSES.
4417	
4418	
4419	A cause is that by which any thing is produced, or by which any thing
4420	is effected.
4421	
4422	Plato gives this triple division of causes,—the material, the
4423	efficient, and the final cause; the principal cause he judges to be the
4424	efficient, which is the mind and intellect.
4425	
4426	Pythagoras and Aristotle judge the first causes are incorporeal beings,
4427	but those that are causes by accident or participation become corporeal
4428	substances; by this means the world is corporeal.
4429	
4430	The Stoics grant that all causes are corporeal, inasmuch as they are
4431	breath.
4432	
4433	
4434	
4435	
4436	CHAPTER XII.
4437	
4438	OF BODIES.
4439	
4440	
4441	A body is that being which hath these three dimensions, breadth,
4442	depth, and length;—or a bulk which makes a sensible resistance;—or
4443	whatsoever of its own nature possesseth a place.
4444	
4445	Plato saith that it is neither heavy nor light in its own nature, when
4446	it exists in its own place; but being in the place where another should
4447	be, then it has an inclination by which it tends to gravity or levity.
4448	
4449	Aristotle saith that, if we simply consider things in their own nature,
4450	the earth only is to be judged heavy, and fire light; but air and water
4451	are sometimes heavy and sometimes light.
4452	
4453	The Stoics think that of the four elements two are light, fire and air;
4454	two ponderous, earth and water; that which is naturally light doth by
4455	its own nature, not by any inclination, recede from its own centre; but
4456	that which is heavy doth by its own nature tend to its centre; for the
4457	centre is not a heavy thing of itself.
4458	
4459	Epicurus thinks that bodies are not to be limited; but the first
4460	bodies, which are simple bodies, and all those composed of them, all
4461	acknowledge gravity; that all atoms are moved, some perpendicularly,
4462	some obliquely; some are carried aloft either by direct impulse or with
4463	vibrations.
4464	
4465	
4466	
4467	
4468	CHAPTER XIII.
4469	
4470	OF THOSE THINGS THAT ARE LEAST IN NATURE.
4471	
4472	
4473	Empedocles, precedent to the four elements, introduceth the most minute
4474	bodies which resemble elements; but they did exist before the elements,
4475	having similar parts and orbicular.
4476	
4477	Heraclitus brings in the smallest fragments, and those indivisible.
4478	
4479	
4480	
4481	
4482	CHAPTER XIV.
4483	
4484	OF FIGURES.
4485	
4486	
4487	A figure is the exterior appearance, the circumscription, and the
4488	boundary of a body.
4489	
4490	The Pythagoreans say that the bodies of the four elements are
4491	spherical, fire being in the supremest place only excepted, whose
4492	figure is conical.
4493	
4494	
4495	
4496	
4497	CHAPTER XV.
4498	
4499	OF COLORS.
4500	
4501	
4502	Color is the visible quality of a body.
4503	
4504	The Pythagoreans called color the outward appearance of a body.
4505	Empedocles, that which is consentaneous to the passages of the eye.
4506	Plato, that they are fires emitted from bodies, which have parts
4507	harmonious for the sight. Zeno the Stoic, that colors are the first
4508	figurations of matter. The Pythagoreans, that colors are of four sorts,
4509	white and black, red and pale; and they derive the variety of colors
4510	from the diversity of the elements, and that seen in animals also from
4511	the variety of food and the air in which they live and are bred.
4512	
4513	
4514	
4515	
4516	CHAPTER XVI.
4517	
4518	OF THE DIVISION OF BODIES.
4519	
4520	
4521	The disciples of Thales and Pythagoras grant that all bodies are
4522	passible and divisible unto infinity. Others hold that atoms and
4523	indivisible parts are there fixed, and admit not of a division into
4524	infinity. Aristotle, that all bodies are potentially but not actually
4525	divisible into infinity.
4526	
4527	
4528	
4529	
4530	CHAPTER XVII.
4531	
4532	HOW BODIES ARE MIXED AND CONTEMPERATED ONE WITH ANOTHER.
4533	
4534	
4535	The ancient philosophers held that the mixture of elements proceeded
4536	from the alteration of qualities; but the disciples of Anaxagoras
4537	and Democritus say it is done by apposition. Empedocles composes the
4538	elements of still smaller bulks, those which are the most minute and
4539	may be termed the elements of elements. Plato assigns three bodies (but
4540	he will not allow these to be elements, nor properly so called), air,
4541	fire, and water, which are mutable into one another; but the earth is
4542	mutable into none of these.
4543	
4544	
4545	
4546	
4547	CHAPTER XVIII.
4548	
4549	OF A VACUUM.
4550	
4551	
4552	All the natural philosophers from Thales to Plato rejected a vacuum.
4553	Empedocles says that there is nothing of a vacuity in nature, nor any
4554	thing superabundant. Leucippus, Democritus, Demetrius, Metrodorus,
4555	Epicurus, that the atoms are infinite in number; and that a vacuum
4556	is infinite in magnitude. The Stoics, that within the compass of
4557	the world there is no vacuum, but beyond it the vacuum is infinite.
4558	Aristotle,[42] that the vacuum beyond the world is so great that the
4559	heaven has liberty to breathe into it, for the heaven is fiery.
4560	
4561	
4562	
4563	
4564	CHAPTER XIX.
4565	
4566	OF PLACE.
4567	
4568	
4569	Plato, to define place, calls it that thing which in its own bosom
4570	receives forms and ideas; by which metaphor he signifies matter, being
4571	(as it were) a nurse or receptacle of beings. Aristotle, that it is
4572	the ultimate superficies of the circumambient body, contiguous to that
4573	which it doth encompass.
4574	
4575	
4576	
4577	
4578	CHAPTER XX.
4579	
4580	OF SPACE.
4581	
4582	
4583	The Stoics and Epicureans make a place, a vacuum, and a space to
4584	differ. A vacuum is that which is void of any thing that may be called
4585	a body; place is that which is possessed by a body; a space that which
4586	is partly filled with a body, as a cask with wine.
4587	
4588	
4589	
4590	
4591	CHAPTER XXI.
4592	
4593	OF TIME.
4594	
4595	
4596	In the sense of Pythagoras, time is that sphere which encompasses the
4597	world. Plato says that it is a movable image of eternity, or the
4598	interval of the world’s motion. Eratosthenes, that it is the solar
4599	motion.
4600	
4601	
4602	
4603	
4604	CHAPTER XXII.
4605	
4606	OF THE ESSENCE AND NATURE OF TIME.
4607	
4608	
4609	Plato says that the heavenly motion is time. Most of the Stoics affirm
4610	that motion itself is time. Most philosophers think that time had no
4611	beginning; Plato, that time had only an ideal beginning.
4612	
4613	
4614	
4615	
4616	CHAPTER XXIII.
4617	
4618	OF MOTION.
4619	
4620	
4621	Plato and Pythagoras say that motion is a change and alteration in
4622	matter. Aristotle, that it is the actual operation of that which may be
4623	moved. Democritus, that there is but one sort of motion, and it is that
4624	which is vibratory. Epicurus, that there are two species of motion,
4625	one perpendicular, and the other oblique. Herophilus, that one species
4626	of motion is obvious only to reason, the other to sense. Heraclitus
4627	utterly denies that there is any thing of quiet or repose in nature;
4628	for that is the state of the dead; one sort of motion is eternal, which
4629	he assigns to beings eternal, the other perishable, to those things
4630	which are perishable.
4631	
4632	
4633	
4634	
4635	CHAPTER XXIV.
4636	
4637	OF GENERATION AND CORRUPTION.
4638	
4639	
4640	Parmenides, Melissus, and Zeno deny that there are any such things
4641	as generation and corruption, for they suppose that the universe is
4642	unmovable. Empedocles, Epicurus, and other philosophers that combine
4643	in this, that the world is framed of small corporeal particles meeting
4644	together, affirm that corruption and generation are not so properly to
4645	be accepted; but there are conjunctions and separations, which do not
4646	consist in any alteration according to their qualities, but are made
4647	according to quantity by coalition or disjunction. Pythagoras, and all
4648	those who take for granted that matter is subject to mutation, say that
4649	generation and corruption are to be accepted in their proper sense, and
4650	that they are accomplished by the alteration, mutation, and dissolution
4651	of elements.
4652	
4653	
4654	
4655	
4656	CHAPTER XXV.
4657	
4658	OF NECESSITY.
4659	
4660	
4661	Thales says that necessity is omnipotent, and that it exerciseth
4662	an empire over every thing. Pythagoras, that the world is invested
4663	by necessity. Parmenides and Democritus, that there is nothing in
4664	the world but what is necessarily, and that this same necessity is
4665	otherwise called fate, justice, providence, and the architect of the
4666	world.
4667	
4668	
4669	
4670	
4671	CHAPTER XXVI.
4672	
4673	OF THE NATURE OF NECESSITY.
4674	
4675	
4676	Plato distinguisheth and refers some things to Providence, others to
4677	necessity. Empedocles makes the nature of necessity to be that cause
4678	which employs principles and elements. Democritus makes it to be a
4679	resistance, impulse, and force of matter. Plato sometimes says that
4680	necessity is matter; at other times, that it is the habitude or respect
4681	of the efficient cause towards matter.
4682	
4683	
4684	
4685	
4686	CHAPTER XXVII.
4687	
4688	OF DESTINY OR FATE.
4689	
4690	
4691	Heraclitus, who attributes all things to fate, makes necessity to
4692	be the same thing with it. Plato admits of a necessity in the minds
4693	and the actions of men, but yet he introduceth a cause which flows
4694	from ourselves. The Stoics, in this agreeing with Plato, say that
4695	necessity is a cause invincible and violent; that fate is the ordered
4696	complication of causes, in which there is an intexture of those things
4697	which proceed from our own determination, so that some things are to be
4698	attributed to fate, others not.
4699	
4700	
4701	
4702	
4703	CHAPTER XXVIII.
4704	
4705	OF THE NATURE OF FATE.
4706	
4707	
4708	According to Heraclitus, the essence of fate is a certain reason which
4709	penetrates the substance of all being; and this is an ethereal body,
4710	containing in itself that seminal faculty which gives an original to
4711	every being in the universe. Plato declares that it is the eternal
4712	reason and the eternal law of the nature of the universe. Chrysippus,
4713	that it is a spiritual faculty, which in due order doth manage and rule
4714	the universe. Again, in his book styled the Definitions, that fate is
4715	the reason of the world, or that it is that law whereby Providence
4716	rules and administers every thing that is in the world; or it is that
4717	reason by which all things past have been, all things present are,
4718	and all things future will be. The Stoics say that it is a chain of
4719	causes, that is, it is an order and connection of causes which cannot
4720	be resisted. Posidonius, that it is a being the third in degree from
4721	Jupiter; the first of beings is Jupiter, the second nature, and the
4722	third fate.
4723	
4724	
4725	
4726	
4727	CHAPTER XXIX.
4728	
4729	OF FORTUNE.
4730	
4731	
4732	Plato says, that it is an accidental cause and a casual consequence
4733	in things which proceed from the election and counsel of men.
4734	Aristotle, that it is an accidental cause in those things which are
4735	done by an impulse to a certain end; and this cause is uncertain and
4736	unstable: there is a great deal of difference betwixt that which flows
4737	from chance and that which falls out by Fortune; for that which is
4738	fortuitous admits also of chance, and belongs to things practical; but
4739	what is by chance cannot be also by Fortune, for it belongs to things
4740	without action: Fortune, moreover, belongs to rational beings, but
4741	chance to rational and irrational beings alike, and even to inanimate
4742	things. Epicurus, that it is a cause not always consistent, but various
4743	as to persons, times, and manners. Anaxagoras and the Stoics, that it
4744	is that cause which human reason cannot comprehend; for there are some
4745	things which proceed from necessity, some things from Fate, some from
4746	choice and free-will, some from Fortune, some from chance.
4747	
4748	
4749	
4750	
4751	CHAPTER XXX.
4752	
4753	OF NATURE.
4754	
4755	
4756	Empedocles believes that Nature is nothing else but the mixture and
4757	separation of the elements; for thus he writes in the first book of his
4758	natural philosophy:
4759	
4760	  Nature gives neither life nor death,
4761	  Mutation makes us die or breathe.
4762	  The elements first are mixed, then all
4763	  Do separate: this mortals Nature call.
4764	
4765	Anaxagoras is of the same opinion, that Nature is coalition and
4766	separation, that is, generation and corruption.
4767

4769	
4770	
4771	BOOK II.
4772	
4773	
4774	Having finished my dissertation concerning principles and elements and
4775	those things which chiefly appertain to them, I will turn my pen to
4776	discourse of those things which are produced by them, and will take my
4777	beginning from the world, which contains and encompasseth all beings.
4778	
4779	
4780	
4781	
4782	CHAPTER I.
4783	
4784	OF THE WORLD.
4785	
4786	
4787	Pythagoras was the first philosopher that gave the name of κόσμος to
4788	the world, from the order and beauty of it; for so that word signifies.
4789	Thales and his followers say the world is one. Democritus, Epicurus,
4790	and their scholar Metrodorus affirm that there are infinite worlds
4791	in an infinite space, for that infinite vacuum in its whole extent
4792	contains them. Empedocles, that the circle which the sun makes in its
4793	motion circumscribes the world, and that circle is the utmost bound of
4794	the world. Seleucus, that the world knows no limits. Diogenes, that
4795	the universe is infinite, but this world is finite. The Stoics make a
4796	difference between that which is called the universe, and that which is
4797	called the whole world;—the universe is the infinite space considered
4798	with the vacuum, the vacuity being removed gives the right conception
4799	of the world; so that the universe and the world are not the same
4800	thing.
4801	
4802	
4803	
4804	
4805	CHAPTER II.
4806	
4807	OF THE FIGURE OF THE WORLD.
4808	
4809	
4810	The Stoics say that the figure of the world is spherical, others that
4811	it is conical, others oval. Epicurus, that the figure of the world may
4812	be globular, or that it may admit of other shapes.
4813	
4814	
4815	
4816	
4817	CHAPTER III.
4818	
4819	WHETHER THE WORLD BE AN ANIMAL.
4820	
4821	
4822	Democritus, Epicurus, and those philosophers who introduced atoms and
4823	a vacuum, affirm that the world is not an animal, nor governed by any
4824	wise Providence, but that it is managed by a nature which is void of
4825	reason. All the other philosophers affirm that the world is informed
4826	with a soul, and governed by reason and Providence. Aristotle is
4827	excepted, who is somewhat different; he is of opinion, that the whole
4828	world is not acted by a soul in every part of it, nor hath it any
4829	sensitive, rational, or intellectual faculties, nor is it guided by
4830	reason and Providence in every part of it; of all which the heavenly
4831	bodies are made partakers, for the circumambient spheres are animated
4832	and are living beings; but those things which are about the earth are
4833	void of those endowments; and though those terrestrial bodies are of an
4834	orderly disposition, yet that is casual and not primogenial.
4835	
4836	
4837	
4838	
4839	CHAPTER IV.
4840	
4841	WHETHER THE WORLD IS ETERNAL AND INCORRUPTIBLE.
4842	
4843	
4844	Pythagoras [and Plato], with the Stoics, affirm that the world was
4845	framed by God, and being corporeal is obvious to the senses, and in
4846	its own nature is obnoxious to destruction; but it shall never perish,
4847	it being preserved by the providence of God. Epicurus, that the world
4848	had a beginning, and so shall have an end, as plants and animals have.
4849	Xenophanes, that the world never had a beginning, is eternal and
4850	incorruptible. Aristotle, that the part of the world which is sublunary
4851	is obnoxious to change, and there terrestrial beings find a decay.
4852	
4853	
4854	
4855	
4856	CHAPTER V.
4857	
4858	WHENCE DOES THE WORLD RECEIVE ITS NUTRIMENT?
4859	
4860	
4861	Aristotle says that, if the world be nourished, it will likewise be
4862	dissolved; but it requires no aliment, and will therefore be eternal.
4863	Plato, that this very world prepares for itself a nutriment, by the
4864	alteration of those things which are corruptible in it. Philolaus
4865	believes that a destruction happens to the world in two ways; either
4866	by fire falling from heaven, or by the lunary water being poured down
4867	through the whirling of the air; and the exhalations proceeding from
4868	thence are the aliment of the world.
4869	
4870	
4871	
4872	
4873	CHAPTER VI.
4874	
4875	FROM WHAT ELEMENT GOD DID BEGIN TO RAISE THE FABRIC OF THE WORLD.
4876	
4877	
4878	The natural philosophers pronounce that the forming of this world took
4879	its original from the earth, it being its centre, for the centre is
4880	the principal part of the globe. Pythagoras, from the fire and the
4881	fifth element. Empedocles determines, that the first and principal
4882	element separated from the rest was the ether, then fire, after that
4883	the earth, which earth being strongly compacted by the force of a
4884	violent revolution, water springs from it, the exhalations of which
4885	water produce the air; the heaven took its origin from the ether, and
4886	fire gave a being to the sun; those things that belong to the earth
4887	are condensed from the remainders. Plato, that the visible world was
4888	framed after the exemplar of the intellectual world; the soul of the
4889	visible world was first produced, then the corporeal figure, first that
4890	which came from fire and earth, afterwards that which came from air
4891	and water. Pythagoras, that the world was formed of five solid figures
4892	which are called mathematical; the earth was produced by the cube,
4893	the fire by the pyramid, the air by the octahedron, the water by the
4894	icosahedron, and the globe of the universe by the dodecahedron. In all
4895	these Plato hath the same sentiments with Pythagoras.

4900	CHAPTER VII.
4901	
4902	IN WHAT FORM AND ORDER THE WORLD WAS COMPOSED.
4903	
4904	
4905	Parmenides believes that there are small coronets alternately twisted
4906	one within another, some made up of a thin, others of a condensed
4907	matter; and there are others between them mixed mutually together of
4908	light and of darkness, and about them all there is a solid substance,
4909	which like a firm wall surrounds these coronets. Leucippus and
4910	Democritus wrap the world round about, as with a garment and membrane.
4911	Epicurus says that that which bounds some worlds is thin, and that
4912	which limits others is gross and condensed; and of these worlds some
4913	are in motion, others are fixed. Plato, that fire takes the first place
4914	in the world, the second the ether, after that the air, under that
4915	the water; the last place the earth possesseth: sometimes he puts the
4916	ether and the fire in the same place. Aristotle gives the first place
4917	to the ether, as that which is impassible, it being a kind of fifth
4918	body; after which he placeth those that are passible, fire, air, and
4919	water, and last of all the earth. To those bodies that are accounted
4920	celestial he assigns a motion that is circular, but to those that are
4921	seated under them, if they be light bodies, an ascending, if heavy, a
4922	descending motion. Empedocles, that the places of the elements are not
4923	always fixed and determined, but they all succeed one another in their
4924	respective stations.

4929	CHAPTER VIII.
4930	
4931	WHAT IS THE CAUSE OF THE WORLD’S INCLINATION.
4932	
4933	
4934	Diogenes and Anaxagoras affirm that, after the world was composed and
4935	the earth had produced living creatures, the world out of its own
4936	propensity made an inclination towards the south. Perhaps this may be
4937	attributed to a wise Providence (they say), that thereby some parts
4938	of the world may be habitable, others uninhabitable, according as the
4939	various climates are affected with a rigorous cold, or a scorching
4940	heat, or a just temperament of cold and heat. Empedocles, that the air
4941	yielding to the impetuous force of the solar rays, the pole received an
4942	inclination; whereby the northern parts were exalted and the southern
4943	depressed, by which means the whole world received its inclination.

4948	CHAPTER IX.
4949	
4950	OF THAT THING WHICH IS BEYOND THE WORLD, AND WHETHER IT BE A VACUUM OR
4951	NOT.
4952	
4953	
4954	Pythagoras and his followers say that beyond the world there is a
4955	vacuum, into which and out of which the world hath its respiration.
4956	The Stoics, that there is a vacuum into which the infinite world by a
4957	conflagration shall be dissolved. Posidonius, not an infinite vacuum,
4958	but as much as suffices for the dissolution of the world; and this he
4959	asserts in his first book concerning the Vacuum. Aristotle affirms,
4960	that there is no vacuum. Plato concludes that neither within nor
4961	without the world there is any vacuum.

4967	
4968	WHAT PARTS OF THE WORLD ARE ON THE RIGHT HAND, AND WHAT PARTS ARE ON

4971	
4972	Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle say that the eastern parts of the

4974	western are of the left-hand of the world. Empedocles, that those that
4975	are of the right-hand are towards the summer solstice, those of the

4981	CHAPTER XI.
4982	
4983	OF HEAVEN, WHAT IS ITS NATURE AND ESSENCE.
4984	
4985	
4986	Anaximenes declares that the circumference of heaven is the limit
4987	of the earth’s revolution. Empedocles, that the heaven is a solid
4988	substance, and hath the form and hardness of crystal, it being composed
4989	of the air compacted by fire, and that in both hemispheres it invests

4997	
4998	INTO HOW MANY CIRCLES IS THE HEAVEN DISTINGUISHED; OR, OF THE DIVISION
4999	OF HEAVEN.
5000	
5001	
5002	Thales, Pythagoras, and the followers of Pythagoras do distribute the

5004	zones; one of which is called the arctic circle, which is always

5012	one Oenopides of Chios challenges to himself the invention of it.

5017	CHAPTER XIII.
5018	
5019	WHAT IS THE ESSENCE OF THE STARS, AND HOW THEY ARE COMPOSED.
5020	
5021	
5022	Thales believes that they are globes of earth set on fire. Empedocles,
5023	that they are fiery bodies arising from that fire which the ether
5024	embraced within itself, and did shatter in pieces when the elements
5025	were first separated one from another. Anaxagoras, that the
5026	circumambient ether is of a fiery substance, which, by a vehement
5027	force in its whirling about, did tear stones from the earth, and by
5028	its own power set them on fire, and establish them as stars in the
5029	heavens. Diogenes thinks they resemble pumice stones, and that they are
5030	the breathings of the world; again he supposeth that there are some
5031	invisible stones, which sometimes fall from heaven upon the earth, and
5032	are there quenched; as it happened at Aegos-potami, where a stony star
5033	resembling fire did fall. Empedocles, that the fixed stars are fastened
5034	to the crystal, but the planets are loosened. Plato, that the stars
5035	for the most part are of a fiery nature, but they are made partakers
5036	of another element, with which they are mixed after the resemblance of
5037	glue. Xenophanes, that they are composed of inflamed clouds, which
5038	in the daytime are quenched, and in the night are kindled again. The
5039	like we see in coals; for the rising and setting of the stars is
5040	nothing else but the quenching and kindling of them. Heraclides and
5041	the Pythagoreans, that every star is a world in an infinite ether, and
5042	itself encompasseth air, earth, and ether; this opinion is current
5043	among the followers of Orpheus, for they suppose that each of the stars
5044	does make a world. Epicurus condemns none of these opinions, for he
5045	embraces any thing that is possible.

5051	
5052	OF WHAT FIGURE THE STARS ARE.
5053	
5054	
5055	The Stoics say that the stars are of a circular form, like the sun,
5056	the moon, and the world. Cleanthes, that they are of a conical figure.
5057	Anaximenes, that they are fastened as nails in the crystalline
5058	firmament; some others, that they are fiery plates of gold, resembling

5065	
5066	OF THE ORDER AND PLACE OF THE STARS.
5067	
5068	
5069	Xenocrates says that the stars are moved in one and the same
5070	superficies. The other Stoics say that they are moved in various
5071	superficies, some being superior, others inferior. Democritus, that the
5072	fixed stars are in the highest place; after those the planets; after
5073	which the sun, Venus, and the moon, in their order. Plato, that the
5074	first after the fixed stars that makes its appearance is Phaenon, the
5075	star of Saturn; the second Phaëton, the star of Jupiter; the third the
5076	fiery, which is the star of Mars; the fourth the morning star, which is
5077	the star of Venus the fifth the shining star, and that is the star of
5078	Mercury; in the sixth place is the sun, in the seventh the moon. Plato
5079	and some of the mathematicians conspire in the same opinion; others
5080	place the sun as the centre of the planets. Anaximander, Metrodorus of
5081	Chios, and Crates assign to the sun the superior place, after him they
5082	place the moon, after them the fixed stars and planets.

5088	
5089	OF THE MOTION AND CIRCULATION OF THE STARS.
5090	
5091	
5092	Anaxagoras, Democritus, and Cleanthes say that all the stars have their

5095	to them are carried from the west to the east. Anaximander, that they

5097	Anaximenes, that they are turned under and about the earth. Plato
5098	and the mathematicians, that the sun, Venus, and Mercury have equal
5099	measures in their motions.

5105	
5106	WHENCE DO THE STARS RECEIVE THEIR LIGHT?
5107	
5108	
5109	Metrodorus says that all the fixed stars derive their light from the
5110	sun. Heraclitus and the Stoics, that earthly exhalations are those by
5111	which the stars are nourished. Aristotle, that the heavenly bodies
5112	require no nutriment, for they being eternal cannot be obnoxious to
5113	corruption. Plato and the Stoics, that the whole world and the stars
5114	are fed by the same things.

5126	are little clouds shining by their peculiar motion. Metrodorus, that
5127	the eyes of frighted and astonished people emit those lights which are
5128	called the Twins.

5133	CHAPTER XIX.
5134	
5135	HOW STARS PROGNOSTICATE, AND WHAT IS THE CAUSE OF WINTER AND SUMMER.
5136	
5137	
5138	Plato says that the summer and winter indications proceed from the
5139	rising and setting of the stars, that is, from the rising and setting
5140	of the sun, the moon, and the fixed stars. Anaximenes, that the others
5141	in this are not at all concerned, but that it is wholly performed by
5142	the sun. Eudoxus and Aratus assign it in common to all the stars, for
5143	thus Aratus sings:
5144

5146	  And them in such beauteous order mixed,

5152	CHAPTER XX.
5153	
5154	OF THE ESSENCE OF THE SUN.
5155	
5156	
5157	Anaximander says, that the sun is a circle eight and twenty times
5158	bigger than the earth, and has a circumference which very much
5159	resembles that of a chariot-wheel, which is hollow and full of fire;
5160	the fire of which appears to us through its mouth, as by a hole in a
5161	pipe; and this is the sun. Xenophanes, that the sun is constituted
5162	of small bodies of fire compact together and raised from a moist
5163	exhalation, which collected together make the body of the sun; or that
5164	it is a cloud enfired. The Stoics, that it is an intelligent flame
5165	proceeding from the sea. Plato, that it is composed of abundance of
5166	fire. Anaxagoras, Democritus, and Metrodorus, that it is an enfired
5167	stone, or a burning mass. Aristotle, that it is a sphere formed out
5168	of the fifth body. Philolaus the Pythagorean, that the sun shines as
5169	crystal, which receives its splendor from the fire of the world and so
5170	reflecteth its light upon us; so that first, the body of fire which
5171	is celestial belongs to the sun; and secondly, the fiery reflection
5172	that proceeds from it, in the form of a mirror; and lastly, the light
5173	which is spread upon us by way of reflection from that mirror; and
5174	this last we call the sun, which is (as it were) an image of an image.
5175	Empedocles, that there are two suns; the one the prototype, which is
5176	a fire placed in the other hemisphere, which it totally fills, and is
5177	always ordered in a direct opposition to the reflection of its own
5178	light; and the sun which is visible to us, formed by the reflection of
5179	that splendor in the other hemisphere (which is filled with air mixed
5180	with heat), the light reflected from the circular sun in the opposite
5181	hemisphere falling upon the crystalline sun; and this reflection is
5182	carried round with the motion of the fiery sun. To give briefly the
5183	full sense, the sun is nothing else but the light and brightness of
5184	that fire which encompasseth the earth. Epicurus, that it is an earthy
5185	bulk well compacted, with hollow passages like a pumice-stone or a
5186	sponge, which is kindled by fire.

5196	Anaximander says, that the sun itself in greatness is equal to the

5198	in which it is moved is seven and twenty times larger than the earth.
5199	Anaxagoras, that it is far greater than Peloponnesus. Heraclitus, that
5200	it is no broader than a man’s foot. Epicurus, that he equally embraceth
5201	all the foresaid opinions,—that the sun may be of magnitude as it
5202	appears, or it may be somewhat greater or somewhat less.

5221	
5222	OF THE TURNING AND RETURNING OF THE SUN, OR THE SUMMER AND WINTER
5223	SOLSTICE.
5224	
5225	
5226	Anaximenes thinks that the stars are forced by a condensed and
5227	resisting air. Anaxagoras, by the repelling force of the northern
5228	air, which is violently pushed on by the sun, and thus rendered more
5229	condensed and powerful. Empedocles, that the sun is hindered from

5231	circular tropics. Diogenes, that the sun, when it comes to its utmost
5232	declination, is extinguished, a rigorous cold damping the heat. The

5243	CHAPTER XXIV.
5244	
5245	OF THE ECLIPSES OF THE SUN.
5246	
5247	
5248	Thales was the first who affirmed that the eclipse of the sun was
5249	caused by the moon’s running in a perpendicular line between it and
5250	the earth; for the moon in its own nature is terrestrial. And by
5251	mirrors it is made perspicuous that, when the sun is eclipsed, the moon
5252	is in a direct line below it. Anaximander, that the sun is eclipsed
5253	when the fiery mouth of it is stopped and hindered from expiration.
5254	Heraclitus, that it is after the manner of the turning of a boat, when
5255	the concave appears uppermost to our sight, and the convex nethermost.
5256	Xenophanes, that the sun is eclipsed when it is extinguished; and that
5257	a new sun is created to rise in the east. He gives a farther account
5258	of an eclipse of the sun which remained for a whole month, and again
5259	of a total eclipse which changed the day into night. Some say that the
5260	cause of an eclipse is the invisible concourse of condensed clouds
5261	which cover the orb of the sun. Aristarchus placeth the sun amongst
5262	the fixed stars, and believeth that the earth [the moon?] is moved
5263	about the sun, and that by its inclination and vergency it intercepts
5264	its light and shadows its orb. Xenophanes, that there are many suns
5265	and many moons, according as the earth is distinguished by climates,
5266	circles, and zones. At some certain times the orb of the sun, falling
5267	upon some part of the world which is uninhabited, wanders in a vacuum
5268	and becomes eclipsed. The same person affirms that the sun, proceeding
5269	in its motion in the infinite space, appears to us to move orbicularly,
5270	receiving that representation from its infinite distance from us.

5319	CHAPTER XXVIII.
5320	
5321	FROM WHENCE IS IT THAT THE MOON RECEIVES HER LIGHT?
5322	
5323	
5324	Anaximander thinks that she gives light to herself, but it is very
5325	slender and faint. Antiphon, that the moon shines by its own proper
5326	light; but when it absconds itself, the solar beams darting on it
5327	obscure it. Thus it naturally happens, that a more vehement light
5328	puts out a weaker; the same is seen in other stars. Thales and his
5329	followers, that the moon borrows all her light of the sun. Heraclitus,
5330	that the sun and moon are after the same manner affected; in their
5331	configurations both are shaped like boats, and are made conspicuous to
5332	us, receiving their light from moist exhalations. The sun appears to us
5333	more refulgent, by reason it is moved in a clearer and purer air; the
5334	moon appears more duskish, it being carried in an air more troubled and
5335	gross.

5340	CHAPTER XXIX.
5341	
5342	OF THE ECLIPSE OF THE MOON.
5343	
5344	
5345	Anaximenes believes that the mouth of the hollow wheel, about which
5346	the moon is turned, being stopped is the cause of an eclipse. Berosus,
5347	that it proceeds from the turning of the dark side of the lunar orb
5348	towards us. Heraclitus, that it is performed just after the manner of
5349	a boat turned upside downwards. Some of the Pythagoreans say, that the
5350	splendor arises from the earth, its obstruction from the Antichthon
5351	(or _counter-earth_). Some of the later philosophers, that
5352	there is such a distribution of the lunar flame, that it gradually
5353	and in a just order burns until it be full moon; in like manner,
5354	that this fire decays by degrees, until its conjunction with the sun
5355	totally extinguisheth it. Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, and all the
5356	mathematicians agree in this, that the obscurity with which the moon is
5357	every month affected ariseth from a conjunction with the sun, by whose
5358	more resplendent beams she is darkened; and the moon is then eclipsed
5359	when she falls upon the shadow of the earth, the earth interposing
5360	between the sun and moon, or (to speak more properly) the earth
5361	intercepting the light of the moon.

5366	CHAPTER XXX.
5367	
5368	OF THE PHASES OF THE MOON, OR THE LUNAR ASPECTS; OR HOW IT COMES TO

5370	
5371	
5372	The Pythagoreans say, that the moon appears to us terraneous, by reason
5373	it is inhabited as our earth is, and in it there are animals of a

5375	the animals in their virtue and energy are fifteen degrees superior to
5376	ours; that they emit nothing excrementitious; and that the days are
5377	fifteen times longer. Anaxagoras, that the reason of the inequality
5378	ariseth from the commixture of things earthy and cold; and that fiery
5379	and caliginous matter is jumbled together, whereby the moon is said to
5380	be a star of a counterfeit aspect. The Stoics, that by reason of the
5381	diversity of her substance the composition of her body is subject to
5382	corruption.

5387	CHAPTER XXXI.
5388	
5389	HOW FAR THE MOON IS REMOVED FROM THE SUN.
5390

5392	Empedocles affirms, that the distance of the moon from the sun is
5393	double her remoteness from the earth. The mathematicians, that her
5394	distance from the sun is eighteen times her distance from the earth.
5395	Eratosthenes, that the sun is remote from the earth seven hundred and
5396	eighty thousand furlongs.
5397	
5398	
5399	
5400	
5401	CHAPTER XXXII.
5402	
5403	OF THE YEAR, AND HOW MANY CIRCULATIONS MAKE UP THE GREAT YEAR OF EVERY
5404	PLANET.
5405	
5406	
5407	The year of Saturn is completed when he has had his circulation in the
5408	space of thirty solar years; of Jupiter in twelve; of Mars in two, of
5409	the sun in twelve months; in so many Mercury and Venus, the spaces of
5410	their circulation being equal; of the moon in thirty days, in which
5411	time her course from her prime to her conjunction is finished. As to
5412	the great year, some make it to consist of eight years solar, some
5413	of nineteen, others of fifty-nine. Heraclitus, of eighteen thousand.
5414	Diogenes, of three hundred and sixty-five such years as Heraclitus
5415	assigns. Others there are who lengthen it to seven thousand seven
5416	hundred and seventy-seven years.

5420	
5421	BOOK III.
5422	
5423	
5424	In my two precedent treatises having in due order taken a compendious
5425	view and given an account of the celestial bodies, and of the moon
5426	which divides between them and the terrestrial, I must now convert my
5427	pen to discourse in this third book of Meteors, which are beings above
5428	the earth and below the moon, and are extended to the site and position
5429	of the earth, which is supposed to be the centre of the sphere of this
5430	world; and from thence will I take my beginning.
5431	
5432	
5433	
5434	
5435	CHAPTER I.
5436	
5437	OF THE GALAXY, OR THE MILKY WAY.
5438	
5439	
5440	It is a cloudy circle, which continually appears in the air, and by
5441	reason of the whiteness of its colors is called the galaxy, or the
5442	milky way. Some of the Pythagoreans say that, when Phaëton set the
5443	world on fire, a star falling from its own place in its circular
5444	passage through the region caused an inflammation. Others say that
5445	originally it was the first course of the sun; others, that it is an
5446	image as in a looking-glass, occasioned by the sun’s reflecting its
5447	beams towards the heavens, and this appears in the clouds and in the
5448	rainbow. Metrodorus, that it is merely the solar course, or the motion
5449	of the sun in its own circle. Parmenides, that the mixture of a thick
5450	and thin substance gives it a color which resembles milk. Anaxagoras,
5451	that the sun moving under the earth and not being able to enlighten
5452	every place, the shadow of the earth, being cast upon the part of
5453	the heavens, makes the galaxy. Democritus, that it is the splendor
5454	which ariseth from the coalition of many small bodies, which, being
5455	firmly united amongst themselves, do mutually enlighten one another.
5456	Aristotle, that it is the inflammation of dry, copious, and coherent
5457	exhalations, by which the fiery train, whose seat is beneath the ether
5458	and the planets, is produced. Posidonius, that it is a combination of
5459	fire, of rarer substance than the stars, but denser than light.
5460	
5461	
5462	
5463	
5464	CHAPTER II.
5465	
5466	OF COMETS AND SHOOTING FIRES, AND THOSE WHICH RESEMBLE BEAMS.
5467	
5468	
5469	Some of the Pythagoreans say, that a comet is one of those stars which
5470	do not always appear, but after they have run through their determined
5471	course, they then rise and are visible to us. Others, that it is the
5472	reflection of our sight upon the sun, which gives the resemblance of
5473	comets much after the same manner as images are reflected in mirrors.
5474	Anaxagoras and Democritus, that two or more stars being in conjunction
5475	by their united light make a comet. Aristotle, that it is a fiery
5476	coalition of dry exhalations. Strato, that it is the light of the star
5477	darting through a thick cloud that hath invested it; this is seen in
5478	light shining through lanterns. Heraclides, native of Pontus, that
5479	it is a lofty cloud inflamed by a sublime fire. The like causes he
5480	assigns to the bearded comet, to those circles that are seen about the
5481	sun or stars, or those meteors which resemble pillars or beams, and
5482	all others which are of this kind. This way unanimously go all the
5483	Peripatetics, believing that these meteors, being formed by the clouds,
5484	do differ according to their various configurations. Epigenes, that a
5485	comet arises from an elevation of spirit or wind, mixed with an earthy
5486	substance and set on fire. Boëthus, that it is a phantasy presented to
5487	us by inflamed air. Diogenes, that comets are stars. Anaxagoras, that
5488	those styled shooting stars fall down from the ether like sparks, and
5489	therefore are soon extinguished. Metrodorus, that it is a forcible
5490	illapse of the sun upon clouds which makes them to sparkle as fire.
5491	Xenophanes, that all such fiery meteors are nothing else but the
5492	conglomeration of the enfired clouds, and the flashing motions of them.
5493	
5494	
5495	
5496	
5497	CHAPTER III.
5498	
5499	OF VIOLENT ERUPTION OF FIRE OUT OF THE CLOUDS. OF LIGHTNING. OF
5500	THUNDER. OF HURRICANES. OF WHIRLWINDS.
5501	
5502	
5503	Anaximander affirms that all these are produced by the wind after this
5504	manner: the wind being enclosed by condensed clouds, by reason of its
5505	minuteness and lightness it violently endeavors to make its passage;
5506	and in breaking through the cloud it gives the noise; and the rending
5507	the cloud, because of the blackness of it, gives a resplendent flame.
5508	Metrodorus, that when the wind falls upon a cloud whose densing firmly
5509	compacts it, by breaking the cloud it causeth a great noise, and by
5510	striking and rending the cloud it gives the flame; and in the swiftness
5511	of its motion, the sun imparting heat to it, it throws out the
5512	thunderbolt. The weak declining of the thunderbolt ends in a violent
5513	tempest. Anaxagoras, that when heat and cold meet and are mixed
5514	together (that is, ethereal parts with airy), thereby a great noise
5515	of thunder is produced, and the color seen against the blackness of
5516	the cloud causes the flashing of fire; the full and great splendor is
5517	lightning, the more enlarged and embodied fire becomes a whirlwind, the
5518	cloudiness of it gives the hurricane. The Stoics, that thunder is the
5519	clashing of clouds one upon another, the flash of lightning is their
5520	fiery inflammation; their more rapid splendor is the thunderbolt, the
5521	faint and weak the whirlwind. Aristotle, that all these proceed from
5522	dry exhalations, which, if they meet with moist vapors, force their
5523	passage, and the breaking of them gives the noise of thunder; they,
5524	being very dry, take fire and make lightning; tempests and hurricanes
5525	arise from the plenitude of matter which each draw to themselves, the
5526	hotter parts attracted make the whirlwinds, the duller the tempests.
5527	
5528	
5529	
5530	
5531	CHAPTER IV.
5532	
5533	OF CLOUDS, RAIN, SNOW, AND HAIL.
5534	
5535	
5536	Anaximenes thinks that by the air being very much condensed clouds are
5537	formed; this air being more compacted, rain is compressed through it;
5538	when water in its falling down freezeth, then snow is generated; when
5539	it is encompassed with a moist air, it is hail. Metrodorus, that a
5540	cloud is composed of a watery exhalation carried into a higher place.
5541	Epicurus, that they are made of vapors; and that hail and rain are
5542	formed in a round figure, being in their long descent pressed upon by
5543	the circumambient air.
5544	
5545	
5546	
5547	
5548	CHAPTER V.
5549	
5550	OF THE RAINBOW.
5551	
5552	
5553	Those things which affect the air in the superior places of it are
5554	of two sorts. Some have a real subsistence, such are rain and hail;
5555	others not. Those which enjoy not a proper subsistence are only in
5556	appearance; of this sort is the rainbow. Thus the continent to us that
5557	sail seems to be in motion.
5558	
5559	Plato says, that men admiring it feigned that it took origination from
5560	one Thaumas, which word signifies admiration. Homer says:
5561	
5562	  Jove paints the rainbow with a purple dye,
5563	  Alluring man to cast his wandering eye.[43]
5564	
5565	Others therefore fabled that the bow hath a head like a bull, by which
5566	it swallows up rivers.
5567	
5568	But what is the cause of the rainbow? It is evident that what apparent
5569	things we see come to our eyes in right or in crooked lines, or by
5570	reflection: these last are incorporeal and to sense obscure, but to
5571	reason they are obvious. Those which are seen in right lines are those
5572	which we see through the air or horn or transparent stones, for all
5573	the parts of these things are very fine and tenuious; but those which
5574	appear in crooked lines are in water, the thickness of the water
5575	presenting them bended to our sight. This is the reason that oars in
5576	themselves straight, when put into the sea, appear to us crooked. The
5577	third manner of our seeing is by reflection, and this is perspicuous
5578	by mirrors. After this third sort the rainbow is affected. We conceive
5579	it is a moist exhalation converted into a cloud, and in a short space
5580	it is dissolved into small and moist drops. The sun declining towards
5581	the west, it will necessarily follow that the whole bow is seen
5582	opposite to the sun; for the eye being directed to those drops receives
5583	a reflection, and by this means the bow is formed. The eye doth not
5584	consider the figure and form, but the color of these drops; the first
5585	of which colors is a shining red, the second a purple, the third is
5586	blue and green. Let us consider whether the reason of this shining red
5587	color be the splendor of the sun falling upon these small drops, the
5588	whole body of light being reflected, by which this bright red color is
5589	produced; the second part being troubled, and the light languishing
5590	in the drops, the color becomes purple (for the purple is the faint
5591	red); but the third part, being more and more troubled, is changed
5592	into the green color. And this is proved by other effects of Nature;
5593	if any one shall put water in his mouth and spit it out so opposite to
5594	the sun that its rays may be reflected on the drops, he shall see the
5595	resemblance of a rainbow; the same appears to men that are blear-eyed,
5596	when they fix their watery eyes upon a candle.
5597	
5598	Anaximenes thinks the bow is thus formed; the sun casting its splendor
5599	upon a thick, black, and gross cloud, and the rays not being in a
5600	capacity to penetrate beyond the superficies. Anaxagoras, that, the
5601	solar rays being reflected from a condensed cloud, the sun being
5602	placed directly opposite to it forms the bow after the mode of the
5603	repercussion of a mirror; after the same manner he assigns the natural
5604	cause of the Parhelia or mock-suns, which are often seen in Pontus.
5605	Metrodorus, that when the sun casts its splendor through a cloud, the
5606	cloud gives itself a blue, and the light a red color.
5607	
5608	
5609	
5610	
5611	CHAPTER VI.
5612	
5613	OF METEORS WHICH RESEMBLE RODS, OR OF RODS.
5614	
5615	
5616	These rods and the mock-suns are constituted of a double nature, a real
5617	subsistence, and a mere appearance;—of a real subsistence, because
5618	the clouds are the object of our eyes; of a mere appearance, for their
5619	proper color is not seen, but that which is adventitious. The like
5620	affections, natural and adventitious, in all such things do happen.
5621	
5622	
5623	
5624	
5625	CHAPTER VII.
5626	
5627	OF WINDS.
5628	
5629	
5630	Anaximander believes that wind is a fluid air, the sun putting into
5631	motion or melting the moist subtle parts of it. The Stoics, that all
5632	winds are a flowing air, and from the diversity of the regions whence
5633	they have their origin receive their denomination; as, from darkness
5634	and the west the western wind; from the sun and its rising the eastern;
5635	from the north the northern, and from the south the southern winds.
5636	Metrodorus, that moist vapors heated by the sun are the cause of the
5637	impetuousness of violent winds. The Etesian, or those winds which
5638	annually commence about the rising of the Little Dog, the air about the
5639	northern pole being more compacted, blow vehemently following the sun
5640	when he returns from the summer solstice.
5641	
5642	
5643	
5644	
5645	CHAPTER VIII.
5646	
5647	OF WINTER AND SUMMER.
5648	
5649	
5650	Empedocles and the Stoics believe that winter is caused by the
5651	thickness of the air prevailing and mounting upwards; and summer by
5652	fire, it falling downwards.
5653	
5654	       *       *       *       *       *
5655	
5656	This description being given by me of Meteors, or those things that are
5657	above us, I must pass to those things which are terrestrial.
5658	
5659	
5660	
5661	
5662	CHAPTER IX.
5663	
5664	OF THE EARTH, WHAT IS ITS NATURE AND MAGNITUDE.
5665	
5666	
5667	Thales and his followers say that there is but one earth. Hicetes the
5668	Pythagorean, that there are two earths, this and the Antichthon, or
5669	the earth opposite to it. The Stoics, that this earth is one, and that
5670	finite and limited. Xenophanes, that the earth, being compacted of fire
5671	and air, in its lowest parts hath laid a foundation in an infinite
5672	depth. Metrodorus, that the earth is mere sediment and dregs of water,
5673	as the sun is of the air.
5674	
5675	
5676	
5677	
5678	CHAPTER X.
5679	
5680	OF THE FIGURE OF THE EARTH.
5681	
5682	
5683	Thales, the Stoics, and their followers say that the earth is globular.
5684	Anaximander, that it resembles a smooth stony pillar. Anaximenes, that
5685	it hath the shape of a table. Leucippus, of a drum. Democritus, that it
5686	is like a quoit in its surface, and hollow in the middle.
5687	
5688	
5689	
5690	
5691	CHAPTER XI.
5692	
5693	OF THE SITE AND POSITION OF THE EARTH.
5694	
5695	
5696	The disciples of Thales say that the earth is the centre of the
5697	universe. Xenophanes, that it is first, being rooted in the infinite
5698	space. Philolaus the Pythagorean gives to fire the middle place, and
5699	this is the hearth-fire of the universe; the second place to the
5700	Antichthon; the third to that earth which we inhabit, which is seated
5701	in opposition unto and whirled about the opposite,—which is the reason
5702	that those which inhabit that earth cannot be seen by us. Parmenides
5703	was the first that confined the habitable world to the two solstitial
5704	(or temperate) zones.
5705	
5706	
5707	
5708	
5709	CHAPTER XII.
5710	
5711	OF THE INCLINATION OF THE EARTH.
5712	
5713	
5714	Leucippus affirms that the earth vergeth towards the southern parts, by
5715	reason of the thinness and fineness that is in the south; the northern
5716	parts are more compacted, they being congealed by a rigorous cold, but
5717	those parts of the world that are opposite are enfired. Democritus,
5718	because, the southern parts of the atmosphere being the weaker, the
5719	earth as it enlarges bends towards the south; the northern parts are
5720	of an unequal, the southern of an equal temperament; and this is the
5721	reason that the earth bends towards those parts where the earth is
5722	laden with fruits and its own increase.
5723	
5724	
5725	
5726	
5727	CHAPTER XIII.
5728	
5729	OF THE MOTION OF THE EARTH.
5730	
5731	
5732	Most of the philosophers say that the earth remains fixed in the same
5733	place. Philolaus the Pythagorean, that it is moved about the element of
5734	fire, in an oblique circle, after the same manner of motion that the
5735	sun and moon have. Heraclides of Pontus and Ecphantus the Pythagorean
5736	assign a motion to the earth, but not progressive, but after the manner
5737	of a wheel being carried on its own axis; thus the earth (they say)
5738	turns itself upon its own centre from west to east. Democritus, that
5739	when the earth was first formed it had a motion, the parts of it being
5740	small and light; but in process of time the parts of it were condensed,
5741	so that by its own weight it was poised and fixed.
5742	
5743	
5744	
5745	
5746	CHAPTER XIV.
5747	
5748	INTO HOW MANY ZONES IS THE EARTH DIVIDED?
5749	
5750	
5751	Pythagoras says that, as the celestial sphere is distributed into
5752	five zones, into the same number is the terrestrial; which zones are
5753	the arctic and antarctic, the summer and winter tropics (or temperate
5754	zones), and the equinoctial; the middle of which zones equally divides
5755	the earth and constitutes the torrid zone; but that part which is in
5756	the middle of the summer and winter tropics is habitable, by reason the
5757	air is there temperate.
5758	
5759	
5760	
5761	
5762	CHAPTER XV.
5763	
5764	OF EARTHQUAKES.
5765	
5766	
5767	Thales and Democritus assign the cause of earthquakes to water. The
5768	Stoics say that it is a moist vapor contained in the earth, making an
5769	irruption into the air, that makes the earthquake. Anaximenes, that
5770	the dryness and rarety of the earth are the cause of earthquakes, the
5771	one of which is produced by extreme drought, the other by immoderate
5772	showers. Anaxagoras, that the air endeavoring to make a passage out of
5773	the earth, meeting with a thick superficies, is not able to force its
5774	way, and so shakes the circumambient earth with a trembling. Aristotle,
5775	that a cold vapor encompassing every part of the earth prohibits the
5776	evacuation of vapors; for those which are hot, being in themselves
5777	light, endeavor to force a passage upwards, by which means the dry
5778	exhalations, being left in the earth, use their utmost endeavor to make
5779	a passage out, and being wedged in, they suffer various circumvolutions
5780	and shake the earth. Metrodorus, that whatsoever is in its own place
5781	is incapable of motion, except it be pressed upon or drawn by the
5782	operation of another body; the earth being so seated cannot naturally
5783	be removed, yet divers parts and places of the earth may move one
5784	upon another. Parmenides and Democritus, that the earth being so
5785	equally poised hath no sufficient cause why it should incline rather
5786	to one side than to the other; so that it may be shaken, but cannot be
5787	removed. Anaximenes, that the earth by reason of its latitude is borne
5788	upon the air which presseth upon it. Others opine that the earth swims
5789	upon the waters, as boards and broad planks, and by that reason is
5790	moved. Plato, that motion is by six manner of ways, upwards, downwards,
5791	on the right-hand and on the left, behind and before; therefore it is
5792	not possible that the earth should be moved in any of these modes,
5793	for it is altogether seated in the lowest place; it therefore cannot
5794	receive a motion, since there is nothing about it so peculiar as to
5795	make it incline any way; but some parts of it are so rare and thin
5796	that they are capable of motion. Epicurus, that the possibility of the
5797	earth’s motion ariseth from a thick and aqueous air beneath the earth,
5798	which may, by moving or pushing it, be capable of its quaking; or that
5799	being so compassed, and having many passages, it is shaken by the wind
5800	which is dispersed through the hollow dens of it.
5801	
5802	
5803	
5804	
5805	CHAPTER XVI.
5806	
5807	OF THE SEA, AND HOW IT IS COMPOSED, AND HOW IT BECOMES TO THE TASTE
5808	BITTER.
5809	
5810	
5811	Anaximander affirms that the sea is the remainder of the primogenial
5812	humidity, the greatest part of which being dried up by the fire, the
5813	influence of the great heat altered its quality. Anaxagoras, that in
5814	the beginning water did not flow, but was as a standing pool; and that
5815	it was burnt by the motion of the sun about it, by which the oily
5816	part of the water being exhaled, the residue became salt and bitter.
5817	Empedocles, that the sea is the sweat of the earth burnt by the sun.
5818	Antiphon, that the sweat of that which was hot was separated from the
5819	other parts which were moist; these by seething and boiling became
5820	bitter, as happens in all sweats. Metrodorus, that the sea was strained
5821	through the earth, and retained some part of the density thereof; the
5822	same is observed in all those things which are strained through ashes.
5823	The schools of Plato, that the element of water being compacted by the
5824	rigor of the air became sweet, but that part which was exhaled from the
5825	earth, being enfired, became of a brackish taste.
5826	
5827	
5828	
5829	
5830	CHAPTER XVII.
5831	
5832	OF TIDES, OR OF THE EBBING AND FLOWING OF THE SEA.
5833	
5834	
5835	Aristotle and Heraclides say, they proceed from the sun, which moves
5836	and whirls about the winds; and these falling with a violence upon the
5837	Atlantic, it is pressed and swells by them, by which means the sea
5838	flows; and their impression ceasing, the sea retracts, hence they ebb.
5839	Pytheas the Massilian, that the fulness of the moon gives the flow, the
5840	wane the ebb. Plato attributes it all to a certain oscillation of the
5841	sea, which by means of a mouth or orifice causes the alternate ebb and
5842	flow; and by this means the seas do rise and flow contrarily. Timaeus
5843	believes that those rivers which fall from the mountains of the Celtic
5844	Gaul into the Atlantic produce a tide. For upon their entering upon
5845	that sea, they violently press upon it, and so cause the flow; but they
5846	disemboguing themselves, there is a cessation of the impetuousness, by
5847	which means the ebb is produced. Seleucus the mathematician attributes
5848	a motion to the earth; and thus he pronounceth that the moon in its
5849	circumlation meets and repels the earth in its motion; between these
5850	two, the earth and the moon, there is a vehement wind raised and
5851	intercepted, which rushes upon the Atlantic Ocean, and gives us a
5852	probable argument that it is the cause the sea is troubled and moved.
5853	
5854	
5855	
5856	
5857	CHAPTER XVIII.
5858	
5859	OF THE HALO, OR A CIRCLE ABOUT A STAR.
5860	
5861	
5862	The halo or circle is thus formed. A thick and dark air intervening
5863	between the moon or any other star and our eye, by which means our
5864	sight is dilated and reflected, when now our sight is incident upon the
5865	outward circumference of the orb of that star, there presently seems
5866	a circle to appear. This circle thus appearing is called the ἅλως or
5867	_halo_; and there is constantly such a circle seen by us, when
5868	such a density of sight happens.

5872	
5873	BOOK IV.
5874	
5875	
5876	Having taken a survey of the general parts of the world, I will take a
5877	view of the particular members of it.
5878	
5879	
5880	
5881	
5882	CHAPTER I.
5883	
5884	OF THE OVERFLOWING OF THE NILE.
5885	
5886	
5887	Thales conjectures that the Etesian or anniversary northern winds
5888	blowing strongly against Egypt heighten the swelling of the Nile, the
5889	mouth of that river being obstructed by the force of the sea rushing
5890	into it. Euthymenes the Massilian concludes that the Nile is filled by
5891	the ocean and that sea which is outward from it, this being naturally
5892	sweet. Anaxagoras, that the snow in Ethiopia which is frozen in winter
5893	is melted in summer, and this makes the inundation. Democritus, that
5894	the snows which are in the northern climates when the sun enters the
5895	summer solstice are dissolved and diffused; from those vapors clouds
5896	are compacted, and these are forcibly driven by the Etesian winds
5897	into the southern parts and into Egypt, from whence violent showers
5898	are poured; and by this means the fens of Egypt are filled with water,
5899	and the river Nile hath its inundation. Herodotus the historian, that
5900	the waters of the Nile receive from their fountain an equal portion of
5901	water in winter and in summer; but in winter the water appears less,
5902	because the sun, making its approach nearer to Egypt, draws up the
5903	rivers of that country into exhalations. Ephorus the historiographer,
5904	that in summer all Egypt seems to be melted and sweats itself
5905	into water, to which the thin and sandy soils of Arabia and Lybia
5906	contribute. Eudoxus relates that the Egyptian priests affirm that, when
5907	it is summer to us who dwell under the northern tropic, it is winter
5908	with them that inhabit under the southern tropic; by this means there
5909	is a various contrariety and opposition of the seasons in the year,
5910	which cause such showers to fall as make the water to overflow the
5911	banks of the Nile and diffuse itself throughout all Egypt.
5912	
5913	
5914	
5915	
5916	CHAPTER II.
5917	
5918	OF THE SOUL.
5919	
5920	
5921	Thales first pronounced that the soul is that being which is in
5922	a perpetual motion, or that whose motion proceeds from itself.
5923	Pythagoras, that it is a number moving itself; he takes a number to be
5924	the same thing with a mind. Plato, that it is an intellectual substance
5925	moving itself, and that motion is in a numerical harmony. Aristotle,
5926	that it is the first actuality (ἐντελέχεια) of a natural organical body
5927	which has life potentially; and this actuality must be understood to be
5928	the same thing with energy or operation. Dicaearchus, that it is the
5929	harmony of the four elements. Asclepiades the physician, that it is the
5930	concurrent exercitation of the senses.
5931	
5932	
5933	
5934	
5935	CHAPTER III.
5936	
5937	WHETHER THE SOUL BE A BODY, AND WHAT IS THE NATURE AND ESSENCE OF IT.
5938	
5939	
5940	All those that have been named by me do affirm that the soul itself is
5941	incorporeal, and by its own nature is in a perpetual motion, and in its
5942	own essence is an intelligent substance, and the actuality of a natural
5943	organical body which has life. The followers of Anaxagoras, that it is
5944	airy and a body. The Stoics, that it is a hot breath. Democritus, that
5945	it is a fiery composition of things which are perceptible by reason,
5946	the same having their forms spherical and without an inflaming faculty;
5947	and it is a body. Epicurus, that it is constituted of four qualities,
5948	of a fiery quality, of an aerial quality, a pneumatical, and of a
5949	fourth quality which hath no name, but it contains the virtue of the
5950	sense. Heraclitus, that the soul of the world is the exhalation which
5951	proceeds from the moist parts of it; but the soul of animals, arising
5952	from exhalations that are exterior and from those that are within them,
5953	is homogeneous to it.
5954	
5955	
5956	
5957	
5958	CHAPTER IV.
5959	
5960	OF THE PARTS OF THE SOUL.
5961	
5962	
5963	Plato and Pythagoras, according to their former account distribute the
5964	soul into two parts, the rational and irrational. By a more accurate
5965	and strict account the soul is branched into three parts; they divide
5966	the unreasonable part into the concupiscible and the irascible. The
5967	Stoics say the soul is constituted of eight parts; five of which are
5968	the senses, hearing, seeing, tasting, touching, smelling, the sixth
5969	is the faculty of speaking, the seventh of generating, the eighth of
5970	commanding; this is the principal of all, by which all the other are
5971	guided and ordered in their proper organs, as we see the arms of a
5972	polypus aptly disposed. Democritus and Epicurus divide the soul into
5973	two parts, the rational, which hath its residence in the breast, and
5974	the irrational, which is diffused through the whole structure of the
5975	body. Democritus, that the quality of the soul is communicated to every
5976	thing, yea, to the dead corpses; for they are partakers of heat and
5977	some sense, when the most of both is expired out of them.
5978	
5979	
5980	
5981	
5982	CHAPTER V.
5983	
5984	WHAT IS THE PRINCIPAL PART OF THE SOUL, AND IN WHAT PART OF THE BODY IT
5985	RESIDES.
5986	
5987	
5988	Plato and Democritus place its residence in the whole head. Strato,
5989	in that part of the forehead where the eyebrows are separated.
5990	Erasistratus, in the Epikranis, or membrane which involves the brain.
5991	Herophilus, in that sinus of the brain which is the basis of it.
5992	Parmenides, in the whole breast; which opinion is embraced by Epicurus.
5993	The Stoics are generally of this opinion, that the seat of the soul is
5994	throughout the heart, or in the spirit which is about it. Diogenes, in
5995	the arterial ventricle of the heart, which is also filled with vital
5996	spirit. Empedocles, in the mass of the blood. There are that say it
5997	is in the neck of the heart, others in the pericardium, others in
5998	the midriff. Certain of the Neoterics, that the seat of the soul is
5999	extended from the head to the diaphragm. Pythagoras, that the animal
6000	part of the soul resides in the heart, the intellectual in the head.
6001	
6002	
6003	
6004	
6005	CHAPTER VI.
6006	
6007	OF THE MOTION OF THE SOUL.
6008	
6009	
6010	Plato believes that the soul is in perpetual motion, but that the mind
6011	is immovable with respect to motion from place to place. Aristotle,
6012	that the soul is not naturally moved, but its motion is accidental,
6013	resembling that which is in the forms of bodies.
6014	
6015	
6016	
6017	
6018	CHAPTER VII.
6019	
6020	OF THE SOUL’S IMMORTALITY.
6021	
6022	
6023	Plato and Pythagoras say that the soul is immortal; when it departs
6024	out of the body, it retreats to the soul of the world, which is a
6025	being of the same nature with it. The Stoics, when the souls leave the
6026	bodies, they are carried to divers places; the souls of the unlearned
6027	and ignorant descend to the coagmentation of earthly things, but
6028	the learned and vigorous endure till the general fire. Epicurus and
6029	Democritus, the soul is mortal, and it perisheth with the body. Plato
6030	and Pythagoras, that part of the soul of man which is rational is
6031	eternal; for though it be not God, yet it is the product of an eternal
6032	Deity; but that part of the soul which is divested of reason dies.
6033	
6034	
6035	
6036	
6037	CHAPTER VIII.
6038	
6039	OF THE SENSES, AND OF THOSE THINGS WHICH ARE OBJECTS OF THE SENSES.
6040	
6041	
6042	The Stoics give this definition of sense: Sense is the apprehension
6043	or comprehension of an object by means of an organ. There are several
6044	ways of expressing what sense is; it is either a habit, a faculty, an
6045	operation, or an imagination which apprehends by means of an organ of
6046	sense,—and also the eighth principal thing, from whence the senses
6047	are derived. The instruments of sense are intelligent spirits, which
6048	from the said commanding part reach unto all the organs of the body.
6049	Epicurus, that sense is a faculty, and that which is perceived
6050	by the sense is the product of it; so that sense hath a double
6051	acceptation,—sense which is the faculty, and the thing received by
6052	the sense, which is the effect. Plato, that sense is that commerce
6053	which the soul and body have with those things that are exterior to
6054	them; the power of which is from the soul, the organ by which is from
6055	the body; but both of them apprehend exterior objects by means of the
6056	imagination. Leucippus and Democritus, that sense and intelligence
6057	arise from external images; so neither of them can operate without the
6058	assistance of an image falling upon us.
6059	
6060	
6061	
6062	
6063	CHAPTER IX.
6064	
6065	WHETHER WHAT APPEARS TO OUR SENSES AND IMAGINATIONS BE TRUE OR NOT.
6066	
6067	
6068	The Stoics say that what the senses represent is true; what the
6069	imagination, is partly false, partly true. Epicurus, that every
6070	impression which either the sense or fancy gives us is true, but of
6071	those things that fall under the account of opinion, some are true,
6072	some false: sense gives us a false representation of those things
6073	only which are the objects of our understanding; but the fancy gives
6074	us a double error, both of things sensible and things intellectual.
6075	Empedocles and Heraclides, that the senses perceive by a just
6076	accommodation of the pores in every case; every thing that is perceived
6077	by the sense being congruously adapted to its proper organ.
6078	
6079	
6080	
6081	
6082	CHAPTER X.
6083	
6084	HOW MANY SENSES ARE THERE?
6085	
6086	
6087	The Stoics say that there are five senses properly so called, seeing,
6088	hearing, smelling, tasting, and touching. Aristotle indeed doth not
6089	add a sixth sense; but he assigns a common sense, which is the judge
6090	of all compounded species; into this each sense casts its proper
6091	representation, in which is discovered a transition of one thing into
6092	another, like as we see in figure and motion where there is a change of
6093	one into another. Democritus, that there are several species of senses,
6094	which appertain to beings destitute of reason, to the Gods, and to wise
6095	men.
6096	
6097	
6098	
6099	
6100	CHAPTER XI.
6101	
6102	HOW THE ACTIONS OF THE SENSES, THE CONCEPTIONS OF OUR MINDS, AND THE
6103	HABIT OF OUR REASON ARE FORMED.
6104	
6105	
6106	The Stoics affirm that every man, as soon as he is born, has the
6107	principal and commanding part of his soul, which is in him like a sheet
6108	of writing-paper, to which he commits all his notions. The first manner
6109	of his inscribing is by denoting those notions which flow from the
6110	senses. Suppose it be of a thing that is white; when the present sense
6111	of it is vanished, there is yet retained the remembrance; when many
6112	memorative notions of the same similitude do concur, then he is said to
6113	have an experience; for experience is nothing else but the abundance of
6114	notions that are of the same form met together. Some of these notions
6115	are naturally begotten according to the aforesaid manner, without the
6116	assistance of art; the others are produced by discipline, learning,
6117	and industry; these only are properly called notions, the others are
6118	prenotions. But reason, which gives us the denomination of rational,
6119	is completed by prenotions in the first seven years. The conception
6120	of the mind is the vision that the intelligence of a rational animal
6121	hath received; when that vision falls upon the rational soul, then it
6122	is called the conception of the mind, for it hath derived its name
6123	from the mind (ἐννόημα from νοῦς). Therefore these visions are not
6124	to be found in any other animals; they are appropriated only to Gods
6125	and to us men. If these we consider generally, they are phantasms; if
6126	specifically, they are notions. As pence or staters, if you consider
6127	them according to their own value, are merely pence and staters; but if
6128	you give them as a price for a naval voyage, they are called not merely
6129	pence, &c., but your fraught.
6130	
6131	
6132	
6133	
6134	CHAPTER XII.
6135	
6136	WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN IMAGINATION (φαντασια[** φαντασία]),
6137	IMAGINABLE (φανταστόν), FANCY (φανταστικόν), AND PHANTOM (φάντασμα)?
6138	
6139	
6140	Chrysippus affirms, these four are different one from another.
6141	Imagination (he says) is that passion raised in the soul which
6142	discovers itself and that which was the efficient of it; for example,
6143	after the eye hath looked upon a thing that is white, the sight of
6144	which produceth in the mind a certain impression, this gives us
6145	reason to conclude that the object of this impression is white, which
6146	affecteth us. So is it with touching and smelling.
6147	
6148	Phantasy or imagination is denominated from φῶς, which denotes
6149	light; for as light discovers itself and all other things which it
6150	illuminates, so this imagination discovers itself and that which is
6151	the cause of it. The imaginable is the efficient cause of imagination;
6152	as any thing that is white, or any thing that is cold, or every thing
6153	that may make an impression upon the imagination. Fancy is a vain
6154	impulse upon the mind of man, proceeding from nothing which is really
6155	imaginable; this is experienced in those that whirl about their idle
6156	hands and fight with shadows; for to the imagination there is always
6157	some real imaginable thing presented, which is the efficient cause of
6158	it; but to the fancy nothing. A phantom is that to which we are led by
6159	such a fanciful and vain attraction; this is to be seen in melancholy
6160	and distracted persons. Of this sort was Orestes in the tragedy,
6161	pronouncing these words:
6162	
6163	  Mother, these maids with horror me affright;
6164	  Oh hurl them not, I pray, into my sight!
6165	  They’re smeared with blood, and cruel, dragon-like,
6166	  Skipping about with deadly fury strike.
6167	
6168	These rave as frantic persons, they see nothing, and yet imagine they
6169	see. Thence Electra thus returns to him:
6170	
6171	  O wretched man, securely sleep in bed;
6172	  Nothing thou seest, thy fancy’s vainly led.[44]
6173	
6174	After the same manner Theoclymenus in Homer.
6175	
6176	
6177	
6178	
6179	CHAPTER XIII.
6180	
6181	OF OUR SIGHT, AND BY WHAT MEANS WE SEE.
6182	
6183	
6184	Democritus and Epicurus suppose that sight is caused by the insinuation
6185	of little images into the visive organ, and by the entrance of certain
6186	rays which return to the eye after striking upon the object. Empedocles
6187	supposes that images are mixed with the rays of the eye; these he
6188	styles the rays of images. Hipparchus, that the visual rays extend
6189	from both the eyes to the superficies of bodies, and give to the sight
6190	the apprehension of those same bodies, after the same manner in which
6191	the hand touching the extremity of bodies gives the sense of feeling.
6192	Plato, that the sight is the splendor of united rays; there is a light
6193	which reaches some distance from the eyes into a congruous air, and
6194	there is likewise a light emitted from bodies, which meets and is
6195	joined with the fiery visual light in the intermediate air (which is
6196	liquid and mutable); and the conjunction of these rays gives the sense
6197	of seeing. This is Plato’s corradiancy, or splendor of united rays.
6198	
6199	
6200	
6201	
6202	CHAPTER XIV.
6203	
6204	OF THOSE IMAGES WHICH ARE PRESENTED TO OUR EYES IN MIRRORS.
6205	
6206	
6207	Empedocles says that these images are caused by certain effluvias
6208	which, meeting together and insisting upon the superficies of the
6209	mirror, are perfected by that fiery quality emitted by the said mirror,
6210	which transmutes withal the air that surrounds it. Democritus and
6211	Epicurus, that the specular appearances are formed by the subsistence
6212	of the images which flow from our eyes; these fall upon the mirror
6213	and remain, while the light rebounds to the eye. The followers of
6214	Pythagoras explain it by the reflection of the sight; for our sight
6215	being extended (as it were) to the brass, and meeting with the smooth
6216	dense surface thereof it is struck back, and caused to return upon
6217	itself: the same appears in the hand, when it is stretched out and then
6218	brought back again to the shoulder. Any one may apply these instances
6219	to explain the manner of seeing.
6220	
6221	
6222	
6223	
6224	CHAPTER XV.
6225	
6226	WHETHER DARKNESS CAN BE VISIBLE TO US.
6227	
6228	
6229	The Stoics say that darkness is seen by us, for out of our eyes there
6230	issues out some light into it; and our eyes do not impose upon us,
6231	for they really perceive there is darkness. Chrysippus says that we
6232	see darkness by the striking of the intermediate air; for the visual
6233	spirits which proceed from the principal part of the soul and reach to
6234	the ball of the eye pierce this air, which, after they have made those
6235	strokes upon it, presses conically on the surrounding air, where this
6236	is homogeneous. For from the eyes those rays are poured forth which are
6237	neither black nor cloudy. Upon this account darkness is visible to us
6238	
6239	
6240	
6241	
6242	CHAPTER XVI.
6243	
6244	OF HEARING.
6245	
6246	
6247	Empedocles says that hearing is formed by the insidency of the air
6248	upon the spiral, which it is said hangs within the ear as a bell, and
6249	is beat upon by the air. Alcmaeon, that the vacuity that is within
6250	the ear makes us to have the sense of hearing, for the air forcing a
6251	vacuum gives the sound; every inanity affords a ringing. Diogenes, the
6252	air which is in the head, being struck upon by the voice, gives the
6253	hearing. Plato and his followers, the air which exists in the head
6254	being struck upon, is reflected to the principal part of the soul, and
6255	this causeth the sense of hearing.
6256	
6257	
6258	
6259	
6260	CHAPTER XVII.
6261	
6262	OF SMELLING.
6263	
6264	
6265	Alcmaeon believes that the principal part of the soul, residing in the
6266	brain, draws to itself odors by respiration. Empedocles, that scents
6267	insert themselves into the breathing of the lungs; for, when there is a
6268	great difficulty in breathing, odors are not perceived by reason of the
6269	sharpness; and this we experience in those who have the defluxion of
6270	rheum.
6271	
6272	
6273	
6274	
6275	CHAPTER XVIII.
6276	
6277	OF TASTE.
6278	
6279	
6280	Alcmaeon says that a moist warmth in the tongue, joined with the
6281	softness of it, gives the difference of taste. Diogenes, that by the
6282	softness and sponginess of the tongue, and because the veins of the
6283	body are joined in it, tastes are diffused by the tongue; for they are
6284	attracted from it to that sense and to the commanding part of the soul,
6285	as from a sponge.
6286	
6287	
6288	
6289	
6290	CHAPTER XIX.
6291	
6292	OF THE VOICE.
6293	
6294	
6295	Plato thus defines a voice,—that it is a breath drawn by the mind
6296	through the mouth, and a blow given to the air and through the
6297	ear, brain, and blood transmitted to the soul. Voice is abusively
6298	attributed to irrational and inanimate beings; thus we improperly
6299	call the neighing of horses or any other sound by the name of voice.
6300	But properly a voice (φωνή) is an articulate sound, which illustrates
6301	(φωτίζει) the understanding of man. Epicurus says that it is an efflux
6302	emitted from things that are vocal, or that give sounds or great
6303	noises; this is broken into those fragments which are after the same
6304	configuration. Like figures are round figures with round, and irregular
6305	and triangular with those of the same nature. These falling upon the
6306	ears produce the sense of hearing. This is seen in leaking vessels, and
6307	in fullers when they fan or blow their cloths.
6308	
6309	Democritus, that the air is broken into bodies of similar
6310	configuration, and these are rolled up and down with the fragments of
6311	the voice; as it is proverbially said, One daw lights with another, or,
6312	God always brings like to like. Thus we see upon the shore, that stones
6313	like to one another are found in the same place, in one place the
6314	long-shaped, in another the round are seen. So in sieves, things that
6315	are of the same form meet together, but those that are different are
6316	divided; as pulse and beans falling from the same sieve are separated
6317	one from another. To this it may be objected: How can some fragments of
6318	air fill a theatre in which there is an infinite company of persons?
6319	The Stoics, that the air is not composed of small fragments, but is a
6320	continued body and nowhere admits a vacuum; and being struck with the
6321	breath, it is infinitely moved in waves and in right circles, until it
6322	fill that air which invests it; as we see in a fish-pool which we smite
6323	by a falling stone cast upon it; yet the air is moved spherically, the
6324	water orbicularly. Anaxagoras says a voice is then formed, when upon a
6325	solid air the breath is incident, which being repercussed is carried to
6326	the ears; after the same manner the echo is produced.
6327	
6328	
6329	
6330	
6331	CHAPTER XX.
6332	
6333	WHETHER THE VOICE IS INCORPOREAL. WHAT IS IT THAT GIVES THE ECHO?
6334	
6335	
6336	Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle say that the voice is incorporeal; for
6337	it is not the air that makes the voice, but the figure which compasseth
6338	the air and its superficies, having received a stroke, give the voice.
6339	But every superficies of itself is incorporeal. True it is that it
6340	moveth with the body, but of itself it hath no body; as we perceive
6341	in a staff that is bended, the matter only admits of an inflection,
6342	while the superficies doth not. According to the Stoics, a voice is
6343	corporeal, since every thing that is an agent or operates is a body; a
6344	voice acts and operates, for we hear it and are sensible of it; for it
6345	falls and makes an impression on the ear, as a seal of a ring gives its
6346	similitude upon the wax. Moreover, every thing that creates a delight
6347	or molestation is a body; harmonious music affects with delight, but
6348	discord is tiresome. And every thing that is moved is a body; and the
6349	voice moves, and having its illapse upon smooth places is reflected, as
6350	when a ball is cast against a wall it rebounds. A voice spoken in the
6351	Egyptian pyramids is so broken, that it gives four or five echoes.
6352	
6353	
6354	
6355	
6356	CHAPTER XXI.
6357	
6358	BY WHAT MEANS THE SOUL IS SENSIBLE, AND WHAT IS THE PRINCIPAL AND
6359	COMMANDING PART OF IT.
6360	
6361	
6362	The Stoics say that the highest part of the soul is the commanding
6363	part of it: this is the cause of sense, imagination, consents, and
6364	desires; and this we call the rational part. From this principal and
6365	commander there are produced seven parts of the soul, which are spread
6366	through the body, as the seven arms in a polypus. Of these seven parts,
6367	five are assigned to the senses, seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting,
6368	touching. Sight is a spirit which is extended from the commanding
6369	part to the eyes; hearing is that spirit which from the principal
6370	reacheth to the ears; smelling a spirit drawn from the principal to the
6371	nostrils; tasting a spirit extended from the principal to the tongue;
6372	touching is a spirit which from the principal is drawn to the extremity
6373	of those bodies which are obnoxious to a sensible touch. Of the rest,
6374	the one called the spermatical is a spirit which reacheth from the
6375	principal to the generating vessels; the other, which is the vocal
6376	and termed the voice, is a spirit extended from the principal to the
6377	throat, tongue, and other proper organs of speaking. And this principal
6378	part itself hath that place in our spherical head which God hath in the
6379	world.
6380	
6381	
6382	
6383	
6384	CHAPTER XXII.
6385	
6386	OF RESPIRATION OR BREATHING.
6387	
6388	
6389	Empedocles thinks, that the first breath the first animal drew was
6390	when the moisture in unborn infants was separated, and by that means
6391	an entrance was given to the external air into the gaping vessels, the
6392	moisture in them being evacuated. After this the natural heat, in a
6393	violent force pressing upon the external air for a passage, begets an
6394	expiration; but this heat returning to the inward parts, and the air
6395	giving way to it, causeth an inspiration. The respiration that now is
6396	arises when the blood is carried to the exterior surface, and by this
6397	fluxion drives the airy substance through the nostrils; thus in its
6398	recess it causeth expiration, but the air being again forced into those
6399	places which are emptied of blood, it causeth an inspiration. To evince
6400	which, he proposeth the instance of a water-clock which gives the
6401	account of time by the running of water.
6402	
6403	Asclepiades supposeth the lungs to be in the manner of a tunnel, and
6404	maketh the cause of breathing to be the fineness of the inward parts of
6405	the breast; for thither the outward air which is more gross hastens,
6406	but is forced backward, the breast not being capable either to receive
6407	or want it. But there being always some of the more tenuous parts of
6408	the air left, so that all of it is not exploded, to that which there
6409	remains the more ponderous external air with equal violence is forced;
6410	and this he compares to cupping-glasses. All spontaneous breathings are
6411	formed by the contracting of the smaller pores of the lungs, and to the
6412	closing up of the pipes in the neck; for these are at our command.
6413	
6414	Herophilus attributes a moving faculty to the nerves, arteries, and
6415	muscles, but believes that the lungs are affected only with a natural
6416	desire of enlarging and contracting themselves. Farther, there is the
6417	first operation of the lungs by attraction of the outward air, which is
6418	drawn in because of the abundance of the external air. Next to this,
6419	there is a second natural appetite of the lungs; the breast, pouring in
6420	upon itself the breath, and being filled, is no longer able to make an
6421	attraction, and throws the superfluity of it upon the lungs, whereby
6422	it is in turn sent forth by way of expiration; the parts of the body
6423	mutually concurring to this function by the alternate participation of
6424	fulness and emptiness. So that to lungs pertain four motions;—first,
6425	when the lungs receive the outward air; secondly, when the outward air
6426	thus entertained is transmitted to the breast; thirdly, when the lungs
6427	again receive that air which they imparted to the breast; fourthly,
6428	when this air then received from the breast is thrown outwards. Of
6429	these four motions two are dilatations, one when the lungs attract the
6430	external air, another when the breast dischargeth itself of it upon the
6431	lungs; two are contractions, one when the breast draws into itself the
6432	air, the second when it expels this which was insinuated into it. The
6433	breast admits only of two motions;—of dilatation, when it draws from
6434	the lungs the breath, and of contraction, when it returns what it did
6435	receive.
6436	
6437	
6438	
6439	
6440	CHAPTER XXIII.
6441	
6442	OF THE PASSIONS OF THE BODY, AND WHETHER THE SOUL HATH A SYMPATHETICAL
6443	CONDOLENCY WITH IT.
6444	
6445	
6446	The Stoics say that all the passions are seated in those parts of
6447	the body which are affected, the senses have their residence in the
6448	commanding part of the soul. Epicurus, that all the passions and all
6449	the senses are in those parts which are affected, but the commanding
6450	part is subject to no passion. Strato, that all the passions and senses
6451	of the soul are in the rational or commanding part of it, and are not
6452	fixed in those places which are affected; for in this part patience
6453	takes its residence, and this is apparent in terrible and dolorous
6454	things, as also in timorous and valiant persons.

6459	BOOK V.
6460	
6461	
6462	
6463	
6464	CHAPTER I.
6465	
6466	OF DIVINATION.
6467	
6468	
6469	Plato and the Stoics introduce divination as a divine enthusiasm, the
6470	soul itself being of a divine constitution, and this prophetic faculty
6471	being an inspiration, or an illapse of the divine knowledge into man;
6472	and so likewise they explain interpretation by dreams. And these same
6473	admit many divisions of the art of divination. Xenophanes and Epicurus
6474	utterly refuse any such art of foretelling future contingencies.
6475	Pythagoras rejects all manner of divination which is by sacrifices.
6476	Aristotle and Dicaearchus admit only these two kinds of it, a fury by a
6477	divine inspiration, and dreams; they deny the immortality of the soul,
6478	yet they affirm that the mind of man hath a participation of something
6479	that is divine.
6480	
6481	
6482	
6483	
6484	CHAPTER II.
6485	
6486	WHENCE DREAMS DO ARISE.
6487	
6488	
6489	Democritus says that dreams are formed by the illapse of adventitious
6490	representations. Strato, that the irrational part of the soul in sleep
6491	becoming more sensible is moved by the rational part of it. Herophilus,
6492	that dreams which are caused by divine instinct have a necessary cause;
6493	but dreams which have their origin from a natural cause arise from
6494	the soul’s forming within itself the images of those things which are
6495	convenient for it, and which will happen; those dreams which are of a
6496	constitution mixed of both these have their origin from the fortuitous
6497	appulse of images, as when we see those things which please us; thus
6498	it happens many times to those persons who in their sleep imagine they
6499	embrace their mistresses.

6504	CHAPTER III.
6505	
6506	OF THE NATURE OF GENERATIVE SEED.
6507	
6508	
6509	Aristotle says, that seed is that thing which contains in itself a
6510	power of moving, whereby it is enabled to produce a being like unto
6511	that from whence it was emitted. Pythagoras, that seed is the sediment
6512	of that which nourisheth us, the froth of the purest blood, of the
6513	same nature as the blood and marrow of our bodies. Alcmaeon, that it
6514	is a part of the brain. Plato, that it is the deflux of the spinal
6515	marrow. Epicurus, that it is a fragment torn from the body and soul.
6516	Democritus, that it proceeds from all the parts of the body, and
6517	chiefly from the principal parts, as the flesh and muscles.

6523	
6524	WHETHER THE SPERM BE A BODY.
6525	
6526	
6527	Leucippus and Zeno say, that it is a body and a fragment of the
6528	soul. Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle, that the spermatic faculty
6529	is incorporeal, as the mind is which moves the body; but the effused
6530	matter is corporeal. Strato and Democritus, that the very power is a
6531	body; for it is like spirit.

6536	CHAPTER V.
6537	
6538	WHETHER WOMEN DO GIVE A SPERMATIC EMISSION AS MEN DO.
6539	
6540	
6541	Pythagoras, Epicurus, and Democritus say, that women have a seminal
6542	projection, but their spermatic vessels are inverted; and it is this
6543	that makes them have a venereal appetite. Aristotle and Plato, that
6544	they emit a material moisture, as sweat we see produced by exercise and
6545	labor; but that moisture has no spermatic power. Hippo, that women
6546	have a seminal emission, but not after the mode of men; it contributes
6547	nothing to generation, for it falls without the matrix; and therefore
6548	some women without coition, especially widows, give the seed. The same
6549	also asserts that from men the bones, from women the flesh proceeds.

6554	CHAPTER VI.
6555	
6556	HOW IT IS THAT CONCEPTIONS ARE MADE.
6557	
6558	
6559	Aristotle says, that conception takes place when the womb is drawn
6560	forward by the natural purgation, and the monthly terms attract from
6561	the whole bulk part of the purest blood, and this is met by the genital
6562	seed of man. On the contrary, there is a failure by the impurity and
6563	inflation of the womb, by the passions of fear and grief, by the
6564	weakness of women, or the decay of strength in men.

6569	CHAPTER VII.
6570	
6571	AFTER WHAT MANNER MALES AND FEMALES ARE GENERATED.
6572	
6573	
6574	Empedocles affirms, that heat and cold give the difference in the
6575	generation of males and females. Hence is it, as histories acquaint us,
6576	that the first men had their original from the earth in the eastern and
6577	southern parts, and the first females in the northern parts thereof.
6578	Parmenides is of opinion perfectly contrariant. He affirms that men
6579	first sprouted out of the northern earth, for their bodies are more
6580	dense; women out of the southern, for theirs are more rare and fine.
6581	Hippo, that the more compacted and strong sperm, and the more fluid and
6582	weak, discriminate the sexes. Anaxagoras and Parmenides, that the seed
6583	of the man is naturally cast from his right side into the right side
6584	of the womb, or from the left side of the man into the left side of
6585	the womb; when there is an alteration in this course of nature, females
6586	are generated. Cleophanes, whom Aristotle makes mention of, assigns
6587	the generation of men to the right testicle, of women to the left.
6588	Leucippus gives the reason of it to the alteration or diversity of
6589	parts, according to which the man hath a yard, the female the matrix;
6590	as to any other reason he is silent. Democritus, that the parts which
6591	are common to both sexes are engendered indifferently by one or the
6592	other; but the peculiar parts by the one that is more prevalent. Hippo,
6593	that if the spermatic faculty be more effectual, the male, if the
6594	nutritive aliment, the female is generated.

6599	CHAPTER VIII.
6600	
6601	BY WHAT MEANS IT IS THAT MONSTROUS BIRTHS ARE EFFECTED.
6602	
6603	
6604	Empedocles believes that monsters receive their origination from the
6605	abundance or defect of seed, or from its division into parts which are
6606	superabundant, or from some perturbation in the motion, or else that
6607	there is an error by a lapse into an improper receptacle; and thus he
6608	presumes he hath given all the causes of monstrous conceptions. Strato,
6609	that it comes from addition, subtraction, or transposition of the seed,
6610	or the distension or inflation of the matrix. And some physicians say
6611	that the matrix suffers distortion, being distended with wind.

6616	CHAPTER IX.
6617	
6618	HOW IT COMES TO PASS THAT A WOMAN’S TOO FREQUENT CONVERSATION WITH A
6619	MAN HINDERS CONCEPTION.
6620	
6621	
6622	Diocles the physician says that either no genital sperm is projected,
6623	or, if there be, it is in a less quantity than nature requires, or
6624	there is no prolific faculty in it; or there is a deficiency of a
6625	due proportion of heat, cold, moisture, and dryness; or there is a
6626	resolution of the generative parts. The Stoics attribute sterility to
6627	the obliquity of the yard, by which means it is not able to ejaculate
6628	in a due manner, or to the unproportionable magnitude of the parts,
6629	the matrix being so contracted as not to be in a capacity to receive.
6630	Erasistratus assigns it to the womb’s being more callous or more
6631	carneous, thinner or smaller, than nature does require.

6636	CHAPTER X.
6637	
6638	WHENCE IT IS THAT ONE BIRTH GIVES TWO OR THREE CHILDREN.
6639	
6640	
6641	Empedocles affirms, that the superabundance of sperm and the division
6642	of it causes the bringing forth of two or three infants. Asclepiades,
6643	that it is performed from the excellent quality of the sperm, after
6644	the manner that from the root of one barleycorn two or three stalks do
6645	grow; sperm that is of this quality is the most prolific. Erasistratus,
6646	that superfetation may happen to women as to irrational creatures; for,
6647	if the womb be well purged and very clean, then there may be divers
6648	births. The Stoics, that it ariseth from the various receptacles that
6649	are in the womb: when the seed illapses into the first and second of
6650	them at once, then there are conceptions upon conception; and so two or
6651	three infants are born.

6656	CHAPTER XI.
6657	
6658	WHENCE IT IS THAT CHILDREN REPRESENT THEIR PARENTS AND PROGENTITORS.
6659	
6660	
6661	Empedocles says, that the similitude of children to their parents
6662	proceeds from the vigorous prevalency of the generating sperm; the
6663	dissimilitude from the evaporation of the natural heat contained in
6664	the same. Parmenides, that when the sperm descends from the right side
6665	of the womb, then the infant gives the resemblance of the father; if
6666	from the left, it is stamped with the similitude of the mother. The
6667	Stoics, that the whole body and soul give the sperm; and hence arise
6668	the resemblances in the characters and figures of the children, as a
6669	painter in his copy imitates the colors which are in the picture before
6670	him. Women have a concurrent emission of seed; if the feminine seed
6671	have the predominancy, then the child resembles the mother; if the
6672	masculine, the father.

6677	CHAPTER XII.
6678	
6679	HOW IT COMES TO PASS THAT CHILDREN HAVE A GREATER SIMILITUDE WITH
6680	STRANGERS THAN WITH THEIR PARENTS.
6681	
6682	
6683	The greatest part of physicians affirm, that this happens casually and
6684	fortuitously; for, when the sperm of the man and woman is too much
6685	refrigerated, then children carry a dissimilitude to their parents.
6686	Empedocles, that a woman’s imagination when she conceives impresses
6687	a shape upon the infant; for women have been enamored with images
6688	and statues, and the children which were born of them gave their
6689	similitudes. The Stoics, that the resemblances flow from the sympathy
6690	and consent of minds, by the insertion of effluvias and rays, not of
6691	images or pictures.

6696	CHAPTER XIII.
6697	
6698	WHENCE ARISETH BARRENNESS IN WOMEN, AND IMPOTENCY IN MEN?
6699	
6700	
6701	The physicians maintain, that sterility in women may arise from the
6702	womb; for if it be after any ways thus affected, there will be
6703	barrenness,—if it be more condensed, or more spongy, or more hardened,
6704	or more callous, or more carneous; or it may be from low spirits,
6705	or from an atrophy or vicious distemper of body; or, lastly, it may
6706	arise from a twisted or distorted configuration. Diocles holds that
6707	the sterility in men ariseth from some of these causes,—either that
6708	they cannot at all ejaculate any sperm, or if they do, it is less
6709	than nature doth require, or else there is no generative faculty in
6710	the sperm, or the genital members are flagging; or from the obliquity
6711	of the yard. The Stoics attribute the cause of sterility to the
6712	contrariant qualities and dispositions of those who lie with one
6713	another; but if it chance that these persons are separated, and there
6714	happen a conjunction of those who are of a suitable temperament, then
6715	there is a commixture according to nature, and by this means an infant
6716	is formed.

6721	CHAPTER XIV.
6722	
6723	HOW IF COMES TO PASS THAT MULES ARE BARREN.
6724	
6725	
6726	Alcmaeon says, that the barrenness of the male mules ariseth from the
6727	thinness of the genital sperm, that is, the seed is too chill; the
6728	female mules are barren, for their womb does not open its mouth (as
6729	he expresses it). Empedocles, the matrix of the mule is so small, so
6730	depressed, so narrow, so invertedly growing to the belly, that the
6731	sperm cannot be regularly cast into it, and if it could, there would be
6732	no capacity to receive it. Diocles concurs in this opinion with him;
6733	for, saith he, in our anatomical dissection of mules we have seen that
6734	their matrices are of such configurations; and it is possible that
6735	there may be the same reason why some women are barren.

6740	CHAPTER XV.
6741	
6742	WHETHER THE INFANT IN THE MOTHER’S WOMB BE AN ANIMAL.
6743	
6744	
6745	Plato says, that the embryo is an animal; for, being contained in the
6746	mother’s womb, motion and aliment are imparted to it. The Stoics say
6747	that it is not an animal, but to be accounted part of the mother’s
6748	belly; like as we see the fruit of trees is esteemed part of the
6749	trees, until it be full ripe; then it falls and ceaseth to belong to
6750	the tree; and thus it is with the embryo. Empedocles, that the embryo
6751	is not an animal, yet whilst it remains in the belly it breathes. The
6752	first breath that it draws as an animal is when the infant is newly
6753	born; then the child having its moisture separated, the extraneous
6754	air making an entrance into the empty places, a respiration is caused
6755	in the infant by the empty vessels receiving of it. Diogenes, that
6756	infants are bred in the matrix inanimate, yet they have a natural
6757	heat; but presently, when the infant is cast into the open air, its
6758	heat draws air into the lungs, and so it becomes an animal. Herophilus
6759	acknowledgeth that infants have a natural, but not a respiratory
6760	motion, and that the nerves are the cause of that motion; that then
6761	they become animals, when being first born they suck in something of
6762	the air.

6767	CHAPTER XVI.
6768	
6769	HOW EMBRYOS ARE NOURISHED, OR HOW THE INFANT IN THE BELLY RECEIVES ITS
6770	ALIMENT.
6771	
6772	
6773	Democritus and Epicurus say, that the embryos in the womb receive their
6774	aliment by the mouth, for we perceive, as soon as ever the infant is
6775	born, it applies its mouth to the breast; in the wombs of women (our
6776	understanding concludes) there are little dugs, and the embryos have
6777	small mouths by which they receive their nutriment. The Stoics, that
6778	by the secundines and navel they partake of aliment, and therefore the
6779	midwife instantly after their birth binds the navel, and opens the
6780	infant’s mouth, that it may receive another sort of aliment. Alcmaeon,
6781	that they receive their nourishment from every part of the body; as a
6782	sponge sucks in water.

6789	WHAT PART OF THE BODY IS FIRST FORMED IN THE WOMB.

6802	CHAPTER XVIII.
6803	
6804	WHENCE IS IT THAT INFANTS BORN IN THE SEVENTH MONTH ARE BORN ALIVE.
6805	
6806	
6807	Empedocles says, that when the human race took first its original from
6808	the earth, the sun was so slow in its motion that then one day in its
6809	length was equal to ten months, as now they are; in process of time one
6810	day became as long as seven months are; and there is the reason that
6811	those infants which are born at the end of seven months or ten months
6812	are born alive, the course of nature so disposing that the infant
6813	shall be brought to maturity in one day after that night in which it
6814	is begotten. Timaeus says, that we count not ten months but nine, by
6815	treason that we reckon the first conception from the retention of
6816	the menstruas; and so it may generally pass for seven months when
6817	really there are not seven; for it sometimes happens that even after
6818	conception a woman is purged in some degree. Polybus, Diocles, and the
6819	Empirics acknowledge that the eighth month gives a vital birth to the
6820	infant, though the life of it is more faint and languid; many therefore
6821	we see born in that month die out of mere weakness. Though we see many
6822	born in that month arrive at the state of man, yet (they affirm) if
6823	children be born in that month, none are willing to rear them.
6824	
6825	Aristotle and Hippocrates, that if the womb is grown full in seven
6826	months, then the child falls from the mother and is born alive; but if
6827	it falls from her but is not properly nourished, the navel being weak
6828	on account of the heavy burden of the infant, then it doth not thrive;
6829	but if the infant continues nine months in the womb, and then breaks
6830	forth from the woman, it is entire and perfect. Polybus, that a hundred
6831	and eighty-two days and a half suffice for the bringing forth of a
6832	living child; that is, six months, in which space of time the sun moves
6833	from one tropic to the other; and this is called seven months, for the
6834	days which are overplus in the sixth are accounted to give the seventh
6835	month. Those children which are born in the eighth month cannot live,
6836	for, the infant then falling from the womb, the navel, which is the
6837	cause of nourishment, is thereby too much stretched; and is the reason
6838	that the infant languishes and hath an atrophy. The astrologers, that
6839	eight months are enemies to every birth, seven are friends and kind
6840	to it. The signs of the zodiac are then enemies, when they fall upon
6841	those stars which are lords of houses; whatever infant is then born
6842	will have a life short and unfortunate. Those signs of the zodiac which
6843	are malevolent and injurious to generation are those pairs of which
6844	the last is reckoned the eighth from the first, as the first and the
6845	eighth, the second and the ninth, &c.; so is the Ram unsociable with
6846	Scorpio, the Bull with Sagittarius, the Twins with the Goat, the Crab
6847	with Aquarius, the Lion with Pisces, the Virgin with the Ram. Upon this
6848	reason those infants that are born in the seventh or tenth months are
6849	like to live, but those in the eighth month will die.

6854	CHAPTER XIX.
6855	
6856	OF THE GENERATION OF ANIMALS, HOW ANIMALS ARE BEGOTTEN, AND WHETHER
6857	THEY ARE OBNOXIOUS TO CORRUPTION.
6858	
6859	
6860	Those philosophers who entertain the opinion that the world had
6861	an original do likewise assert that all animals are generated and
6862	corruptible. The followers of Epicurus, who gives an eternity to the
6863	world, affirm the generation of animals ariseth from the various
6864	permutation of parts mutually among themselves, for they are parts of
6865	this world. With them Anaxagoras and Euripides concur:
6866	
6867	                            For nothing dies,
6868	  But different changes give their various forms.
6869	
6870	Anaximander’s opinion is, that the first animals were generated in
6871	moisture, and were enclosed in bark on which thorns grew; but in
6872	process of time they came upon dry land, and this thorny bark with
6873	which they were covered being broken, they lived for a short space of
6874	time. Empedocles says, that the first generation of animals and plants
6875	was by no means completed, for the parts were disjoined and would not
6876	admit of a union; the second preparation for their being generated was
6877	when their parts were united and appeared in the form of images; the
6878	third preparation for generation was when their parts mutually amongst
6879	themselves gave a being to one another; the fourth, when there was no
6880	longer a mixture of similar elements (like earth and water), but a
6881	union of animals among themselves,—in some the nourishment being made
6882	dense, in others female beauty provoking a lust of spermatic motion.
6883	All sorts of animals are discriminated by their proper temperament and
6884	constitution; some are carried by a proper appetite and inclination to
6885	water; some, which partake of a more fiery quality, to breathe in the
6886	air; those that are heavier incline to the earth; but those animals
6887	whose parts are of a just and equal temperament are fitted equally for
6888	all places.

6893	CHAPTER XX.
6894	
6895	HOW MANY SPECIES OF ANIMALS THERE ARE, AND WHETHER ALL ANIMALS HAVE THE
6896	ENDOWMENTS OF SENSE AND REASON.
6897	
6898	
6899	There is a certain treatise of Aristotle, in which animals are
6900	distributed into four kinds, terrestrial, aqueous, fowl, and heavenly;
6901	and he calls the stars and the world also animals, yea, and God himself
6902	he defines to be an animal endowed with reason and immortal. Democritus
6903	and Epicurus esteem all animals rational which have their residence in
6904	the heavens. Anaxagoras says that animals have only that reason which
6905	is operative, but not that which is passive, which is justly styled the
6906	interpreter of the mind, and is like the mind itself. Pythagoras and
6907	Plato, that the souls of all those who are styled brutes are rational;
6908	but by the evil constitution of their bodies, and because they have
6909	a want of a discoursive faculty, they do not act rationally. This is
6910	manifested in apes and dogs, which have voice but not speech. Diogenes,
6911	that this sort of animals are partakers of intelligence and air, but
6912	by reason of the density in some parts of them, and by the superfluity
6913	of moisture in others, they enjoy neither understanding nor sense; but
6914	they are affected as madmen are, the commanding rational part being
6915	defectuous and impeached.

6920	CHAPTER XXI.
6921	
6922	WHAT TIME IS REQUIRED TO SHAPE THE PARTS OF ANIMALS IN THE WOMB.
6923	
6924	
6925	Empedocles believes, that the joints of men begin to be formed from
6926	the thirty-sixth day, and their shape is completed in the nine and
6927	fortieth. Asclepiades, that male embryos, by reason of a greater
6928	natural heat, have their joints begun to be formed in the twenty-sixth
6929	day,—many even sooner,—and that they are completed in all their parts
6930	on the fiftieth day; the parts of the females are articulated in two
6931	months, but by the defect of heat are not consummated till the fourth;
6932	but the members of brutes are completed at various times, according to
6933	the commixture of the elements of which they consist.

6938	CHAPTER XXII.
6939	
6940	OF WHAT ELEMENTS EACH OF THE MEMBERS OF US MEN IS COMPOSED.
6941	
6942	
6943	Empedocles says, that the fleshy parts of us are constituted by the
6944	contemperation of the four elements in us; earth and fire mixed with a
6945	double proportion of water make the nerves; but when it happens that
6946	the nerves are refrigerated where they meet the air, then the nails are

6948	with four parts of fire and the same of earth, duly mixed together;
6949	sweat and tears flow from the liquefaction of these bodies of ours.

6954	CHAPTER XXIII.
6955	
6956	WHAT ARE THE CAUSES OF SLEEP AND DEATH?
6957	
6958	
6959	Alcmaeon says, that sleep is caused when the blood retreats to the
6960	concourse of the veins, but when the blood diffuses itself, then we
6961	awake; and when there is a total retirement of the blood, then men die.
6962	Empedocles, that a moderate cooling of the blood causeth sleep, but a
6963	total remotion of heat from blood causeth death. Diogenes, that when
6964	all the blood is so diffused as that it fills all the veins, and forces
6965	the air contained in them to the back and to the belly that is below
6966	it, the breast being thereby more heated, thence sleep arises; but if
6967	every thing that is airy in the breast forsakes the veins, then death
6968	succeeds. Plato and the Stoics, that sleep ariseth from the relaxation
6969	of the sensitive spirit, it not receiving such total remission as if
6970	it fell to the earth, but so that that spirit is carried about the
6971	intestine parts of the eyebrows, in which the principal part has its
6972	residence; but when there is a total remission of the sensitive spirit,
6973	then death ensues.

6978	CHAPTER XXIV.
6979	
6980	WHEN AND FROM WHENCE THE PERFECTION OF A MAN COMMENCES.
6981	
6982	
6983	Heraclitus and the Stoics say, that men begin their completeness when
6984	the second septenary of years begins, about which time the seminal
6985	serum is emitted. Trees first begin their perfection when they give
6986	their seeds; till then they are immature, imperfect, and unfruitful.
6987	After the same manner a man is completed in the second septenary
6988	of years, and is capable of learning what is good and evil, and of
6989	discipline therein.

6994	CHAPTER XXV.
6995	
6996	WHETHER SLEEP OR DEATH APPERTAINS TO THE SOUL OR BODY.
6997	
6998	
6999	Aristotle’s opinion is, that both the soul and body sleep; and this
7000	proceeds from the moisture in the breast, which doth steam and arise
7001	in the manner of a vapor into the head, and from the aliment in the
7002	stomach, whose natural heat is cooled in the heart. Death is the
7003	perfect refrigeration of all heat in the body; but death is only of
7004	the body, and not of the soul, for the soul is immortal. Anaxagoras
7005	thinks, that sleep makes the operations of the body to cease; it is a
7006	corporeal passion and affects not the soul. Death is the separation of
7007	the soul from the body. Leucippus, that sleep is only of the body; but
7008	when the smaller particles cause immoderate evaporation from the soul’s
7009	heat, this makes death; but these affections of death and sleep are of
7010	the body, not of the soul. Empedocles, that death is nothing else but
7011	separation of those fiery parts by which man is composed, and according
7012	to this sentiment both body and soul die; but sleep is only a smaller
7013	separation of the fiery qualities.

7016	
7017	
7018	CHAPTER XXVI.
7019	
7020	HOW PLANTS GROW, AND WHETHER THEY ARE ANIMALS.
7021	
7022	
7023	Plato and Empedocles believe, that plants are animals, and are informed
7024	with a soul; of this there are clear arguments, for they have tossing
7025	and shaking, and their branches are extended; when the woodmen bend
7026	them they yield, but they return to their former straightness and
7027	strength again when they are let loose, and even draw up weights that
7028	are laid upon them. Aristotle doth grant that they live, but not that
7029	they are animals; for animals are affected with appetite, sense, and
7030	reason. The Stoics and Epicureans deny that they are informed with a
7031	soul; by reason that all sorts of animals have either sense, appetite,
7032	or reason; but plants move fortuitously, and not by means of any soul.
7033	Empedocles, that the first of all animals were trees, and they sprang
7034	from the earth before the sun in its glory enriched the world, and
7035	before day and night were distinguished; but by the harmony which is
7036	in their constitution they partake of a masculine and feminine nature;
7037	and they increase by that heat which is exalted out of the earth, so
7038	that they are parts belonging to it, as embryos in the womb are parts
7039	of the womb. Fruits in plants are excrescences proceeding from water
7040	and fire; but the plants which have a deficiency of water, when this
7041	is dried up by the heat of summer, lose their leaves; whereas they
7042	that have plenty thereof keep their leaves on still, as the olive,
7043	laurel, and palm. The differences of their moisture and juice arise
7044	from the difference of particles and various other causes, and they
7045	are discriminated by the various particles that feed them. And this
7046	is apparent in vines; for the excellence of wine flows not from the
7047	difference in the vines, but from the soil from whence they receive
7048	their nutriment.

7080	CHAPTER XXIX.
7081	
7082	WHAT IS THE CAUSE OF A FEVER, OR WHETHER IT IS AN AFFECTION OF THE BODY
7083	ANNEXED TO A PRIMARY PASSION.
7084	
7085	
7086	Erasistratus gives this definition of a fever: A fever is a quick
7087	motion of blood, not produced by our consent, which enters into the
7088	vessels proper unto the vital spirits. This we see in the sea; it is
7089	in a serene calm when nothing disturbs it, but is in motion when a
7090	violent preternatural wind blows upon it, and then it rageth and is
7091	circled with waves. After this manner it is in the body of man; when
7092	the blood is in a nimble agitation, then it falls upon those vessels
7093	in which the spirits are, and there being in an extraordinary heat,
7094	it fires the whole body. The opinion that a fever is an appendix to a
7095	preceding affection pleaseth him. Diocles proceeds after this manner:
7096	Those things which are internal and latent are manifested by those
7097	which externally break forth and appear; and it is clear to us that a
7098	fever is annexed to certain outward affections, for example, to wounds,
7099	inflaming tumors, inguinary abscesses.

7104	CHAPTER XXX.
7105	
7106	OF HEALTH, SICKNESS, AND OLD AGE.
7107	
7108	
7109	Alcmaeon says that the preserver of health is an equal proportion of
7110	the qualities of heat, moisture, cold, dryness, bitterness, sweetness,
7111	and the other qualities; on the contrary, the prevailing empire of one
7112	above the rest is the cause of diseases and author of destruction. The
7113	efficient cause of disease is the excess of heat or cold, the material
7114	cause is superabundance or defect, the place is the blood or brain.
7115	But health is the harmonious commixture of the elements. Diocles, that
7116	sickness for the most part proceeds from the irregular disposition of
7117	the elements in the body, for that makes an ill habit or constitution
7118	of it. Erasistratus, that sickness is caused by the excess of food,
7119	indigestion, and corruptions; on the contrary, health is the moderation
7120	of the diet, and the taking that which is convenient and sufficient for
7121	us. It is the unanimous opinion of the Stoics that the want of heat
7122	brings old age, for (they say) those persons in whom heat more abounds
7123	live the longer. Asclepiades, that the Ethiopians soon grow old, and
7124	at thirty years of age are ancient men, their bodies being excessively
7125	heated and scorched by the sun; in Britain persons live a hundred and
7126	twenty years, on account of the coldness of the country, and because
7127	the people contain the fiery element within their bodies; for the
7128	bodies of the Ethiopians are more fine and thin, because they are
7129	relaxed by the sun’s heat, while they who live in northern countries
7130	have a contrary state of their bodies, for they are condensed and
7131	robust, and by consequence live the longer.
