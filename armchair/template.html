Of {{n_sc}} ancient answers about nature, {{s_lo}} hold up

In March 1610 Galileo reported that a telescope turned on the Milky Way showed it to be a mass of stars, as Democritus had said some two thousand years before. Histories of science collect moments like this one. This page counts the misses as well, using an ancient handbook that set down the philosophers' answers side by side, long before anyone knew which would last. Two AI raters graded each answer against present science.

Why count the misses?

The usual list of anticipations is built backwards. It starts from a modern result, such as atoms, a round Earth or galaxies beyond our own, and searches the old texts for a sentence that resembles it. With enough texts, some sentence always does. The historian Quentin Skinner gave the habit a name in 1969, the mythology of prolepsis: reading an old author for the significance a later age gives the words, not for what the author meant. A fair count needs a sample that was drawn before the outcome was known, and that keeps the claims nobody now remembers.

Such a sample exists. The Placita, or opinions of the philosophers, is a handbook of answers to questions about nature. The ancient tradition attributed it to Plutarch; modern scholars trace it to a compiler called Aetius, whom they date to the first century AD or a little later. Its five books go question by question: what things are made of, the shape of the world, what the stars are, why the Moon shines, what causes earthquakes, how we see and how embryos grow. Under each question the compiler lists the schools' answers side by side, with names. He was recording opinion, not picking winners, so the right answers sit beside the wrong ones in the proportions he found.

How were the answers scored?

The text is John Dowel's English translation, as printed in W. W. Goodwin's edition of Plutarch's essays (Project Gutenberg eBook 78851). Each attributed answer became one row: {{n_op}} rows in {{n_ch}} chapters. Each row has a short neutral paraphrase and the line numbers of the passage. A paraphrase does not say whether the answer is right.

Two raters graded every row against present science. Each worked alone, without seeing the other's grades. The grades are: strong, the core claim matches present science and could have been wrong; partial, right in one key respect but wrong in mechanism or detail; loose, a resemblance in words only; wrong; and unscorable, for answers with nothing physical to check, such as claims about the gods. Both raters found {{n_sc}} rows checkable. Across all rows, unscorable ones included, they gave the same grade {{agree5}} percent of the time (Cohen's kappa {{kappa5}}). Where the grades differ, the page uses the lower one.

The raters were two separate sessions of an AI language model, not historians. Their agreement shows that the grades are consistent, not that they are expert judgements. Every grade is in the download, with its reason, so a reader can check any row.

What does the crowd show?

Of the {{n_sc}} checkable answers, {{s_lo}} are strong ({{s_lo_pct}} percent, about one in {{one_in}}). Another {{p_lo}} are partial, {{l_lo}} are loose and {{w_lo}} are wrong ({{w_lo_pct}} percent). If the higher of the two grades is used instead, strong answers rise to {{s_hi}} and wrong ones fall to {{w_hi}}. Either way, for each answer that holds up in full, the handbook carries between {{ratio_hi}} and {{ratio_lo}} wrong ones.

The right answers cluster. Of the {{s_lo}} strong answers, {{s_book2}} are in the book on the heavens: the Moon takes its light from the Sun; an eclipse of the Sun is the Moon passing in front of it; the Sun and the Moon are spheres. These were questions that patient naked-eye observation could settle, and the handbook's astronomers had settled several of them. At the other end, for {{q_all_wrong}} of the {{q_sc}} questions with a checkable answer, every checkable answer is wrong. They include what causes lightning, what lights the stars, where dreams come from, and how conception happens.

The famous names look different in the crowd. Democritus, credited with the atom, has {{dem_n}} checkable answers in the handbook: {{dem_s}} strong, {{dem_p}} partial and {{dem_w}} wrong. In the same pages he holds that the Earth is shaped like a discus, hollow in the middle, and that earthquakes are caused by water. The Stoics, seldom cited as precursors of anything, have the most strong answers of any school ({{sto_s}} of {{sto_n}}), and {{sto_w}} wrong ones. Even the Milky Way chapter, the source of Democritus's best-known hit, gives {{mw_n}} answers. His is the only one close to right, and it adds that the packed stars light one another. How long did the right answers wait?

A second set of cases comes from the wider literature rather than the handbook: {{c_n}} ideas often cited as anticipations, or as confident claims that failed. {{timeline}}

The ancient hits waited a median of {{gap_anc}} years, and in more than half of them the wait ended when an instrument arrived, not an argument. The Milky Way waited for the telescope in 1610. The claim that light takes time to travel, which Aristotle reports from Empedocles and rejects, waited for Ole Rømer's timings of Jupiter's moon Io in 1676. Atoms waited for Jean Perrin, who counted particles in suspensions under a microscope in 1908 and 1909. Hits from 1400 on waited a median of {{gap_mod}} years. Kant argued in 1755 that the faint elliptical nebulae were whole star systems like our own. Edwin Hubble's distances to two such nebulae, measured from Cepheid stars in them (stars whose pulsing gives away their true brightness), were read out at a meeting in 1925, {{gap_kant}} years later.

Wrong claims from the same period did not last as long: a median of {{gap_modmiss}} years before a measurement refuted them. Both medians count only ideas that have been settled; an idea still open would lengthen them. In 1835 Auguste Comte gave the chemical make-up of the stars as an example of what could never be known; in 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff identified sodium in the Sun's spectrum. Wilhelm Ostwald argued in 1895 that atoms were a dispensable hypothesis, and conceded in 1908, after Perrin's first results. A claim that names something observable is tested once the instrument exists, and by 1800 the instruments came quickly.

Right in part, wrong in the same passage

The middle grades, partial and loose, hold the most instructive cases. Aristotle worked out that in a void all bodies would fall at the same speed, then treated that result as absurd and used it to deny that a void could exist. Edgar Allan Poe, in Eureka (1848), saw that a dark night sky could mean that light from the most distant stars has not yet reached us, then said there was no reason to believe it. Lord Kelvin made the calculation in 1901 and showed that it accounts for the dark sky. Kant's flat, rotating disc of matter around the young Sun resembles the discs that the Hubble Space Telescope photographed in Orion in 1993, but his mechanism for the rotation breaks the conservation of angular momentum. Even refutations carried errors: Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 table of simple substances, which ended the four elements, lists light and caloric among them.

When physics answered philosophy

Traffic also ran the other way. Kant held that the axioms of Euclid's geometry are known before experience. But the eclipse expeditions of 1919 measured starlight bent by the Sun as general relativity predicted, and the space of that theory is not Euclidean. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued in 1935 that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, on assumptions now called local realism: that no influence travels faster than light, and that measured properties exist before they are measured. Experiments in the following decades favoured quantum mechanics, and in 2015 a test in Delft closed the main loopholes at once, {{gap_epr}} years after the paper. A philosophical thesis became a measurement, and the measurement went against it.

The handbook's compiler could not know which of his answers would survive. Most did not. The ones that did were real achievements, and they were never alone.

Method

Placita. Source: Gutenberg eBook 78851, lines {{line_a}} to {{line_b}}. One row for each distinct attributed answer. If one sentence gives the same answer for several people, that is one row. Grades: two raters worked independently. The consensus grade is the lower of the two. A row counts as checkable only if neither rater graded it unscorable. Agreement: Cohen's kappa is {{kappa5}} on all five grades. On the two-way split between right in the core claim (strong or partial) and not right, kappa is {{kappa2}} and agreement is {{agree2}} percent. A question has a strong answer if at least one of its rows has a strong consensus grade.

Cases. Each case has the year of the passage and one named event, chosen before the gap was computed: a publication or an observation, not textbook acceptance. The gap is the event year minus the idea year. When the idea is BCE and the event is AD, subtract one more, because there is no year 0. The build recomputes every gap and stops if one differs from the case file. "Ancient" means an idea before AD 500; "from 1400 on" means 1400 and after. Medians for hits use strong and partial cases; medians for misses use wrong cases.

Evidence ratings. The ratings are this page's judgement. Strong: a primary text and a dated measurement support the claim. Moderate: the claim rests on a source that compresses or may misattribute.

Limits

The handbook is one compiler's selection, not a census of ancient thought. It favours the schools he knew best. Paraphrase loses nuance, and a grade on a paraphrase can be harsher or kinder than a grade on the Greek. "Present science" moves: an answer graded wrong today could gain partial credit later, and the reverse. The raters are two sessions of one AI model, so their errors may be correlated in ways that two human experts' would not. The {{c_n}} cases are not a sample: they are the cases people cite, plus the best-known misses, and they show gaps, not rates. Some passage references in the case files rest on secondary sources. The notes mark which ones.
