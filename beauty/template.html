On average, critically endangered birds draw about {{ppml_x}} times the research, a gap only just distinguishable from none

Imagine typing a bird's scientific name into OpenAlex, the open index of research, and sorting everything that comes back for 2015 to 2024. Do it for each of the {{n_modelled}} species on this page and most of the pile turns out not to be research at all: only {{share_research_all}}% is. Another {{share_dataset_all}}% are datasets, nearly all of them GBIF occurrence downloads (records created automatically each time someone downloads sightings from a biodiversity database), and {{share_reference_all}}% are Red List assessments and Birds of the World encyclopedia entries. Set that paperwork aside, count research alone and compare like with like, birds of the same family, size, range, time since they were named and rated attractiveness. A typical bird of Least Concern, the Red List's lowest-risk category, then had {{lc_tot}} research works in those ten years and a typical Critically Endangered one {{cr_tot}}, about {{cr_tot_x1}} times as many. {{zero_all_pct}}% of the birds had no research work at all.

Being endangered goes with being studied. Yet none of the {{n_read_word}} published studies of research effort read in full for this page found that link ({{n_null_word}} found no link, {{n_negative_word}} the opposite). That is the trouble with any count of research effort: the number depends on which records are called research, and the headline rarely says which. Nor is it settled here which way the arrow runs, because a bird cannot be listed as endangered without evidence, and evidence is research.

What does a search for a bird find?

Three kinds of record name a bird without being research about it. The largest comes from GBIF, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, which gives each download of its occurrence data a citable identifier and lists the species in its description. So each time a researcher, a student or a consultant downloads the sightings for a region, every bird in them gets a record. These records are {{share_dataset_all}}% of all the records counted here. The Red List publishes each species assessment under its own identifier, and each reassessment gets a new one; the Cornell Lab's Birds of the World does the same for its encyclopedia accounts, and together these two are {{share_reference_all}}%. Research is {{share_research_all}}% (journal articles, reviews, preprints, theses, books and reports), and research is what this page counts. The remaining {{share_other_all}}% is peer-review reports, errata and other items. Less than a third of what a search returns, in other words, is the thing the search is usually taken to measure.

For the typical bird, threatened species have more records of every kind. With the same terms held equal, a Critically Endangered bird has {{ds_cr_x}} times the GBIF downloads of a Least Concern bird and {{ref_cr_x}} times the Red List and Birds of the World entries, because a listing writes some of its own record as reassessments accumulate. Does the paperwork, then, explain the gap? It does not. With every record counted, the Critically Endangered bird has {{old_cr_x}} times the total of a Least Concern bird; counting only research, {{cr_tot_x}} times. The gap widens when the paperwork is removed, because the downloads, which are most of each bird's count, rise less steeply with threat ({{ds_cr_x}} times) than research does. The multiple depends on what is counted, and the narrower count makes it larger, not smaller.

Averages tell a different story, and the reason matters. A Critically Endangered bird has an average of {{cr_mean_research}} research works and a Least Concern bird {{lc_mean_research}}, almost no difference. The Least Concern average is lifted by about {{n_famous_lc}} well-known birds with a hundred research works or more each: the red junglefowl (the chicken's wild ancestor), the rock dove, the mallard and the house sparrow have hundreds or thousands apiece. When a famous few carry an average, the average describes the famous few. Threat and research go together for the typical bird, the obscure majority.

How much more research does an endangered bird get?

Research works per bird species by Red List category, in three series: as counted, adjusted, and adjusted with public attention also held fixed. "Typical" is the back-transformed mean of log(1 + research works), which for counts this skewed is closer to a median than to a mean. The adjusted series show the count that each bird would have in each category in turn, with all its other properties unchanged; notice that the adjusted series rises at every step of threat. Whiskers are 95% intervals from the resampling of whole bird families.

As counted, a Least Concern bird has {{lc_raw}} research works and a Critically Endangered bird {{cr_raw}}. Adjusted, the figures are {{lc_tot}} and {{cr_tot}} (95% interval {{cr_tot_ci}} for the Critically Endangered species), and the adjusted values rise at each step between: Near Threatened {{nt_tot}}, Vulnerable {{vu_tot}}, Endangered {{en_tot}}. (As counted, Near Threatened is just below Least Concern.) Why would holding things equal make the gap wider rather than smaller? Threatened birds tend to have narrow ranges and recent descriptions, and such birds are hard to study, so a raw comparison sets the endangered bird against a handicap. The adjustment holds these terms equal and removes it.

The size of the multiple depends on what is being compared, and the page gives each. For the typical bird it is {{cr_tot_x}} times (95% interval {{cr_x_lo}} to {{cr_x_hi}}, from the resampling of whole families), and for journal articles and reviews alone it is about the same, {{journal_cr_x}} times. For the average count the gap is smaller and much less certain. A count model compares averages, so the few hundred birds with large literatures dominate it, and that model gives {{ppml_x}} times, with an interval from {{ppml_lo}} to {{ppml_hi}}: only just distinguishable from no difference, and with Vulnerable birds below Near Threatened ones. The clearer finding, then, is about the typical bird, the obscure majority. A Critically Endangered member of it has several times the research of a comparable Least Concern one. The famous few dominate the averages whatever their status.

Some of the gap travels with public attention. When English Wikipedia views are also held equal, the multiple falls from {{cr_tot_x}} to {{cr_dir_x}}. But one snapshot cannot show which causes which: a bird with more study may get a better article and more readers, and the reverse is equally possible.

What else goes with being studied?

What the fit of the full model depends on: the decrease in R² when each term is removed and the model is fitted again, with 95% bootstrap intervals over families as whiskers. The full model adds Wikipedia views to the adjusted model above, which raises R² from {{r2_noviews}} to {{r2_full}}. Notice how little is left at the bottom for rated attractiveness.

Wikipedia views give the largest part of the fit ({{views_drop}}), and a bird's family gives nearly as much ({{family_drop}}), because some families are studied as model organisms, game or pests. Red List category comes {{cat_rank_word}} of seven ({{cat_drop}}), behind years since description and range size. Rated attractiveness, scored from photographs by several thousand people, adds {{attr_drop}}. Beauty, the quality that this page was made to test, explains almost nothing once the other terms are known, and its coefficient is indistinguishable from zero (p {{attr_p}}). The page's own address, beauty.html, is named for a hypothesis the data declined to support.

The literature disagrees

{{n_models_word}} species-level models of research effort, across mammals, birds, reptiles, turtles and plants, have been published since 2014, and {{n_threat_word}} of them have a threat-status term in a model. Of the {{n_read}} whose full text could be read for this page, {{n_null}} find threat status null and {{n_negative}} find it negative. The remaining {{n_assessed_word}} finds that an assessment of any kind, not a threatened status, goes with more study. One of the negative results is the only other study of all birds, from 2014, and it is a comparison with nothing else held equal. So who is right? Those studies counted papers in Web of Science, Zoological Record or Scopus, mostly on a single class, while this page uses a different index, a later window and one class, and any of the three could produce a different sign. A single result against a literature is more likely to be wrong than the literature. This page reports what one instrument measured on one class over one decade.

Method

Birds are the only class with a free, species-level source for each term. AVONET gives body mass, range size and family for {{n_avonet}} species, and the iratebirds project gives a rated attractiveness score for {{n_attract}} of them. The GBIF backbone gives the year of description of each species, and for the {{n_year_col}} species with no GBIF year the year is from the Catalogue of Life. English Wikipedia gives a public-attention measure for the {{n_article}} species with an article. OpenAlex gives the number of papers and other works of 2015 to 2024 whose title or abstract names the species, split by record type. Research means these types: article, review, preprint, letter, book, book chapter, dissertation, conference paper and report, a list fixed before any model was fitted on the split. The Red List gives the category. The joined data keeps only species with every term and a category that ranks risk: {{n_modelled}} birds.

The response is log(1 + research works). The adjusted model is ordinary least squares with Least Concern as the reference category, and its terms are a fixed effect for each family, log body mass, log range size, years since description to {{ref_year}}, rated attractiveness and Red List category. The full model adds log(1 + mean monthly Wikipedia views). Standard errors are clustered by family. Category means are predictions, not coefficients: each species is predicted under each category with its other terms unchanged, and the predictions are averaged and back-transformed. The count model is a Poisson regression on the same terms. Intervals on the means are percentile bootstrap over families ({{n_boot}} resamples, seed {{seed}}). Family fixed effects are a substitute for a phylogeny, which is assumed; the published bird model used a tree.

The Red List column is from {{redlist_source_word}}, version {{red_list_version}}, matched by scientific name to the AVONET names; {{n_category}} of {{n_avonet}} species matched. Species with no English Wikipedia article get zero views, so the obscure species stay in the data. Another {{n_dropped_dd}} species are not in the model because they have no category that the model can use: for most, the name has no match in the Red List, and a few are Data Deficient, Extinct or Extinct in the Wild. The species query uses only the OpenAlex title-and-abstract filter, because its short search parameter changed meaning in February 2026. That parameter now means the full text, which returned {{drift_ratio}} times as many works for {{drift_common}}. A test asserts the query that the fetcher builds, so the broader search cannot slip back in unnoticed.

Limits

Association, not cause, and here that is not a formality. A bird cannot be listed Critically Endangered without evidence: the higher categories need population, trend and range data, which is research, while Least Concern needs much less. The arrow may therefore run from research to listing, and the gradient on this page is what rising evidence requirements would produce on their own. The category that means nobody has enough data points the same way. The {{dd_n}} Data Deficient birds have a median of {{dd_median}} research works, against {{lc_median}} for Least Concern, in a wider frame of {{dd_frame}} birds with a category and a count. Those Data Deficient birds are too few for a coefficient, so the page gives none.

Record types are OpenAlex's, not this page's. OpenAlex counts a report or a thesis as research, and a short note the same as a monograph. The count also misses any paper that names a bird only by its common name, and a few scientific names are ordinary words: Indicator indicator, the greater honeyguide, collects each paper that writes "indicator indicator". A spot check of the record sources behind {{probe_n}} sampled species found that {{probe_gbif_pct}}% of the datasets were GBIF downloads and that {{probe_ref_pct}}% of the reference entries were Red List assessments or Birds of the World accounts.

The model needs a rated attractiveness score, and {{sel_dropped}} of the {{sel_n_a}} otherwise-complete birds do not have one. Does leaving them out bend the result? The same terms, without attractiveness, were fitted on both samples: the Critically Endangered term is {{sel_cr_a}} on the wider sample and {{sel_cr_b}} on the modelled one, both on the log scale of the model, where the headline is {{cr_tot_coef}}. The intervals overlap, so the selection changes the size a little and does not change the direction.

The attention measure is the weakest input. {{n_no_article}} species have no English Wikipedia article and get zero, and {{n_shared_views}} species share {{n_shared_articles}} articles, each with the full traffic of its article. With views held equal, a refit on only the species with an article approximately halves the Critically Endangered term, from {{cr_dir_coef}} to {{sens_article_cr}} on the same scale. How much of the gap survives once attention is held equal therefore depends on how the birds without an article are treated.

The species model stops at birds because birds are the only class with a rated-attractiveness dataset, and the class figure stops at vertebrates because the Red List has assessments for few species outside them. Mammals, with a public h-index dataset of their own, are the obvious next class. That model is not built.

The code

The archive holds the fetch scripts, one for each source and each resumable, along with the build (which joins, fits and bootstraps), the figure script and the page renderer. Each input whose license allows it is in the archive: AVONET and the attractiveness scores (CC BY 4.0), the Wikipedia and OpenAlex counts (CC0) and the Red List summary table (a cited aggregate). The Red List column is not. The IUCN Red List's terms (version 3.1) bar redistribution of Red List data "in their original format" (section 4), but they also place "no restrictions on use of the IUCN Red List Categories associated with each named taxonomic entity" (section 3). The archive takes the cautious reading. fetch_iucn.py gets the column with a free personal token, and manifest.json carries the SHA-256 of the {{cat_rows}} species and categories that this page used, {{cat_sha_short}}, so that build_beauty.py --verify-categories can tell a reader whether their fetch matches. One shipped file, data/lc_matched.csv, lists {{lc_matched_n}} species that are all Least Concern, and so shows that category for them. Whether it continues to ship is an open decision.

Since February 2026, OpenAlex meters its API. The record-type counts cost ${{types_cost}} to fetch for {{types_cost_n}} species. A reader who trusts the shipped counts pays nothing; a reader who fetches them again needs a key and {{types_days}} days of a free account's allowance, or about ${{types_cost_round}} of credit. That is about a tenth of a cent a bird.
