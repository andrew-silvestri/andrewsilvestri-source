The typical critically endangered bird has about {{cr_tot_x1}} times the research of a comparable common one, but most of what a literature search turns up for any bird is not research

Search the open research index OpenAlex for a bird's scientific name and, across the {{n_modelled}} species on this page, only {{share_research_all}}% of what comes back for 2015 to 2024 is research. {{share_dataset_all}}% are datasets, nearly all of them GBIF occurrence downloads, records created automatically each time someone downloads sightings from a biodiversity database, and {{share_reference_all}}% are Red List assessments and Birds of the World encyclopedia entries. Counting research alone, and comparing like with like (birds of the same family, size, range, time since they were named and rated attractiveness), a typical bird of Least Concern, the Red List's lowest-risk category, had {{lc_tot}} research works in those ten years and a Critically Endangered one {{cr_tot}}: about {{cr_tot_x1}} times as many. {{zero_all_pct}}% of the birds had no research work at all. Being endangered goes with being studied, which none of the {{n_read_word}} published studies of research effort read in full for this page found ({{n_null_word}} found no link, {{n_negative_word}} the opposite); which way the arrow runs is not settled here, because a bird cannot be listed as endangered without evidence, and evidence is research.

What a search for a bird finds

Three kinds of record name a bird without being research about it. GBIF, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, gives every download of its occurrence data a citable identifier and lists the species in its description, so each time a researcher, a student or a consultant pulls the sightings for a region, every bird in them collects a record: {{share_dataset_all}}% of everything counted here. The Red List publishes each assessment of a species under its own identifier, and every reassessment gets a new one; the Cornell Lab's Birds of the World does the same for its encyclopedia accounts. Together those are {{share_reference_all}}%. {{share_research_all}}% is journal articles, reviews, preprints, theses, books and reports, and that is the count this page uses; the rest, {{share_other_all}}%, is peer-review reports, errata and other odds and ends.

For the typical bird, threatened ones carry more of every kind. With the same terms held equal, a Critically Endangered bird has {{ds_cr_x}} times the GBIF downloads of a Least Concern one and {{ref_cr_x}} times the Red List and Birds of the World entries: a listing writes some of its own record, as reassessments pile up. But the paperwork is not where the gap comes from. Counting every record, the Critically Endangered bird has {{old_cr_x}} times a Least Concern one; counting only research, {{cr_tot_x}} times. Taking the paperwork out makes the gap larger, because the downloads that make up most of every bird's count rise less steeply with threat ({{ds_cr_x}} times) than research does.

Averages tell a different story, and it is worth knowing why. A Critically Endangered bird averages {{cr_mean_research}} research works and a Least Concern one {{lc_mean_research}}, hardly a difference, because about {{n_famous_lc}} well-known Least Concern birds with a hundred research works or more each, the red junglefowl (the chicken's wild ancestor), the rock dove, the mallard, the house sparrow, have hundreds or thousands each and pull the Least Concern average up. The typical bird, the obscure majority, is where threat and research go together.

Threat and research

Research works per bird species by Red List category: as counted, adjusted, and adjusted with public attention held fixed as well. "Typical" is the back-transformed mean of log(1 + research works), closer to a median than to a mean for counts this skewed. The adjusted series ask what each bird would have if it were in each category in turn, everything else about it unchanged. Whiskers are 95% intervals from resampling whole bird families.

As counted, a Least Concern bird has {{lc_raw}} research works and a Critically Endangered one {{cr_raw}}. Adjusted, the figures are {{lc_tot}} and {{cr_tot}}, with a 95% interval of {{cr_tot_ci}} for the Critically Endangered species, and the adjusted values rise with every step: Near Threatened {{nt_tot}}, Vulnerable {{vu_tot}}, Endangered {{en_tot}}. (As counted, Near Threatened sits just below Least Concern.) The adjustment widens the gap rather than closing it, because threatened birds tend to be narrowly ranged and recently described, the kinds of bird that are hard to study, and holding that equal removes a handicap.

The size of the multiple depends on what is being compared, and the page gives each. On the typical bird it is {{cr_tot_x}} times (95% interval {{cr_x_lo}} to {{cr_x_hi}}, resampling whole families), and on journal articles and reviews alone about the same, {{journal_cr_x}} times. On the average count the gap is smaller and much less certain. A count model, which compares averages and so is dominated by the few hundred birds with large literatures, gives {{ppml_x}} times, with an interval from {{ppml_lo}} to {{ppml_hi}}: only just distinguishable from no difference, and in that model Vulnerable birds sit below Near Threatened ones. So the finding is about the typical bird, the obscure majority: a critically endangered one of those has several times the research of a comparable Least Concern one, while the famous few dominate the averages whatever their status.

Some of the gap travels with public attention. Holding English Wikipedia views equal as well takes the multiple from {{cr_tot_x}} to {{cr_dir_x}}. Which causes which is not knowable from one snapshot; a bird that is studied more may get a better article and more readers, as easily as the reverse.

What else goes with being studied

What the full model's fit depends on: the fall in R² when each term is removed and the model refitted, with 95% bootstrap intervals over families. The full model adds Wikipedia views to the adjusted model above; R² rises from {{r2_noviews}} to {{r2_full}}.

How often people look a bird up on Wikipedia carries the most of the fit ({{views_drop}}), and which family it belongs to nearly as much ({{family_drop}}): some families are studied as model organisms, game or pests. Red List category is {{cat_rank_word}} of seven ({{cat_drop}}), behind years since description and range size. Rated attractiveness, scored from photographs by several thousand people, adds {{attr_drop}}: beauty, the quality this page set out to test, explains almost nothing once the rest is known, with a coefficient indistinguishable from zero (p {{attr_p}}).

Why the published record disagrees

{{n_models_word}} species-level models of research effort across mammals, birds, reptiles, turtles and plants have been published since 2014, {{n_threat_word}} of them with a threat-status term in a model. Of the {{n_read}} whose full text could be read for this page, {{n_null}} find threat status null, {{n_negative}} find it negative (one of them, the one other study of all birds, from 2014, a comparison with nothing else held equal) and {{n_assessed_word}} finds that being assessed at all, rather than being threatened, goes with more study. Those studies counted papers in Web of Science, Zoological Record or Scopus, mostly on a single class. This page uses a different index, a later window and one class, and any of the three could produce a different sign. A single result against a literature is more likely to be wrong than the literature, so what this page reports is what one instrument measured on one class over one decade.

Method

Birds are the one class where every term has a free, species-level source. AVONET gives body mass, range size and family for {{n_avonet}} species. The iratebirds project gives a rated attractiveness score for {{n_attract}} of them. The GBIF backbone gives the year each species was described ({{n_year_col}} from the Catalogue of Life where GBIF had none). English Wikipedia gives a public-attention measure for the {{n_article}} species with an article. OpenAlex gives the number of papers and other works of 2015 to 2024 whose title or abstract names the species, split by record type; research means the types article, review, preprint, letter, book, book chapter, dissertation, conference paper and report, a list fixed before any model was fitted on the split. The Red List gives the category. Joined, keeping the species with every term and a category that ranks risk, that is {{n_modelled}} birds.

The response is log(1 + research works). The adjusted model is ordinary least squares with a fixed effect for every family, log body mass, log range size, years since description to {{ref_year}}, rated attractiveness, and the Red List category with Least Concern as the reference; the full model adds log(1 + mean monthly Wikipedia views). Standard errors are clustered by family. Category means are predictions, not coefficients: every species is predicted under each category with its other terms as they are, and the predictions are averaged and back-transformed. The count model is a Poisson regression on the same terms. Intervals on the means are percentile bootstrap over families, {{n_boot}} resamples, seed {{seed}}. Family fixed effects stand in for a phylogeny, which is assumed; the published bird model used a tree.

The Red List column came from {{redlist_source_word}}, version {{red_list_version}}, matched by scientific name to the names AVONET uses; {{n_category}} of {{n_avonet}} matched. Species with no English Wikipedia article are given zero views, which keeps the obscure species in. {{n_dropped_dd}} species otherwise in the data are left out for want of a category the model can use: most because their name finds no match in the Red List, and a few because they are Data Deficient, Extinct or Extinct in the Wild. The species query is pinned to OpenAlex's title-and-abstract filter, because its short search parameter changed meaning in February 2026: it now means the full text, which returned {{drift_ratio}} times as many works for {{drift_common}}; a test asserts the query the fetcher builds.

Limits

Association, not cause, and here that is not a formality. A bird cannot be listed Critically Endangered without evidence: the higher categories need population, trend and range data, which is research, and Least Concern needs much less. So the arrow may run from research to listing, and the gradient on this page is what rising evidence requirements would produce on their own. The category that means nobody has enough data points the same way: the {{dd_n}} Data Deficient birds have a median of {{dd_median}} research works, against {{lc_median}} for Least Concern, in a wider frame of {{dd_frame}} birds with a category and a count. That is too few species for a coefficient and is given none.

Record types are OpenAlex's, not this page's. A report or a thesis is counted as research and a short note the same as a monograph; a paper that names a bird only by its common name is missed, and a few scientific names are also ordinary words (Indicator indicator, the greater honeyguide, collects every paper that writes "indicator indicator"). A spot check of the record sources behind {{probe_n}} sampled species found {{probe_gbif_pct}}% of the datasets were GBIF downloads and {{probe_ref_pct}}% of the reference entries Red List assessments or Birds of the World accounts.

The model needs a rated attractiveness score, which {{sel_dropped}} of the {{sel_n_a}} otherwise-complete birds do not have. Fitting the same terms on both samples, without attractiveness, gives a Critically Endangered term of {{sel_cr_a}} on the wider sample against {{sel_cr_b}} on the modelled one (on the model's log scale, where the headline is {{cr_tot_coef}}), with overlapping intervals: the selection moves the size a little and not the direction. The attention measure is the weakest input: {{n_no_article}} species have no English Wikipedia article and are given zero, and {{n_shared_views}} species share {{n_shared_articles}} articles, each credited with the whole of an article's traffic. Refitting only on species with an article roughly halves the Critically Endangered term with views held equal, from {{cr_dir_coef}} to {{sens_article_cr}} on the same scale: how much of the gap survives holding attention equal depends on how the birds without an article are treated.

The species model stops at birds because birds are the one class with a rated-attractiveness dataset, and the class figure stops at vertebrates because the Red List has assessed few species outside them. Mammals, with a public h-index dataset of their own, are the obvious next class and are not built.

The code

The fetch scripts, one for each source and each resumable; the build, which joins, fits and bootstraps; the figure script; and the page renderer. Every input whose license allows it travels: AVONET and the attractiveness scores under CC BY 4.0, the Wikipedia and OpenAlex counts under CC0, the Red List summary table as a cited aggregate. The Red List column does not: the IUCN Red List's terms (version 3.1) bar redistributing Red List data "in their original format" (section 4), while placing "no restrictions on use of the IUCN Red List Categories associated with each named taxonomic entity" (section 3), and the archive takes the cautious reading. fetch_iucn.py gets the column with a free personal token, and manifest.json carries the SHA-256 of the {{cat_rows}} species and categories this page was built from, {{cat_sha_short}}, so build_beauty.py --verify-categories can tell a reader whether their fetch matches. One shipped file, data/lc_matched.csv, lists {{lc_matched_n}} species that are all Least Concern, and so does reveal that category for them; whether it keeps shipping is an open decision.

OpenAlex has metered its API since February 2026. The record-type counts cost ${{types_cost}} to fetch for {{types_cost_n}} species; a reader who trusts the shipped counts pays nothing, and one who re-fetches them needs a key and {{types_days}} days of a free account's allowance, or about ${{types_cost_round}} of credit.
