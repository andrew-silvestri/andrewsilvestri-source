Beef from cleared Brazilian pasture puts as much greenhouse gas into the air as a petrol car driven that far. Most of it is the animal: methane from the cow's gut ({{enteric_pct}}%) and the forest cleared for its pasture ({{land_pct}}%). Shipping it to Texas adds {{beef_freight_pct}}%. Under a stricter accounting rule, which charges some of the animal's emissions to its leather and tallow, the beef would still be about {{iso_km}} km. The other high-footprint foods here follow the same pattern ({{hi_foods}}). At least {{farm_min}}% of each one's emissions happen before it leaves the country or state it was grown in. Shipping by sea or rail is at most {{ship_max_pct}}% of any food's footprint on its usual route. Flying is what changes the picture: a banana flown instead of shipped by sea comes out {{banana_air_x}} times the shipped one.

The calculator

The same model covers {{n_items}} items in three groups: food, transport and clothing. It covers {{n_regions}} producing regions and four freight modes: sea, road, rail and air. Select an item, where it was made and where it ends up, and the calculator traces the chain stage by stage. It calculates each answer again and does not look up a stored answer, so any combination works, including the absurd ones.

What the model finds

The table gives each item on one typical route, in kilograms of CO2e. This unit counts every gas as the amount of carbon dioxide that would warm the planet as much over a century. For food, the route is from where it was grown to where it is eaten. For a vehicle or a garment, the route is from where it was made to where it is used.

The growing, not the journey

The two tomatoes make the point most clearly. The model runs {{n_routes}} sea routes between two different regions. On each, heat for a Dutch-style glasshouse adds between {{heat_lo}} and {{heat_hi}} kg to a kilogram of tomatoes. The journey is the same for both. On one shared route, the Netherlands to New York, the heated tomato is {{nl_x}} times the field one. Food miles are what people reach for, and by sea they are usually the wrong lever. A banana crosses {{banana_km}} km by sea and still comes in at {{banana_sea}} kg. This is because sea freight costs almost nothing per tonne-kilometer (one tonne carried one kilometer). A field tomato flown instead of shipped comes out {{field_air_x}} times higher. Distance matters a great deal by air, and over long hauls by truck, and much less by sea or rail.

The field tomato is the exception to the pattern above. Its footprint is small, and most of it comes after the farm. Growing it is {{fcult_pct}}% of the total; the packing, the shipping, the shop and the tomatoes thrown away are most of the rest. Beef is the opposite. The methane and the land clearing happen before the animal leaves the ranch, so the route is small beside them. Air freight, the one shipping choice that shows, adds about {{beef_air_pct}}%.

How the model works

The model takes three inputs: an item, where it was produced, and where it ends up. It gives the chain in sequence: a line of stages from the field to the bin. Under each stage, a tree of branches shows where the emissions of that stage came from. The branches have branches. A tomato needs fertiliser, the fertiliser needs ammonia, the ammonia needs natural gas, and the extraction of that gas leaks methane.

The figure shows that chain, traced by the same engine that the calculator runs, with the display cutoff of the calculator set to off. At its default, the calculator drops the gas step as too small to list. Each box is what the tomato is charged for that process. The total decreases by two orders of magnitude in three steps.

Allocation, where the argument is

Every stage carries a multiplier that gives the share of the stage that belongs to the product. A slaughterhouse also makes leather and tallow, a dairy cow also becomes beef, and a coffee mill also sells pulp. These other outputs are co-products, and the split of a stage's burden between them is called allocation. To charge the whole burden to the one output of interest is a choice, and in many published footprints the choice is invisible. Here it sits on every stage, with the basis it rests on.

The cheese is that argument in its purest form. The share of a dairy cow's methane that belongs to her milk rather than her meat is a judgement. For cheese the judgement is most of the answer, because the herd stages are {{herd_pct}}% of the kilogram. The figure below shows every published method, from Flysjö and colleagues' comparison of the methods. With system expansion, the same kilogram of French cheese is {{ch_se}} to {{ch_se_hi}} kg. System expansion credits the cow's meat by counting the beef it replaces elsewhere. It is {{ch_flysjo}} under the physical split that Flysjö's team calculated for Swedish milk. It is {{ch_idf}} under the International Dairy Federation's 2015 formula for a typical herd ({{milk_share}}% to milk, the model's default). It is {{ch_econ}} by economic value, {{ch_prot}} by protein and {{ch_mass}} by mass. The highest over the lowest, with the bottom of the system-expansion range, is ×{{ch_spread}}. If the milk carried the whole cow, it would be {{ch_none}}. But that is the case with no allocation at all, not a basis. So the figure shows it for scale, and the spread does not include it.

The leather shoe is the same argument where it barely applies. A hide is a co-product of a beef animal. The model gives it {{hide_share}}% of the animal. That share is an assumption, below the 2.7% economic mean Lunesu and colleagues measured, and it comes to {{hide_pct}}% of the pair. A change to the highest physical share they found takes the pair from {{shoe_lo}} to {{shoe_hi}} kg, ×{{shoe_spread}}. Two caveats apply even to that small share. First, the figure for the animal behind the share has no source. Second, the beef also carries that animal in full, so the two products together are charged for a little more than one whole animal. The cost of the shoe is the tanning, which serves only the leather and is charged in full. Allocation decides the answer where the shared stage is the answer, and nowhere else.

Lifetime, allocation by another name

A car’s factory does not belong to any one journey, and neither does an airframe. A per-kilometer figure charges you one part in a lifetime — the vehicle divided by the distance it will travel before it is scrapped. That lifetime is an assumption, not a measurement. The model assumes {{car_life}} km for a car, and the calculator lets you change it. Retire the petrol car at half that distance and it comes to {{petrol_half}} kg per 100 km instead of {{petrol_now}}. Keep it twice as long and it is {{petrol_double}}. The electric car carries more of its burden in the factory and less in fuel, so it moves further: {{ev_half}} at half the life, {{ev_now}} at the default, {{ev_double}} at double. For a flight, the same control is the airframe’s life in passenger-kilometers. So the model charges you your share of the construction of the aircraft, divided in the same way as your share of the fuel.

How it is checked

Every total must fall inside a band drawn from published figures, and the run fails if one falls outside. For food, most bands come from Poore and Nemecek's survey of the published literature. A band is a range, not a single figure, because these are distributions across thousands of farms. The field tomato's band runs a little below their range, and the model's {{field_total}} kg sits in that lower part. Most vehicle and clothing bands carry the figure they were drawn from. These are the UK government's conversion factors, Levi Strauss's assessment of its own jeans, and an MIT study of a running shoe. The bands are wide. A result inside one is a weaker test than a match with the study, which the jeans and the trainers do not achieve. The test also checks that the parts of every stage sum to its total, and that the cheese's milk share reaches the processes beneath it. It also checks that a change of route or grid moves the answer the way physics says it should. Last, it checks that the corrected inputs below are the values their sources give.

One test exists because of a bug that hid well. The model charged production-side electricity at the grid of the consuming region, so a French dairy looked dirty when someone in India ate the cheese. The bug is invisible in any single run. It shows only when you change the destination and something moves that has no business moving. A test now walks a fixed list of food production stages and fails if any of them draws power from the wrong grid. The clothing and vehicle stages, and coffee roasting, are not on that list.

Corrections, 24 October 2026

A review of this site's pages in October 2026 found four inputs that did not agree with their own sources or notes. The corrections moved the totals below. The leather shoe moved most. Its tanning sat inside the hide's stage, so the hide's {{hide_share}}% share of the animal applied to the tanning as well. Charged in full, the pair is {{shoe_now}} kg, not {{shoe_before}}. Second, in October 2025 the IPCC corrected a calculation error in the table of warming potentials that this page cites. Methane from cattle's guts, rice paddies, coffee lagoons and landfill now counts {{gwp_bio}} times carbon dioxide (it was 27). Nitrous oxide counts {{gwp_n2o}} (273), and the methane that leaks from gas wells counts {{gwp_fossil}} (the model had used an older report's 28). Third, the soil factor now counts the nitrous oxide that leaves later from nitrogen that washes or blows off the field. The IPCC chapter it cites does the same. The factor is {{soil_new}} kg CO2e per kilogram of nitrogen, against the {{soil_old}} the model used before for the direct part alone. Fourth, the cheese's milk share is now the International Dairy Federation's formula that the page cites ({{milk_share}}%). The model had used a Swedish figure from another study (85%).

Limits

The largest open question is where the share of a co-product applies. The model charges a share only at the stage where the products separate. The slaughterhouse's {{slaughter_share}}% to the meat applies to the slaughterhouse, and the beef carries the animal behind it in full. The allocation standard this page cites splits all the inputs of a process that makes more than one product. The animal is the main input. With that method, the kilogram of beef would be {{upstream_beef}} kg, {{upstream_pct}}% lower. The other foods with a co-product would also decrease: {{up_list}}. Beef would still be at the top of the table.

It is possible that the model counts food losses twice. The model increases every stage before the bin to include the food that is lost along the way. But the comment beside the stage amounts says that the amounts already include losses. Without the derivation of the amounts, the model cannot say which is correct. If the losses are already included, the food totals would decrease by {{loss_lo}}% to {{loss_hi}}%, most for the field tomato and the banana.

Several inputs are representative values, not measurements of any farm or factory. The beef's land-use figure is a Brazilian pasture-expansion figure, and it does not change when the calculator is set to another origin. Some terms mix methane and nitrous oxide without a record of the split: manure, the chicken sheds and the hide's animal. These terms stay at the old warming table, which overstates them by up to about {{mixed_over}}%. The soil factor applies IPCC's wet-climate leaching to every field. The disposal of every garment is priced as food waste in landfill, at most {{disp_max}}% of the total of any garment. The grid figures have the label operating margin (the power plants that respond to extra demand) but look like annual averages. Which is correct matters most for the electric car and the train, and the model cannot say by how much. Freight factors do not include the production and delivery of the fuel, which would increase them. Almonds carry a contested credit for carbon held in orchard trees.

This is an explainer, not a footprint for any real product or a basis for any purchasing or reporting decision.
