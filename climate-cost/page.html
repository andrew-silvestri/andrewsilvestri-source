The calculator

The same model for {{n_items}} items in three groups, food, transport and clothing, across {{n_regions}} producing regions and four freight modes: sea, road, rail and air. Pick an item, where it was made and where it ends up, and it traces the chain stage by stage. It recomputes rather than looking up a stored answer, so any combination works, including the absurd ones.

What the model says

Each item at one typical route, in kilograms of CO2e: every gas counted as the amount of carbon dioxide that would warm the planet as much over a century. For food the route is where it was grown and where it is eaten; for a vehicle or a garment, where it was made and where it is used.

The growing, not the journey

The two tomatoes make the point most cleanly. On each of the {{n_routes}} sea routes between two different regions, heating a Dutch-style glasshouse adds between {{heat_lo}} and {{heat_hi}} kg to a kilogram of tomatoes, and the journey is the same for both. On one shared route, the Netherlands to New York, the heated tomato is {{nl_x}} times the field one. Food miles are what people reach for, and by sea they are usually the wrong lever: a banana crosses {{banana_km}} km by sea and still comes in at {{banana_sea}} kg, because sea freight costs almost nothing per tonne-kilometer (one tonne carried one kilometer). A field tomato flown instead of shipped comes out {{field_air_x}} times higher. Distance matters a great deal by air, and over long hauls by truck, and much less by sea or rail.

The field tomato is also the exception to the pattern above. Its footprint is small, and most of it comes after the farm: growing it is {{fcult_pct}}% of the total, and the packing, the shipping, the shop and the tomatoes thrown away make up most of the rest. Beef is the opposite. The methane and the land clearing happen before the animal leaves the ranch, so the route is small beside them; air freight, the one shipping choice that shows, adds about {{beef_air_pct}}%.

How the model works

Give it three things: an item, where it was produced, and where it ends up. It returns the chain as it happens: a line of stages from the field to the bin, with a branching tree under each stage showing where that stage's emissions came from. The branches have branches. A tomato needs fertiliser, the fertiliser needs ammonia, the ammonia needs natural gas, and getting that gas out of the ground leaks methane.

That chain, traced by the same engine the calculator runs, with the calculator's display cutoff turned off: at its default the calculator drops the gas step as too small to list. Each box is what the tomato is charged for that process, and the total tapers by two orders of magnitude in three steps.

Allocation, which is where the disagreement lives

Every stage carries a multiplier saying what share of it belongs to the product. A slaughterhouse also makes leather and tallow. A dairy cow also becomes beef. A coffee mill also sells pulp. Those other outputs are co-products, and how a stage's burden is split between them is called allocation. Charging the whole burden to the one output you care about is a choice, and in many published footprints it is an invisible one. Here it sits on every stage, with the basis it rests on.

The cheese is that argument in its purest form. How much of a dairy cow's methane belongs to her milk rather than her meat is a judgement, and for cheese the judgement is most of the answer, because the herd stages are {{herd_pct}}% of the kilogram. Every published way of making it is in the figure below, from Flysjö and colleagues' comparison of the methods. The same kilogram of French cheese is {{ch_se}} to {{ch_se_hi}} kg if the cow's meat is credited by system expansion (counting the beef it replaces elsewhere), {{ch_flysjo}} under the physical split Flysjö's team computed for Swedish milk, {{ch_idf}} under the International Dairy Federation's 2015 formula for a typical herd ({{milk_share}}% to milk, the model's default), {{ch_econ}} by economic value, {{ch_prot}} by protein and {{ch_mass}} by mass. Highest over lowest, using the bottom of the system-expansion range, that is ×{{ch_spread}}. If the milk carried the whole cow it would be {{ch_none}}, but that is the case with no allocation at all, not a basis, so it is drawn for scale and left out of the spread.

The leather shoe is the same argument where it barely applies. A hide is a co-product of a beef animal. The model gives it {{hide_share}}% of the animal, an assumption below the 2.7% economic mean Lunesu and colleagues measured, and that share comes to {{hide_pct}}% of the pair. Moving it to the highest physical share they found takes the pair from {{shoe_lo}} to {{shoe_hi}} kg, ×{{shoe_spread}}. Two caveats sit under even that small share: the figure for the animal it is a share of has no source, and the beef also carries that animal in full, so across the two products a little over one whole animal is charged. What the shoe costs is the tanning, which serves only the leather and is charged in full. Allocation decides the answer where the shared stage is the answer, and nowhere else.

Lifetime, which is allocation wearing a different hat

A car’s factory does not belong to any one journey. Neither does an airframe. What a per-kilometer figure charges you is one part in a lifetime — the vehicle divided by the distance it will cover before it is scrapped — and that lifetime is an assumption rather than a measurement. The model assumes {{car_life}} km for a car, and the calculator lets you move it. Retire the petrol car at half that and it comes to {{petrol_half}} kg per 100 km instead of {{petrol_now}}; keep it twice as long and it is {{petrol_double}}. The electric car, which carries more of its burden in the factory and less in fuel, moves further: {{ev_half}} at half the life, {{ev_now}} at the default, {{ev_double}} at double. For a flight the same control is the airframe’s life in passenger-kilometers, so what the model charges you is your share of building the aircraft, divided the same way your share of the fuel is.

How it is checked

Every total is held to a band drawn from published figures, and the run fails if one falls outside. For food the bands come mostly from Poore and Nemecek's survey of the published literature, a range rather than a single figure, because these are distributions across thousands of farms; the field tomato's band runs a little below their range, and the model's {{field_total}} kg sits in that lower part. Most vehicle and clothing bands carry the figure they were drawn from: the UK government's conversion factors, Levi Strauss's assessment of its own jeans, and an MIT study of a running shoe. The bands are wide, and landing inside one is a weaker test than matching the study, which the jeans and the trainers do not. The test also checks that every stage's parts add up to its total, that the cheese's milk share reaches the processes beneath it, that changing a route or a grid moves the answer the way physics says it should, and that the corrected inputs below are the values their sources give.

One test exists because of a bug that hid well. Production-side electricity was being charged at the consuming region's grid, so a French dairy looked dirty when someone in India ate the cheese. It is invisible in any single run, and only shows up when you change the destination and something moves that has no business moving. A test now walks a fixed list of food production stages and fails if any of them draws power from the wrong grid; the clothing and vehicle stages, and coffee roasting, are not on that list.

Corrections, 24 October 2026

A review of this site's pages in October 2026 found four inputs that did not match their own sources or notes, and correcting them moved the totals below. The leather shoe moved most: its tanning sat inside the hide's stage, so the hide's {{hide_share}}% share of the animal was applied to the tanning as well. Charged in full, the pair is {{shoe_now}} kg, not {{shoe_before}}. Second, in October 2025 the IPCC corrected a calculation error in the table of warming potentials this page cites: methane from cattle's guts, rice paddies, coffee lagoons and landfill now counts {{gwp_bio}} times carbon dioxide (it was 27), nitrous oxide {{gwp_n2o}} (273), and the methane leaked from gas wells {{gwp_fossil}} (the model had used an older report's 28). Third, the soil factor now counts the nitrous oxide that leaves later from nitrogen that washes or blows off the field, as the IPCC chapter it cites does: {{soil_new}} kg CO2e per kilogram of nitrogen, against the {{soil_old}} the model used before for the direct part alone. Fourth, the cheese's milk share is now the International Dairy Federation's formula the page cites ({{milk_share}}%), where the model had used a Swedish figure from another study (85%).

Limits

The largest open question is where a co-product's share applies. The model charges a share only at the stage where the products separate: the slaughterhouse's {{slaughter_share}}% to the meat touches the slaughterhouse, and the animal behind it is charged in full to the beef. The allocation standard this page cites splits all the inputs of a process that makes more than one product, and the animal is the main input. Applied that way the kilogram of beef would be {{upstream_beef}} kg, {{upstream_pct}}% lower, and the other foods with a co-product would fall too: {{up_list}}. Beef would still top the table.

Food losses may be counted twice. The model scales every stage before the bin up for the food that is lost along the way, while the comment beside the stage amounts says losses are already in them; without the amounts' derivation the model cannot say which is right. If they are already in, the food totals would fall by {{loss_lo}}% to {{loss_hi}}%, most for the field tomato and the banana.

Several inputs are representative values rather than measurements of any farm or factory. The beef's land-use figure is a Brazilian pasture-expansion figure and does not change when the calculator is set to another origin. Terms that mix methane and nitrous oxide without recording the split (manure, the chicken sheds, the hide's animal) were left at the old warming table, which overstates them by up to about {{mixed_over}}%. The soil factor applies IPCC's wet-climate leaching to every field. Every garment's disposal is priced as food waste in landfill, at most {{disp_max}}% of any garment's total. The grid figures are labelled operating margin (the power plants that respond to extra demand) but look like annual averages; which is right matters most for the electric car and the train, and the model cannot say by how much. Freight factors leave out producing and delivering the fuel, which would raise them. Almonds carry a contested credit for carbon held in orchard trees.

This is an explainer, not a footprint for any real product or a basis for any purchasing or reporting decision.
