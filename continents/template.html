Precisely measured continents still defy a forecast

Imagine an instrument fixed to the bedrock under Denver that could be read at any moment, past or future. Read today, it says the ground moves {{denver_speed}} millimetres a year, and plate interiors hold their shape to {{band_far}} of a millimetre a year. Turn the dial far enough either way, though, and nobody can say where that ground will be. The two facts do not conflict, and the gap between them is the story.

Start with today's reading. A tectonic plate is a rigid body, and rigid to an almost absurd degree: fit a single rotation to every GNSS station more than {{far_km}} kilometers from a plate boundary, and the stations more than {{interior_km}} kilometers from a boundary sit on that rotation to a median of {{band_far}} millimetres a year, across thousands of kilometers of continent. Within {{near_km}} kilometers of a boundary, the same fit misses the median station by {{band_near}}. That value is a median over receivers, and the receivers near boundaries crowd into a few dense networks (California's above all), so it tells as much about where geodesy happens as about the boundaries. Almost the whole surface of the Earth is a few rafts that do not deform. Everything that happens to them happens in belts a few hundred kilometers wide.

So why can nobody say where that ground will be? Because the precision belongs to a rate, and a forecast needs a position, which means assuming that the rate holds. Run forward, the fit gives arithmetic, not a forecast, because the rotations do not hold: the page shows {{n_slower}} measured cases where a boundary slowed by more than five millimetres a year within the last few million years. Rigidity is the best-known result here, and it is worth less than it appears.

Turn the dial backward and the instrument gives way to models. Eight published reconstructions of the same past agree to within {{past_near_lo_km}} to {{past_near_hi_km}} kilometers at {{past_near_age}} million years, depending on the place, and the {{n_models_500}} models that reach {{past_far_age}} million years disagree by up to {{past_far_km}} kilometers. Most of that is in longitude, which palaeomagnetism cannot fix on its own (in latitude alone, the widest spread at that age is {{past_far_lat_km}} kilometers). Turn it far enough forward and four published futures agree with each other for {{agree_until}} million years. After that, the two furthest apart disagree about the land-or-ocean status of {{future_max}} per cent of the Earth's surface.

The projection is Equal Earth, which is equal-area, so an area on one panel means the same as the same area on any other. All four geometries come from a single 2018 paper by one group of three authors, who built them in a deliberately standardised way, each standing for one mode of the supercontinent cycle; that paper says the modes "should be treated as end-members of a spectrum of possibilities". Novopangaea has no peer-reviewed primary source at all: it was devised for a television series and reached print in a trade book. The grids are a digitisation of maps that were drawn, not the output of a plate model, and the read-me of the deposit says only that they "were made in MATLAB and based off the maps presented in" the 2018 paper. What to notice is the size of the disagreement. At {{common_age}} million years, the two of these four furthest apart ({{future_max_pair}}) disagree about the land-or-ocean status of {{future_max}} per cent of the Earth's surface, and the average pair about {{future_mean}} per cent. Each scenario puts land on {{land_lo}} to {{land_hi}} per cent of the surface, and two unrelated maps with that much land would disagree on about {{chance_lo}} to {{chance_hi}} per cent. The average pair, in other words, disagrees about as much as two maps drawn independently would. How rigid is a plate?

The reading comes from GNSS receivers, and {{n_all}} stations carry a MIDAS velocity. The analysis keeps the stations with at least {{min_years}} years of record and an uncertainty of at most {{max_sigma}} millimetres a year on both horizontal components. That leaves {{n_after_cuts}} stations, and {{n_with_plate}} of them carry a plate assignment. Nine plates have enough stations far enough from a boundary for a fit.

The shading shows distance to the nearest plate boundary on a uniform lattice, and the boundaries of Bird's PB2002 model are drawn over it. Nine plates carry a label with the speed that their fitted rotation gives at that point. The shading is a computed field, not a map of the stations, and the difference matters: North America has {{n_na}} stations more than {{far_km}} kilometers from a boundary, against Africa's {{n_af}}. A map of receivers would tell more about where geodesy happens than about the Earth. The speeds come from a fitted model; the next figure shows how well it fits.

The miss of each station against the rotation of its own plate, plotted against its distance from a plate boundary. The fit uses interior stations only and is then applied everywhere, so the near-boundary points are a test of a prediction, not a report of a fit. Beyond {{far_km}} kilometers, the cloud collapses onto a floor. The group at the top right is Hawaii ({{big_island_plotted}} stations on the Big Island, {{big_island_n}} of them excluded from the Pacific fit), which sits further from a plate boundary than almost anything else on Earth. The worst of those stations missed the first, uncleaned Pacific fit by {{hawaii_max}} millimetres a year. They miss because the ground on the island's volcanoes, Kīlauea and Mauna Loa among them, moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the Pacific plate.

The Pacific shows why the fit needs a guard. Fitted on all {{pa_before_n}} of its far-field stations, it misses by an RMS of {{pa_before_rms}} millimetres a year (an RMS, which a few bad stations dominate; the misses elsewhere on this page are medians), and its rotation sits {{pa_before_itrf}} away from the rotation of the international model. Nearly all of that comes from three groups of stations: {{big_island_n}} stations on the Big Island of Hawaii; four stations in western Alaska, which the source file lists under the Pacific plate but which sit on the Bering block; and one station on Salas y Gómez, filed under the Pacific but actually on Nazca, {{ilsg_resid}} millimetres a year out.

A stated rule removes {{pa_dropped}} of the {{pa_before_n}} stations: fit, drop anything past {{mad_k}} median absolute deviations, fit again, repeat. After that, the RMS of the plate falls to {{pa_after_rms}}. The page gives the rule rather than a list of station names, because a list of names is a hardcoded answer to the contamination of today.

Plates change speed

Plate motion is not a constant, and that is a measurement, not an argument. NNR-MORVEL56 averages sea-floor spreading over the last {{morvel_interval}} million years, while ITRF2020-PMM averages space geodesy over decades. Where the two disagree, either a boundary changed speed or one of the models is wrong.

Motion of the first plate relative to the second, at a point on the named boundary. Only the relative motion between pairs is compared, because the two models sit in different reference frames and only relative motion is frame-independent. Of these boundaries, {{n_slower}} slowed by more than five millimetres a year: two continental collisions, at the Himalaya and the Zagros, and the subduction of Nazca below South America at the Peru-Chile trench. The other boundaries are two mid-ocean ridges, a rift and a continental transform (the San Andreas), and one of the ridges, the {{sped_up}}, is now {{sped_up_by}} millimetres a year faster. MORVEL's averaging interval is not one number. It is {{morvel_interval_detail}}. MORVEL's website says that its authors "retain all commercial rights to the graphics and results generated from this Web site"; the table used here is from Argus and colleagues 2011, which states "This paper is not subject to U.S. copyright."

India against Eurasia is the case to remember. Its geological average is {{ie_morvel}} millimetres a year and its geodetic rate is {{ie_itrf}}, so it moves {{ie_gap}} slower now than over the last few million years. Arabia against Eurasia slowed by {{ar_gap}} and Nazca against South America by {{nz_gap}}, while the mid-Atlantic and the East African rift agree to about two. That is the argument against extrapolation, and it is stronger than any reminder that a hundred million years is a long time. The poles measured today were measurably different three million years ago.

How much do reconstructions of the past disagree?

Further back, the question is no longer how precisely anything is measured but how much published models of the same past disagree. This page asked {{n_models}} global plate models where {{n_places}} present-day places were, every {{age_step}} million years back to {{past_oldest}}. Each request names its model, because the service documents its default model as liable to change, which is candid of the service and no help to a page that wants the same answer twice. The rotation files of named models, too, were updated in place as recently as last year.

Reconstructed latitude, not position, because latitude is the honest quantity. Palaeomagnetism determines how far from the equator a rock formed, and it cannot determine its longitude at all unless further assumptions are made. A fan of latitudes is therefore the measurement; a map of positions is the measurement plus the assumption of each model about east and west. Lines end where the coverage of a model ends, and lines that break in the middle are models that place no crust at that place and age. The models share ancestry, so the spread between them is not a spread of independent answers. This page infers, and the source does not say, that the true spread of admissible reconstructions is therefore wider, not narrower.

Three regimes, one pair of axes

Three different quantities on one pair of axes, and that is the point. The solid line is measured: the largest distance between any two reconstructions of the same place and age, among the models that reach that age. The number of those models is {{model_count_steps}}, so the shape of the line mixes age with the number of models in the comparison. The dashed ray is the miss measured today, run forward at {{band_far}} millimetres a year, which is {{band_far}} kilometers per million years; it is the extrapolation that this page argues against, drawn for comparison. The dotted line is a proxy: the separation between the land centroids of the four futures. A centroid averages a whole configuration, so two arrangements that barely overlap can have close centroids, and two that overlap can have distant ones. The comparison with the measured line above it is assumed, and the direction of its bias is not known. The annotation on the dotted line gives the number that the future can be held to in its own units. The ceiling is the antipodal distance, {{antipodal}} kilometers.

Two traps in the inputs

Two of the inputs contain a trap — one that produces not an error but a confident, plausible and wrong number. Each trap has a check that runs before the number it guards exists, and a failure means that there is no page, not a warning in a log.

The four future scenarios do not use a common longitude convention: two declare −180 to 180, and two declare 0 to 360. Read as they come, two of the four sit half a world out, and the four present-day grids (four copies of the same map of today) appear to disagree about {{naive_pct}} per cent of the planet, not {{t0_pct}}. That {{naive_pct}} would have been the headline number of this page. It would have come from real files, its size would have turned on a reading choice that no headline mentions, and nothing anywhere downstream would show the error.

The obvious guard is that the four grids must agree at the present day. That guard is not sufficient, and this distinction is the reason for this section. The same code reads all four grids, so a mistake common to all four leaves them in perfect agreement while every map here is wrong. The checks that can find such a mistake are the ones whose answer comes from outside the data: {{n_probes}} named points whose land-or-sea answer comes from an ordinary world map, and the Earth's own land fraction.

Two of those checks are negative controls, and they are there because a check that nobody has seen fail is not known to work. Suppose a future release of the scenario grids quietly harmonises the longitude conventions, or a future release of the velocity file reassigns the Hawaiian stations. The guarded numbers would still be produced, and the account on this page of why they are guarded would become false. So the build also requires that the failures are still reproducible, and it stops if they are not.

Method

Velocities are MIDAS estimates of GNSS station motion, in IGS20, which is the IGS realisation of ITRF2020; this frame makes the comparison with ITRF2020-PMM a comparison of models, not of reference frames, and the build asserts it. The rotation of each plate is fitted by weighted least squares on v = ω × r, the form in equation 1 of Altamimi and colleagues 2017. Neither MORVEL nor the NNR-MORVEL56 paper states it.

A fitted rotation can match a published one at the plate's own stations and still not be pinned; this was measured, not assumed. How can both be true? The stations of a plate cover a patch, and in rotation space one direction moves the rotation far but barely changes any velocity in that patch. For South America, the rotation can move {{break_deg}} degrees per million years ({{break_pct}} per cent of the plate's own rate) while the difference at its own stations stays inside the {{b1c_tol}} millimetre a year check. So the build compares the rotation vectors directly, in degrees per million years, and that check pins the plate. The station-level check stays beside it, because it is the right check for a contaminated station — a different failure.

The NNR-MORVEL56 table comes from Table 1 of the paper itself, and the build cross-checks it against an independent transcription of the same table, because the authors' own copy is a JPEG on a host that no longer answers. The argument against extrapolation on this page rests on those numbers.

Distance to a plate boundary is the distance to the nearest PB2002 vertex, not to the nearest point on the segment, which slightly overestimates distance where a segment is sparsely sampled. The cuts, the {{far_km}} kilometer threshold, the exclusion rule and the {{polar_cut}}-degree polar mask are all assumed, and the page states each one where it is used.

Limits

No reconstruction here carries a formal uncertainty, because none is published: the field's own review names uncertainty quantification as an open problem, and an invented error bar would be worse than no error bar. The six places in the fan are not a global grid. The future grids carry no plate identities, so this page can say that a scenario puts land in the south polar region but cannot say that the land is Antarctica.

The last age shown is {{past_oldest}} million years, where {{n_models_500}} of the {{n_models}} models still reach. The four futures are not ranked either; the paper that built all of them ranks none above the others. Nothing here places Denver, or any named place, at any confidence, in any of the four futures.

The code

fetch_ngl.py, fetch_static.py, fetch_osf.py and fetch_gplates.py get the data and pin it. otis_grid.py and plates.py hold the readers and the gates. break_b1.py is the experiment that broke the first version of one of them. build_continents.py computes and gates everything and writes the payload. fig_continents.py draws the six figures. test_continents.py checks the failure modes.

The archive is larger than the others on this site, and deliberately so. Most of it is three files: the {{stations_mb}} station table, {{land_mb}} of reconstructed coastlines for the viewer, and {{masks_kb}} of packed scenario masks. With those files, a reader rebuilds everything here without a new fetch. A new fetch would need seven megabytes of velocities, nearly five hundred reconstructed frames and a scenario archive that unpacks to about four hundred megabytes. That is the difference between an archive that documents the build and one that reproduces it.
