Precisely measured continents still defy a forecast

The ground under Denver moves {{denver_speed}} millimetres a year, and plate interiors hold their shape to {{band_far}} of a millimetre a year. Yet nobody can say where that ground will be. The two facts do not conflict, and the gap between them is the story.

A tectonic plate is a rigid body, and it is rigid to an almost absurd degree. A single rotation is fitted to every GNSS station more than {{far_km}} kilometers from a plate boundary. The stations more than {{interior_km}} kilometers from a boundary sit on that rotation to a median of {{band_far}} millimetres a year. This holds across thousands of kilometers of continent. Within {{near_km}} kilometers of a boundary, the same fit misses the median station by {{band_near}}. That value is a median over receivers, and the receivers near boundaries are concentrated in a few dense networks, California's above all. Thus the value tells as much about where geodesy happens as about the boundaries. Almost the whole surface of the Earth is a few rafts that do not deform. Everything that happens to them happens in belts a few hundred kilometers wide.

It is the best-known result here, and it is worth less than it appears. Run forward, the fit gives arithmetic, not a forecast. The rotations do not hold. The page shows {{n_slower}} measured cases where a boundary slowed by more than five millimetres a year within the last few million years. Run backward, eight published reconstructions of the same past agree to within {{past_near_lo_km}} to {{past_near_hi_km}} kilometers at {{past_near_age}} million years, depending on the place. The {{n_models_500}} models that reach {{past_far_age}} million years disagree by up to {{past_far_km}} kilometers. Most of that is in longitude, which palaeomagnetism cannot fix on its own. In latitude alone, the widest spread at that age is {{past_far_lat_km}} kilometers. Run far enough forward, four published futures agree with each other for {{agree_until}} million years. After that, the two furthest apart disagree about the land-or-ocean status of {{future_max}} per cent of the Earth's surface.

The projection is Equal Earth, which is equal-area, so an area on one panel means the same as the same area on another panel. All four geometries come from a single 2018 paper by one group of three authors, who built them in a deliberately standardised way. Each geometry stands for one mode of the supercontinent cycle. That paper says the modes "should be treated as end-members of a spectrum of possibilities". Novopangaea has no peer-reviewed primary source at all: it was devised for a television series and reached print in a trade book. The grids are a digitisation of maps that were drawn, not the output of a plate model. The read-me of the deposit says only that they "were made in MATLAB and based off the maps presented in" the 2018 paper. The measured present

{{n_all}} stations carry a MIDAS velocity. The analysis keeps the stations with at least {{min_years}} years of record. It also requires an uncertainty of at most {{max_sigma}} millimetres a year on both horizontal components. {{n_after_cuts}} stations remain, and {{n_with_plate}} of them carry a plate assignment. Nine plates have enough stations far enough from a boundary for a fit.

The shading shows distance to the nearest plate boundary on a uniform lattice. The boundaries of Bird's PB2002 model are drawn over it. Nine plates carry a label with the speed that their fitted rotation gives at that point. The shading is a computed field, not a map of the stations. North America has {{n_na}} stations more than {{far_km}} kilometers from a boundary, and Africa has {{n_af}}. Thus a map of receivers would tell more about where geodesy happens than about the Earth. The speeds come from a fitted model; the next figure shows how well it fits.

The miss of each station against the rotation of its own plate, plotted against its distance from a plate boundary. The fit uses interior stations only, and the analysis then applies it everywhere. Thus the near-boundary points are a test of a prediction, not a report of a fit. Beyond {{far_km}} kilometers, the cloud collapses onto a floor. The group at the top right is Hawaii: {{big_island_plotted}} stations on the Big Island, {{big_island_n}} of them excluded from the Pacific fit. They sit further from a plate boundary than almost anything else on Earth. The worst of them missed the first, uncleaned Pacific fit by {{hawaii_max}} millimetres a year. They miss because the ground on the volcanoes of the island moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the Pacific plate. Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are among those volcanoes.

The Pacific shows why the fit needs a guard. Fitted on all {{pa_before_n}} of its far-field stations, it misses by an RMS of {{pa_before_rms}} millimetres a year. That is an RMS, which a few bad stations dominate; the misses elsewhere on this page are medians. Its rotation sits {{pa_before_itrf}} away from the rotation of the international model. Nearly all of that comes from three groups of stations. The first is {{big_island_n}} stations on the Big Island of Hawaii. The second is four stations in western Alaska: the source file lists them under the Pacific plate, but they sit on the Bering block. The third is one station on Salas y Gómez, filed under the Pacific but actually on Nazca, {{ilsg_resid}} millimetres a year out. A stated rule removes {{pa_dropped}} of the {{pa_before_n}} stations: fit, drop anything past {{mad_k}} median absolute deviations, fit again, repeat. After that, the RMS of the plate decreases to {{pa_after_rms}}. The page gives the rule, not a list of station names, because a list of names is a hardcoded answer to the contamination of today.

Plates change speed

Plate motion is not a constant, and that is a measurement, not an argument. NNR-MORVEL56 averages sea-floor spreading over the last {{morvel_interval}} million years. ITRF2020-PMM averages space geodesy over decades. Where the two disagree, either a boundary changed speed or one of the models is wrong.

Motion of the first plate relative to the second, at a point on the named boundary. The comparisons use only the relative motion between pairs. The two models sit in different reference frames, and only relative motion is frame-independent. Of these boundaries, {{n_slower}} slowed by more than five millimetres a year. Two of them are continental collisions, at the Himalaya and the Zagros. The third is the subduction of Nazca below South America at the Peru-Chile trench. The other boundaries are two mid-ocean ridges, a rift and a continental transform, the San Andreas. One of the ridges, the {{sped_up}}, is now {{sped_up_by}} millimetres a year faster. MORVEL's averaging interval is not one number. It is {{morvel_interval_detail}}. MORVEL's website says that its authors "retain all commercial rights to the graphics and results generated from this Web site". The table used here is from Argus and colleagues 2011, which states "This paper is not subject to U.S. copyright."

India against Eurasia is the case to remember. Its geological average is {{ie_morvel}} millimetres a year, and its geodetic rate is {{ie_itrf}}. That is {{ie_gap}} slower now than over the last few million years. Arabia against Eurasia slowed by {{ar_gap}}, and Nazca against South America slowed by {{nz_gap}}. The mid-Atlantic and the East African rift agree to about two. That is the argument against extrapolation, and it is stronger than any reminder that a hundred million years is a long time. The poles measured today were measurably different three million years ago.

The reconstructed past

Further back, the question is no longer how precisely anything is measured. It is how much published models of the same past disagree. {{n_models}} global plate models were asked where {{n_places}} present-day places were, every {{age_step}} million years to {{past_oldest}}. Each request names its model, because the service documents its default model as liable to change. Also, the rotation files of named models were updated in place as recently as last year.

Reconstructed latitude, not position. Latitude is the honest quantity. Palaeomagnetism determines how far from the equator a rock formed, and it cannot determine its longitude at all unless further assumptions are made. Thus a fan of latitudes is the measurement. A map of positions is the measurement plus the assumption of each model about east and west. Lines end where the coverage of a model ends. Lines that break in the middle are models that place no crust at that place and age. The models share ancestry, so the spread between them is not a spread of independent answers. This page infers, and the source does not say, that the true spread of admissible reconstructions is therefore wider, not narrower.

The three regimes

Three different quantities on one pair of axes, and that is the point. The solid line is measured: the largest distance between any two reconstructions of the same place and age, among the models that reach that age. The number of those models is {{model_count_steps}}. Thus the shape of the line mixes age with the number of models in the comparison. The dashed ray is the miss measured today, run forward at {{band_far}} millimetres a year, which is {{band_far}} kilometers per million years. That ray is the extrapolation that this page argues against, drawn for comparison. The dotted line is a proxy: the separation between the land centroids of the four futures. A centroid averages a whole configuration, so two arrangements that barely overlap can have close centroids, and two that overlap can have distant ones. The comparison with the measured line above it is assumed, and the direction of its bias is not known. The annotation on the dotted line gives the number that the future can be held to in its own units. The ceiling is the antipodal distance, {{antipodal}} kilometers.

Two traps in the inputs

Two of the inputs contain a trap that produces a confident, plausible and wrong number, not an error. Each trap has a check that runs before the number it guards exists. A failure means that there is no page, not a warning in a log.

The four future scenarios do not use a common longitude convention: two declare −180 to 180, and two declare 0 to 360. Read as they come, two of the four sit half a world out. The four present-day grids are four copies of the same map of today. Read as they come, they appear to disagree about {{naive_pct}} per cent of the planet, not {{t0_pct}}. That {{naive_pct}} would have been the headline number of this page. It would have come from real files, and nothing anywhere downstream would show the error.

The obvious guard is that the four grids must agree at the present day. That guard is not sufficient, and this distinction is the reason for this section. The same code reads all four grids. Thus a mistake common to all four leaves them in perfect agreement while every map here is wrong. The checks that can find such a mistake are the ones whose answer comes from outside the data. These are {{n_probes}} named points whose land-or-sea answer comes from an ordinary world map, and the Earth's own land fraction.

Two of those checks are negative controls. They are there because a check that nobody has seen fail is not known to work. If a future release of the scenario grids quietly harmonises the longitude conventions, the guarded numbers would still be produced. The same is true if a future release of the velocity file reassigns the Hawaiian stations. In either case, the account on this page of why the numbers are guarded would become false. Thus the build also requires that the failures are still reproducible, and it stops if they are not.

Method

Velocities are MIDAS estimates of GNSS station motion, in IGS20, which is the IGS realisation of ITRF2020. This frame makes the comparison with ITRF2020-PMM a comparison of models, not of reference frames, and the build asserts it. The rotation of each plate is fitted by weighted least squares on v = ω × r. This is the form in equation 1 of Altamimi and colleagues 2017; neither MORVEL nor the NNR-MORVEL56 paper states it.

A fitted rotation can match a published one at the plate's own stations and still not be pinned; this was measured, not assumed. The stations of a plate cover a patch. In rotation space, one direction moves the rotation far but barely changes any velocity in that patch. For South America, the rotation can move {{break_deg}} degrees per million years, {{break_pct}} per cent of the plate's own rate. Over that movement, the difference at its own stations stays inside the {{b1c_tol}} millimetre a year check. Thus the build compares the rotation vectors directly, in degrees per million years, and that check pins the plate. The station-level check stays beside it, because it is the right check for a contaminated station, which is a different failure.

The NNR-MORVEL56 table comes from Table 1 of the paper itself, and the build cross-checks it against an independent transcription of the same table. The authors' own copy is a JPEG on a host that no longer answers. The argument against extrapolation on this page rests on those numbers.

Distance to a plate boundary is the distance to the nearest PB2002 vertex, not to the nearest point on the segment. This slightly overestimates distance where a segment is sparsely sampled. The cuts, the {{far_km}} kilometer threshold, the exclusion rule and the {{polar_cut}}-degree polar mask are all assumed. The page states each one where it is used.

Limits

No reconstruction here carries a formal uncertainty, because none is published: the field's own review names uncertainty quantification as an open problem. An invented error bar would be worse than no error bar. The six places in the fan are not a global grid. The future grids carry no plate identities. Thus this page can say that a scenario puts land in the south polar region, but it cannot say that the land is Antarctica.

Nothing here says where any named place will be: not at any confidence, and not for any of the four futures. Nor are the four ranked; the paper that built all of them ranks none above the others. The last age shown is {{past_oldest}} million years, where {{n_models_500}} of the {{n_models}} models still reach.

The code

fetch_ngl.py, fetch_static.py, fetch_osf.py and fetch_gplates.py get the data and pin it. otis_grid.py and plates.py hold the readers and the gates. break_b1.py is the experiment that broke the first version of one of them. build_continents.py computes and gates everything and writes the payload. fig_continents.py draws the six figures. test_continents.py checks the failure modes.

The archive is larger than the others on this site, and deliberately so. Most of it is three files: the {{stations_mb}} station table, {{land_mb}} of reconstructed coastlines for the viewer, and {{masks_kb}} of packed scenario masks. With those files, a reader rebuilds everything here without a new fetch. A new fetch would need seven megabytes of velocities, nearly five hundred reconstructed frames and a scenario archive that unpacks to about four hundred megabytes. That is the difference between an archive that documents the build and one that reproduces it.
