The plastics feedstock that U.S. gas users burned as fuel

In {{y_est}}, up to {{phi}} per cent of the ethane in U.S. gas, a raw material for plastic, was burned as fuel instead, because too few plastics plants and export ships could take it. Ethane left in the gas does not become plastic: it goes to gas users with the rest of the gas, is burned in their furnaces, stoves and power plants, and makes the gas hotter. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) found that in July {{y_heat}} the gas delivered in West Virginia carried {{wv}} per cent more heat than the national average, and in North Dakota {{nd}} per cent more, which it said may indicate ethane left in. It also found that "{{q_stoves}}" (Btu, British thermal units, measure heat). The choice is made at gas processing plants, which can take the ethane out of the gas and sell it as a liquid, mostly to be made into ethylene for plastics, or leave it in. EIA calls leaving it in ethane rejection. In May {{y_est}} EIA reported estimates of {{lo}} to {{hi}} barrels a day, about {{plo}} to {{phi}} per cent of the ethane in the gas the plants processed. Plants have taken more of it out since, as plastics plants and export ships took more, but EIA wrote in {{y_rec1}} that ethane can still be left in when its price comes close to that of gas.

Measuring what was left in

Gas plants produce five liquids: ethane, propane, two butanes and natural gasoline. When plants process more gas, they produce more of all five liquids. Thus an increase in the volume of ethane does not show much by itself. Ethane's share of the five liquids shows more, because EIA identifies ethane as different: "{{q_unlike}}". When plants leave ethane in the gas, its share decreases. EIA used the same measure in {{y_28052}}: it reported that the share decreased from {{s2010r}} per cent in 2010 to {{s2015r}} per cent in 2015.

The chart shows ethane's share month by month. The share changes by a few points within each year. Below the share, the chart shows ethane and propane production, each on its own scale.

The price, in EIA's words

According to EIA, ethane sold for about the price of natural gas from late 2012 to mid-2017, measured by the heat each carries. Since then, it has sold at a premium. This page found no ethane price series that is free to republish. Thus it does not compute a price signal of its own; it gives EIA's words, at the dates EIA wrote them.

Method

Volumes. The data is EIA's monthly field production of natural gas plant liquids by product, for the United States, in thousand barrels a day. The products are ethane, propane, normal butane, isobutane and natural gasoline. EIA also gives their total, and the build checks that the total equals the sum of the five each month. A year's share is the mean of its monthly shares. The share over the latest twelve months, {{ttm_from}} to {{ttm_to}}, is the ethane of those months divided by their total. Growth since {{g_base}} compares the latest twelve months with the {{g_base}} average. EIA also compared ethane with the gas produced. In {{y_ngwu}} it wrote that "{{q_test}}". This page uses the share because it is national and monthly.

The {{y_est}} estimate. On {{d_16191}}, EIA wrote: "{{q_estimates_full}}" (thousand barrels a day). EIA did not say whose estimates they were. In {{y_est}}, plants recovered {{rec}} thousand barrels a day on average. Thus the range is {{plo1}} to {{phi1}} per cent of the ethane recovered and left in together.

Checked against EIA. The build reproduces figures that EIA printed from the same series. Among them are ethane's share, {{s2010r}} per cent in 2010 and {{s2015r}} per cent in 2015, and {{y2018}} million barrels a day in 2018. The code lists all of them. This page does not include EIA statements that the series does not reproduce.

Limits

This page is a record of what EIA published, not a model. It has no price series, so it cannot show month by month when the recovery of ethane paid. It can only show the volumes and repeat what EIA wrote. The share changes with the gas itself, which is wetter or drier from one basin to the next. It also changes with the quantity of ethane that plants leave in. Thus the share shows the direction of rejection, not its amount, and it cannot show how much ethane is still left in the gas today. In {{y_rec1}}, EIA wrote that "{{q_closer}}". The totals are national: EIA found rejection "{{q_places}}", and a national share does not show clearly where it occurred.
