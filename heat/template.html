At the average price Texas industry pays for electricity, an electric boiler has not beaten a gas one in any month since 2001

Factories make steam by burning gas. Swapping the burner for an electric heater is the simplest way to electrify it: nothing new to invent, so the cost decides. On EIA's Texas industrial prices, electricity would have had to cost about {{frac_word}} of what industry paid in {{last_month}} for the electric boiler to break even, or gas {{gas_mult}} times what it cost. A plant can do better by keeping its gas boiler, adding a large electric one, and switching to it only in the hours when power on the wholesale market of ERCOT, the grid operator for most of Texas, is cheaper than gas. Even then, with typical delivery charges, the electric boiler would have saved enough to cover a year of its own cost in only {{lg_central_n}} of the {{flex_n}} full years from {{flex_first}}, though the cheap hours have more than doubled: {{hours_recent}} a year in the last five full years, against {{hours_before}} in the five before.

What the electricity would have to cost

An electric boiler turns about {{eta_e}} per cent of its electricity into heat. This page assumes a gas boiler turns {{eta_g}} per cent of its gas into heat, a modern boiler's figure; LBNL's study of boiler electrification gives fossil-fuel boilers 70 to 80 per cent. So for the two to cost the same, a kilowatt hour of electricity has to cost about what a kilowatt hour of gas does, a little more for the gas boiler's losses, and a little more again because the gas boiler costs more to build and run. This page works that break-even out for every month since {{first_year}}, from that month's Texas industrial gas price, and sets it against what Texas industry paid for electricity the same month. Even at 70 per cent, the efficiency least favourable to gas, no month falls below the break-even: the closest is {{robust_min}} times, in {{robust_month}}.

A second boiler that runs only when power is cheap

A large plant can buy power on ERCOT's real-time market, where it is sometimes very cheap and sometimes below zero ({{neg_last}} hours in {{flex_last}}). The case for an electric boiler is strongest as a second boiler: keep the gas one, and switch to electricity in the hours when electricity is cheaper than gas. Each such hour saves the difference. On top of the wholesale price the plant pays ERCOT's own charges on every megawatt hour (${{e_low}} to ${{e_high}}) and, if it is connected at transmission voltage, delivery charges set by how much power it draws at its peak. Texas utilities set a large customer's transmission charge from its use in the four busiest 15-minute periods of the summer, so a plant that runs the boiler in one of them pays about ${{tcrf_kw}} a year more per kilowatt. This page uses three cases: low (no delivery charge on the boiler), central (${{d_central}} per kilowatt a year, one utility's distribution charge) and high (central plus one of those summer periods, ${{d_high}}).

The electric boiler has to pay for itself out of what is left. LBNL's study puts its cost at about ${{cap_small}} per kilowatt for a small boiler (under {{small_mw}} MW) and about ${{cap_large}} for a large one (over {{large_mw}} MW), which is about ${{cost_small}} and ${{cost_large}} a year to own over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} per cent; this page's model uses ${{cap_model}}, about ${{cost_model}} a year.

And the carbon

Electrifying heat only cuts emissions if the electricity is clean enough. An electric boiler running all year emits less than a gas boiler only where the grid emits less than {{parity}} tonnes of carbon dioxide per megawatt hour. ERCOT's average, from EPA's eGRID (generation, before grid losses), is {{avg_t}}: on the average grid the electric boiler emits about {{avg_x}} times as much per unit of heat. A new load is met at the margin by whichever plants are next to run; EPA's AVERT puts that rate in Texas at {{marg_t}} tonnes per megawatt hour for a load that is the same every hour, grid losses included, which makes the electric boiler's emissions about {{marg_x}} times the gas boiler's. A second boiler that runs only in cheap hours could see a cleaner margin than either; this page does not estimate it.

Method

Prices. EIA's Texas industrial electricity price (retail sales: total revenue over total sales, all industrial customers) and Texas industrial natural gas price, monthly, {{first_month}} to {{last_month}}, the last month both series have. Gas is converted from dollars per thousand cubic feet with EIA's Texas heat content of gas delivered to consumers for the year ({{hc_lo}} to {{hc_hi}}; the nearest year outside them). Real-time electricity for the second boiler: ERCOT's hub average, every hour from {{flex_first}}, the same price files as this site's battery page.

The break-even. The cost of a unit of delivered heat from each boiler: fuel divided by efficiency, plus capital recovered over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} per cent real (LBNL's study uses 10), plus fixed operations, at an assumed {{hours}} hours a year at full output. The electric boiler costs ${{capex_e}} and the gas boiler, assumed, ${{capex_g}} per million Btu an hour of capacity, with fixed operations {{fom_e}} and {{fom_g}} per cent of capital a year. The break-even electricity price is the one that makes the two equal. Fuel decides it: leaving capital and operations out moves it by {{nonfuel}} cents. The capital costs are held at today's values back to 2001, which moves it by no more than that.

The second boiler. Each hour the plant uses whichever fuel makes heat more cheaply, at that month's average gas price; an hour when electricity wins saves the gas it did not burn, less what the electricity and ERCOT's per-megawatt-hour charges cost. The year's savings, less the delivery charges per kilowatt, are set against the electric boiler's own annual capital and fixed operations, since the gas boiler is there either way. {{flag_text}}

Limits

EIA's electricity price is an average over every industrial customer, large and small; a big plant on a negotiated contract pays less, which is why the second boiler is priced at wholesale. EIA's gas price covers only part of the industrial gas sold in Texas, about {{gas_cov}} per cent of deliveries in recent years. Gas is a monthly average against hourly power. The charges are estimates from two utilities' published tariffs and the market monitor's figures, not a quote, and leave out each connection's fixed monthly charges and any grid connection work. The capital costs are LBNL's ranges, read from its chart, not vendor prices. The emissions use annual rates and leave out methane leaked before the gas reaches the plant; at a 70 per cent gas boiler the multiples are {{e70_avg}} and {{e70_marg}}. This is an explainer and teaching tool, not a design study or an investment case.
