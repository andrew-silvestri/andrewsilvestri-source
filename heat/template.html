Texas industry finds little reason to electrify its boilers

At the average price Texas industry pays for electricity, an electric boiler has not beaten a gas one in any month since 2001. Factories make steam by burning gas, and swapping the burner for an electric heater is the simplest way to electrify it: there is nothing new to invent, so the cost decides. On EIA's Texas industrial prices, electricity would have had to cost about {{frac_word}} of what industry paid in {{last_month}} for the electric boiler to break even, or gas {{gas_mult}} times what it cost. A plant can do better by keeping its gas boiler, adding a large electric one, and switching to it only in the hours when power on the wholesale market of ERCOT, the grid operator for most of Texas, is cheaper than gas. Even then, with typical delivery charges, the electric boiler would have saved enough to cover a year of its own cost in only {{lg_central_n}} of the {{flex_n}} full years from {{flex_first}}. The cheap hours have more than doubled, though: {{hours_recent}} a year in the last five full years, against {{hours_before}} in the five before.

Where electricity breaks even

An electric boiler turns about {{eta_e}} per cent of its electricity into heat. This page assumes that a gas boiler turns {{eta_g}} per cent of its gas into heat, which is a modern boiler's figure. LBNL's study of boiler electrification gives 70 to 80 per cent for fossil-fuel boilers. Thus, for the two boilers to cost the same, a kilowatt hour of electricity must cost about the same as a kilowatt hour of gas. The break-even adds a little for the gas boiler's losses, and a little more because the gas boiler costs more to build and operate. This page calculates that break-even for every month since {{first_year}}, from that month's Texas industrial gas price. It compares the break-even with the price that Texas industry paid for electricity in the same month. Even at 70 per cent, the efficiency least favourable to gas, no month falls below the break-even: the closest is {{robust_min}} times, in {{robust_month}}.

A boiler for the cheap hours

A large plant can buy power on ERCOT's real-time market, where power is sometimes very cheap and sometimes below zero ({{neg_last}} hours in {{flex_last}}). The case for an electric boiler is strongest when it is a second boiler. The plant keeps the gas boiler and uses electricity in the hours when electricity is cheaper than gas. Each such hour saves the difference. In addition to the wholesale price, the plant pays ERCOT's own charges on every megawatt hour (${{e_low}} to ${{e_high}}). If it is connected at transmission voltage, it also pays delivery charges, which depend on how much power it draws at its peak. Texas utilities set a large customer's transmission charge from its use in the four busiest 15-minute periods of the summer. Thus a plant that runs the boiler in one of these periods pays about ${{tcrf_kw}} a year more per kilowatt. This page uses three cases: low (no delivery charge on the boiler), central (${{d_central}} per kilowatt a year, one utility's distribution charge) and high (central plus one of those summer periods, ${{d_high}}).

The electric boiler must pay for itself from the savings that remain. LBNL's study gives about ${{cap_small}} per kilowatt for a small boiler (under {{small_mw}} MW) and about ${{cap_large}} for a large boiler (over {{large_mw}} MW). Over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} per cent, these boilers cost about ${{cost_small}} and ${{cost_large}} a year to own. This page's model uses ${{cap_model}}, about ${{cost_model}} a year.

The carbon arithmetic

Electrifying heat cuts emissions only if the electricity is clean enough. An all-year electric boiler emits less than a gas boiler only where the grid emits less than {{parity}} tonnes of carbon dioxide per megawatt hour. ERCOT's average, from EPA's eGRID (generation, before grid losses), is {{avg_t}}. On the average grid, the electric boiler thus emits about {{avg_x}} times as much per unit of heat. At the margin, the plants that are next to run supply a new load. EPA's AVERT gives that rate in Texas as {{marg_t}} tonnes per megawatt hour for a load that is the same every hour, grid losses included. At that rate, the electric boiler's emissions are about {{marg_x}} times the gas boiler's. A second boiler that runs only in cheap hours could see a cleaner margin than either; this page does not estimate it.

Method

Prices. The data is EIA's Texas industrial electricity price (retail sales: total revenue over total sales, all industrial customers) and Texas industrial natural gas price. Both are monthly, from {{first_month}} to {{last_month}}, which is the last month that both series have. This page converts gas from dollars per thousand cubic feet with EIA's Texas heat content of gas delivered to consumers for each year. For years outside {{hc_lo}} to {{hc_hi}}, it uses the nearest year. Real-time electricity for the second boiler: ERCOT's hub average, every hour from {{flex_first}}. These are the same price files as on this site's battery page.

The break-even. This page calculates the cost of a unit of delivered heat from each boiler. The cost is fuel divided by efficiency, plus capital recovered over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} per cent real, plus fixed operations. LBNL's study uses 10 per cent. The calculation assumes {{hours}} hours a year at full output. The electric boiler costs ${{capex_e}} per million Btu an hour of capacity, and the gas boiler costs an assumed ${{capex_g}}. Fixed operations are {{fom_e}} and {{fom_g}} per cent of capital a year. The break-even electricity price is the price that makes the two costs equal. Fuel decides it: without capital and operations, the break-even moves by {{nonfuel}} cents. The capital costs stay at today's values back to 2001, which moves the break-even by no more than that.

The second boiler. Each hour, the plant uses the fuel that makes heat at the lower cost, at that month's average gas price. In an hour when electricity is cheaper, the plant saves the gas that it does not burn. From this saving, the model subtracts the cost of the electricity and ERCOT's per-megawatt-hour charges. The model compares the year's savings, less the delivery charges per kilowatt, with the electric boiler's own annual capital and fixed operations. It does not count the gas boiler, because the plant has it in both cases. {{flag_text}}

Limits

EIA's electricity price is an average over all industrial customers, large and small. A big plant on a negotiated contract pays less; for this reason, the model uses wholesale prices for the second boiler. EIA's gas price covers only part of the industrial gas sold in Texas, about {{gas_cov}} per cent of deliveries in recent years. The gas price is a monthly average, but the power price is hourly. The charges are estimates from two utilities' published tariffs and the market monitor's figures, not a quote. They do not include the fixed monthly charges of each connection or any grid connection work. The capital costs are LBNL's ranges, read from its chart, not vendor prices. The emissions use annual rates and do not include methane that leaks before the gas reaches the plant. With a 70 per cent gas boiler, the multiples are {{e70_avg}} and {{e70_marg}}. This page is an explainer and teaching tool, not a design study or an investment case.
