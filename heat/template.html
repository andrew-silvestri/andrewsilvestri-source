Texas industry finds little reason to electrify its boilers

At the average price Texas industry pays for electricity, an electric boiler has not beaten a gas one in any month since 2001. Factories make steam by burning gas, and swapping the burner for an electric heater is the simplest way to electrify it: there is nothing new to invent, so cost alone decides. For the easiest piece of electrification there is, that is a long losing streak. On EIA's Texas industrial prices, electricity would have had to cost about {{frac_word}} of what industry paid in {{last_month}} for the electric boiler to break even (or gas {{gas_mult}} times what it cost). But the verdict rests on which price a plant pays, and a large plant can pay less than the average. Can it buy its power more cleverly? It can keep its gas boiler, add a large electric one beside it, and switch over only in the hours when power on the wholesale market of ERCOT, the grid operator for most of Texas, is cheaper than gas. Even then, with typical delivery charges, the electric boiler would have saved enough to cover a year of its own cost in only {{lg_central_n}} of the {{flex_n}} full years from {{flex_first}}. Yet the cheap hours have more than doubled: {{hours_recent}} a year in the last five full years, against {{hours_before}} in the five before.

Where does electricity break even?

An electric boiler turns about {{eta_e}} percent of its electricity into heat. This page assumes that a gas boiler turns {{eta_g}} percent of its gas into heat, a modern boiler's figure; LBNL's study of boiler electrification gives 70 to 80 percent for fossil-fuel boilers. So for the two boilers to cost the same, a kilowatt hour of electricity must cost about what a kilowatt hour of gas does. The break-even adds a little for the gas boiler's losses, and a little more because the gas boiler costs more to build and operate. This page calculates that break-even for every month since {{first_year}}, from that month's Texas industrial gas price, and sets it against the price Texas industry paid for electricity in the same month. Does the verdict hang on the gas boiler's assumed efficiency? It does not. Even at 70 percent, the efficiency least favourable to gas, no month falls below the break-even: the closest is {{robust_min}} times, in {{robust_month}}.

Can a second boiler pay for itself?

A large plant can buy power on ERCOT's real-time market, where power is sometimes very cheap and sometimes below zero ({{neg_last}} hours in {{flex_last}}). The strongest case for an electric boiler, then, is as a second boiler: the plant keeps the gas boiler and switches to electricity only in the hours when electricity is cheaper than gas, saving the difference in each of them. The wholesale price is not the whole bill, though. On top of it the plant pays ERCOT's own charges on every megawatt hour (${{e_low}} to ${{e_high}}), and, if it is connected at transmission voltage, delivery charges that depend on how much power it draws at its peak.

Those delivery charges are where timing bites. Texas utilities set a large customer's transmission charge from its use in the four busiest 15-minute periods of the summer. A plant that runs the boiler in one of those periods pays about ${{tcrf_kw}} a year more per kilowatt, a stiff fee for a quarter of an hour. This page uses three cases: low (no delivery charge on the boiler), central (${{d_central}} per kilowatt a year, one utility's distribution charge) and high (central plus one of those summer periods, ${{d_high}}). The electric boiler must then pay for itself from the savings that remain. LBNL's study gives about ${{cap_small}} per kilowatt for a small boiler (under {{small_mw}} MW) and about ${{cap_large}} for a large one (over {{large_mw}} MW). Over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} percent, these boilers cost about ${{cost_small}} and ${{cost_large}} a year to own. This page's model uses ${{cap_model}}, about ${{cost_model}} a year.

An all-year electric boiler emits more

Electrifying heat cuts emissions only if the electricity is clean enough. An all-year electric boiler emits less than a gas boiler only where the grid emits less than {{parity}} tonnes of carbon dioxide per megawatt hour. ERCOT's average, from EPA's eGRID (generation, before grid losses), is {{avg_t}}, so on the average grid the electric boiler emits about {{avg_x}} times as much per unit of heat. But a new load is not met by the average plant. It is met at the margin, by the plants that are next to run, and EPA's AVERT puts that rate in Texas at {{marg_t}} tonnes per megawatt hour for a load that is the same every hour, grid losses included. At that rate, the electric boiler's emissions are about {{marg_x}} times the gas boiler's. The two rates answer different questions, and the multiple depends on which one is asked. A second boiler that runs only in cheap hours could see a cleaner margin than either. This page does not estimate it.

Method

Prices. The data is EIA's Texas industrial electricity price (retail sales: total revenue over total sales, all industrial customers) and its Texas industrial natural gas price, both monthly from {{first_month}} to {{last_month}}, the last month that both series have. This page converts gas from dollars per thousand cubic feet with EIA's Texas heat content of gas delivered to consumers for each year, and for years outside {{hc_lo}} to {{hc_hi}} it uses the nearest year. Real-time electricity for the second boiler is ERCOT's hub average, every hour from {{flex_first}}: the same price files as on this site's battery page.

The break-even. This page calculates the cost of a unit of delivered heat from each boiler: fuel divided by efficiency, plus capital recovered over {{life}} years at an assumed {{r_pct}} percent real (LBNL's study uses 10 percent), plus fixed operations. The calculation assumes {{hours}} hours a year at full output. The electric boiler costs ${{capex_e}} per million Btu an hour of capacity, and the gas boiler an assumed ${{capex_g}}; fixed operations are {{fom_e}} and {{fom_g}} percent of capital a year. The break-even electricity price is the price that makes the two costs equal. Fuel decides it. Without capital and operations, the break-even moves by {{nonfuel}} cents, and holding the capital costs at today's values back to 2001 moves it by no more than that.

The second boiler. Each hour, the plant uses whichever fuel makes heat at the lower cost, at that month's average gas price. In an hour when electricity is cheaper, the plant saves the gas that it does not burn, and from this saving the model subtracts the cost of the electricity and ERCOT's per-megawatt-hour charges. It then compares the year's savings, less the delivery charges per kilowatt, with the electric boiler's own annual capital and fixed operations. The gas boiler is not counted, because the plant has it in both cases. {{flag_text}}

Limits

EIA's electricity price is an average over all industrial customers, large and small, and a big plant on a negotiated contract pays less; for this reason, the model uses wholesale prices for the second boiler. EIA's gas price covers only part of the industrial gas sold in Texas, about {{gas_cov}} percent of deliveries in recent years, and it is a monthly average set against an hourly power price. The charges are estimates from two utilities' published tariffs and the market monitor's figures, not a quote, and they leave out each connection's fixed monthly charges and any grid connection work. The capital costs are LBNL's ranges, read from its chart, not vendor prices. The emissions use annual rates and do not include methane that leaks before the gas reaches the plant; with a 70 percent gas boiler, the multiples are {{e70_avg}} and {{e70_marg}}. This page is an explainer and teaching tool, not a design study or an investment case.
