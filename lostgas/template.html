At about {{h_median}} per cent, utilities' lost gas is lost in meter error

Customers pay, through rates set under regulators' rules, for the gas their utility cannot account for. In Texas and Oklahoma, the two states this page follows, all of it comes to about a dollar or two a year on a home's bill. Some of that gas was burned by customers whose meters missed it. Every U.S. gas utility reports the share each year. But the typical report is a smaller number than ordinary meter error can produce on its own, so it cannot tell a leak from a meter's mistake. In this page's model of a system that loses nothing at all, meter error alone puts the figure anywhere from about {{band_lo_r}} to {{band_hi_r}} per cent at its assumptions. About a third of reports are zero or negative, and a negative means the meters counted more gas going out than coming in, which no leak can do.

What utilities file

The figure is lost and unaccounted-for gas. It is the gas that a distribution system bought or produced, minus the gas that its customers and the company used (with some adjustments). The figure gives this difference as a percentage of the gas used, over the twelve months to June 30. Each operator files the figure with the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), the federal pipeline safety regulator. It files one report for each state that it serves. PHMSA's own report to Congress says what it is: "{{q_mix}}"

Who pays

In Texas, the Railroad Commission's rule presumes that all lost and unaccounted-for gas is gas actually lost. A utility can prove in a rate case that part of it is something else, and the rule names "{{q_tx_meter}}" among those things. For ratemaking, the rule sets a limit of {{tx_cap}} per cent of the gas metered into a distribution system. It disallows the expense of lost gas above that limit unless the Commission allows more on the facts of a case. The Commission can still judge whether the cost is reasonable. The rule binds the utilities that the Commission regulates. Of the {{tx_n}} Texas reports for {{h_year}}, {{tx_muni}} come from systems owned by cities, whose rates the cities set.

Oklahoma's rule requires gas to be measured "{{q_ok}}", but it sets no percentage. The state's rules for the pass-through of gas costs to customers do not mention lost gas. Of the {{ok_n}} Oklahoma reports for {{h_year}}, {{ok_muni}} come from city-owned systems. One large utility's tariff, which the Oklahoma Corporation Commission approved, says "{{q_ok_pga}}". The purchased gas adjustment (PGA) is the part of a bill that passes the cost of gas through to customers. The tariff gives a two per cent safe harbor, and above it the Commission can disallow the excess. The tariff's figure counts gas that the company used as well as gas lost, so it is not on PHMSA's basis.

Delivered, but counted as lost

{{clip}}

Gas enters a distribution system through a city-gate station. The clip shows one type of station meter, an orifice meter: a plate with a hole in it, across the pipe. The meter infers the flow from the pressure drop across the plate. It uses a coefficient from an equation fitted to laboratory data, not from a test of that meter. The gas leaves through the customer meters, and a large system has thousands of them. Each of these meters has a small error. The reported figure is the difference between what the meters show came in and what they show went out.

The own error of each meter averages away across many meters. An error that the meters share does not. The clearest shared error is temperature. A customer meter that does not correct for temperature counts the actual volume of the gas. The bill treats that volume as if the gas were at 60 °F. Cold gas is denser, so the meter counts too little of it: at {{m_temp}} °F, about {{m_tbias}} per cent too little. This page assumes that {{m_uncomp}} per cent of a system's meters do not correct for temperature; this is not a measured figure. At that share, about {{m_shared}} per cent of the gas delivered is never billed by a meter. That gas still shows as unaccounted for, and its cost goes into the gas charge that every customer pays. Thus that part of "lost" gas is not an extra charge for gas that nobody received. It is gas that customers burned, billed through the gas charge and not through their meters. Warm gas has the opposite effect and decreases the figure, which is one way that a system can report a negative.

What the figure cannot show

A negative figure cannot be gas physically lost, because the meters show more gas went out than came in. Many exact zeros follow an old rule of the form, not a measurement. Until the {{rule_prev}} reports, PHMSA told companies: "{{q_zero}}" From the {{rule_year}} reports, negatives are allowed. Of the {{neg_rule}} negative reports that year, {{neg_rule_zero}} came from systems that reported exactly zero the year before. In {{h_year}}, {{h_zero}} reports were exactly zero and {{h_neg}} were negative.

Nor is the figure a measure of leaks. The same report to Congress says: "{{q_proxy}}" A report gives one percentage and nothing that can separate a real loss from a meter error. It can show a system whose figure is far outside what meters alone produce, year after year. It cannot show how much of a typical figure is loss.

Method

The reports. The data are PHMSA's gas distribution annual reports, Form PHMSA F 7100.1-1, Part G, field PERCENT_UNACC_GAS, from its bulk file of {{y0}} to {{y1}} reports. The form defines the figure as "{{q_partg}}", over the twelve months that end on June 30. Each report covers one operator in one state, by the state of operation. From {{commodity_from}}, the reports say which gas a system carries. This page keeps only natural gas systems and leaves out {{n_excluded}} propane, landfill, synthetic and other reports. The chart puts the {{h_n}} reports for {{h_year}} into right-inclusive quarter-point bins, so the bin that ends at zero holds the exact zeros.

A system that loses nothing. The model is one system with no loss at all. A city-gate station measures the receipts, and {{m_n}} customer meters measure the deliveries. The figure is (in − out) / out. Each error is a relative error on a volume, with a normal distribution. Each error has a part that stays with the system and a part that the model draws again each year. Every input is an assumption. The city-gate standard uncertainty is {{m_cg}} per cent. The shared error of the customer meters is {{m_fleet}} per cent between systems, and its drift is {{m_drift}} per cent a year. The own error of each meter is {{m_meter}} per cent. Of the meters, {{m_uncomp}} per cent have no temperature correction. The mean gas temperature is {{m_temp}} °F, and it varies by {{m_tempsd}} °F from year to year. The billing-period mismatch is {{m_timing}} per cent. The shaded range is the middle 90 per cent of {{m_systems}} such systems. The reported year-to-year changes are as wide as the changes in this model at about {{m_scale}} times these uncertainties. Thus the uncertainties are not set high. On PHMSA's basis, gas consumed, Texas's {{tx_cap}} per cent of gas metered in is {{tx_cap_c}} per cent. This assumes that the gas consumed is the gas that came in less the gas lost.

The meter model

The pipeline meter model behind the clip, and what its errors are worth

The city-gate side of the model uses an earlier piece of this work. That work is a Monte Carlo model of a pipeline system of orifice meters. It is built on ISO 5167-2's Reader-Harris/Gallagher discharge coefficient and NIST's reference implementation of AGA Report No. 8. Limits

The reports are the operators' own figures, and nothing here checks them. The inputs of the no-loss model are assumptions, not measurements of any system. The model draws its errors from a normal distribution. A station meter that fails for part of a year can move a figure much further. So can a regulator off its set point or a wrong correction factor. The model has nothing for theft, unmetered use, estimated reads, accounting adjustments or leaks. The dollars are approximations. The text of this page names no operator. The Sources cite the tariff of one Oklahoma utility by name, and the downloadable table gives PHMSA's operator names as PHMSA publishes them.
