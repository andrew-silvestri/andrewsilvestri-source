At about {{h_median}} percent, utilities' lost gas is lost in meter error

The gas a utility reports as lost is, at the typical system, too small a number to tell a leak from a meter's mistake. Every U.S. gas utility reports the share each year, and the typical report is smaller than the spread that ordinary meter error can produce on its own: in this page's model of a system that loses nothing at all, meter error alone puts the figure anywhere from about {{band_lo_r}} to {{band_hi_r}} percent at its assumptions. The figure looks like a measurement of loss, but what it means turns on assumptions about meters that a report never states. Customers pay for that gas anyway, through rates set under regulators' rules. In Texas and Oklahoma, the two states this page follows, all of it comes to about a dollar or two a year on a home's bill, and some of it was burned by customers whose meters missed it. About a third of reports are zero or negative. A negative means the meters counted more gas going out than coming in, which no leak can do.

What does a utility file?

The figure is lost and unaccounted-for gas: the gas that a distribution system bought or produced, minus the gas that its customers and the company used (with some adjustments), given as a percentage of the gas used over the twelve months to June 30. Each operator files it with the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), the federal pipeline safety regulator, in one report for each state that it serves. At the typical system it cannot separate loss from the meters' own error, and how much of it meter error could explain turns on two assumptions this page has to make: the temperature of the gas and how many customer meters correct for it. PHMSA's own report to Congress says what it is: "{{q_mix}}"

Customers pay, up to a point

In Texas, the Railroad Commission's rule presumes that all lost and unaccounted-for gas is gas actually lost, though a utility can prove in a rate case that part of it is something else; the rule names "{{q_tx_meter}}" among those things. For ratemaking, the rule sets a limit of {{tx_cap}} percent of the gas metered into a distribution system and disallows the expense of lost gas above that limit unless the Commission allows more on the facts of a case. The Commission can still judge whether the cost is reasonable. The rule binds the utilities that the Commission regulates, and of the {{tx_n}} Texas reports for {{h_year}}, {{tx_muni}} come from systems owned by cities, whose rates the cities set.

Oklahoma's rule requires gas to be measured "{{q_ok}}", but it sets no percentage, and the state's rules for passing gas costs through to customers do not mention lost gas. Of the {{ok_n}} Oklahoma reports for {{h_year}}, {{ok_muni}} come from city-owned systems. Where the rules are silent, a tariff speaks. One large utility's tariff, which the Oklahoma Corporation Commission approved, says "{{q_ok_pga}}"; the purchased gas adjustment (PGA) is the part of a bill that passes the cost of gas through to customers. The tariff gives a two percent safe harbor, and above it the Commission can disallow the excess. Its figure counts gas that the company used as well as gas lost, so it is not on PHMSA's basis.

Delivered, but counted as lost

{{clip}}

Gas enters a distribution system through a city-gate station. The clip shows one type of station meter, the orifice meter (a plate with a hole in it, across the pipe), which infers the flow from the pressure drop across the plate using a coefficient from an equation fitted to laboratory data, not from a test of that meter. The gas leaves through the customer meters, and a large system has thousands of them, each with a small error of its own. The reported figure is the difference between what the meters show came in and what they show went out. Every one of those errors lands in it.

Why does all that error not simply average away? The own error of each meter does, across many meters. An error that the meters share does not, and the clearest shared error is temperature. A customer meter that does not correct for temperature counts the actual volume of the gas, and the bill treats that volume as if the gas were at 60 °F. Cold gas is denser, so the meter counts too little of it: at {{m_temp}} °F, about {{m_tbias}} percent too little. Warm gas has the opposite effect and decreases the figure, which is one way that a system can report a negative.

This page assumes that {{m_uncomp}} percent of a system's meters do not correct for temperature, and that share is an assumption, not a measured figure. At that share, about {{m_shared}} percent of the gas delivered is never billed by a meter. That gas still shows as unaccounted for, and its cost goes into the gas charge that every customer pays. So that part of "lost" gas is not an extra charge for gas that nobody received. It is gas that customers burned, billed through the gas charge instead of through their meters.

What the figure cannot show

A negative figure cannot be gas physically lost, because the meters show more gas went out than came in. In {{h_year}}, {{h_zero}} reports were exactly zero and {{h_neg}} were negative. Many of the exact zeros follow an old rule of the form, not a measurement: until the {{rule_prev}} reports, PHMSA told companies, "{{q_zero}}" From the {{rule_year}} reports, negatives are allowed, and of the {{neg_rule}} negative reports that year, {{neg_rule_zero}} came from systems that had reported exactly zero the year before. Those zeros recorded the form's instructions more faithfully than the gas.

Nor is the figure a measure of leaks, and the same report to Congress says so: "{{q_proxy}}" A report gives one percentage and nothing that can separate a real loss from a meter error. So what can it show? A system whose figure is far outside what meters alone produce, year after year. It cannot show how much of a typical figure is loss.

Method

The reports. The data are PHMSA's gas distribution annual reports (Form PHMSA F 7100.1-1, Part G, field PERCENT_UNACC_GAS), from its bulk file of {{y0}} to {{y1}} reports. The form defines the figure as "{{q_partg}}", over the twelve months that end on June 30. Each report covers one operator in one state, by the state of operation. From {{commodity_from}} the reports say which gas a system carries, and this page keeps only natural gas systems, leaving out {{n_excluded}} propane, landfill, synthetic and other reports. The chart puts the {{h_n}} reports for {{h_year}} into right-inclusive quarter-point bins, so the bin that ends at zero holds the exact zeros.

A system that loses nothing. The model is one system with no loss at all: a city-gate station measures the receipts, {{m_n}} customer meters measure the deliveries, and the figure is (in − out) / out. Each error is a relative error on a volume, drawn from a normal distribution, with a part that stays with the system and a part that the model draws again each year. Every input is an assumption. The city-gate standard uncertainty is {{m_cg}} percent. The shared error of the customer meters is {{m_fleet}} percent between systems, with a drift of {{m_drift}} percent a year, and the own error of each meter is {{m_meter}} percent. Of the meters, {{m_uncomp}} percent have no temperature correction. The mean gas temperature is {{m_temp}} °F, varying by {{m_tempsd}} °F from year to year, and the billing-period mismatch is {{m_timing}} percent. The shaded range is the middle 90 percent of {{m_systems}} such systems.

Are those uncertainties set high, to make the range wide? The reported year-to-year changes are as wide as the changes in this model at about {{m_scale}} times these uncertainties, so if anything the range is narrow. One conversion is needed to compare Texas's rule with the reports. On PHMSA's basis, gas consumed, Texas's {{tx_cap}} percent of gas metered in is {{tx_cap_c}} percent, assuming that the gas consumed is the gas that came in less the gas lost.

Limits

The reports are the operators' own figures, and nothing here checks them. The inputs of the no-loss model are assumptions, not measurements of any system, and the model draws its errors from a normal distribution. A station meter that fails for part of a year can move a figure much further, and so can a regulator off its set point or a wrong correction factor. The model has nothing for theft, unmetered use, estimated reads, accounting adjustments or leaks. The dollars are approximations. The text of this page names no operator; the Sources cite the tariff of one Oklahoma utility by name, and the downloadable table gives PHMSA's operator names as PHMSA publishes them.
