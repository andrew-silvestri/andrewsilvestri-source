Defaults moved low-carbon choices by up to {{d_hi}} points; labels and signs, by under two

Most advice on climate behavior is advice about words: the right message, the right messenger, the right frame. The field trials point somewhere less glamorous. When researchers counted what people actually bought or signed up for, the best-tested labels and signs moved the share choosing the low-carbon option by a point or two. Changing what people get if they do nothing moved it by tens of points. This page puts those trials on one scale, shows how message effects shrink as samples grow, and ends with a kit of compromise asks, each graded by how well it has held up.

One scale for very different trials

Each row of the chart above is a field trial in which people made a real choice, a meal bought or a tariff signed, and the researchers counted the share who chose the lower-carbon option with and without a change. The difference between those two shares is the effect, in percentage points: the gap between two percentages, not a percent of either. A worked example shows the arithmetic. In a German energy supplier's online shop in 2012, {{eb_b}} percent of the contracts sold were green when customers had to select the green tariff themselves, and {{eb_t}} percent when it came selected and they had to switch it off. That is {{eb_pp}} percentage points, about a tenfold rise, for a green premium of 0.3 euro cents a kilowatt-hour.

The rule for a row is strict on purpose. Surveys of what people say they would choose are left out, because stated intentions run ahead of purchases; so are laboratory games. Home energy reports, which cut electricity use by about 2 percent, are left out of this chart for a different reason: they measure how much people use, not the share who chose something, and the two cannot share an axis. They return in the next section. What remains is a small, uneven set, and the gap between its two halves is still too wide for the unevenness to close.

Evidence: strong for the electricity rows, which are randomized trials with thousands of households each; moderate for the food rows, several of which compare weeks or sites rather than randomized people.

Why does the default win?

A default works on three things at once: the effort of changing, the hint that the offered option is the normal one, and plain inattention. None of them asks anyone to be persuaded. That is also why a default is weaker evidence of conviction. In the Sacramento pricing trial, households that stayed on time-of-use pricing only because it was the default cut their use at the evening peak by about a third as much, per household, as those who chose the plan for themselves. They still cut it, and because about five times as large a share of households ended up on the plan, the default saved more per household offered it. It also lasted: by September 2013, after a second summer, 92.6 percent of the defaulted households were still on the plan, against 18.1 percent of those invited to join.

Outside randomized trials the pattern holds over years. When a Swiss utility switched its customers to a green tariff unless they objected, about 80 percent of households were still on it six years later. That is a before-and-after case, not an experiment, but no message in the record has kept a fifth of that effect for a fifth of that time.

Message effects shrink when the sample grows

The small numbers for words are not an accident of the trials chosen. Studies of behavior change shrink in a predictable way when they are repeated at scale. Nudge trials published in academic journals reported an average gain of 8.7 percentage points in take-up; the same kinds of nudges run by government behavioral-science units across more than 23 million people averaged 1.4. The authors attribute most of the gap to publication bias, the tendency of small studies with large effects to reach print. Home energy reports kept about three quarters of their effect, {{opower_first}} percent of electricity use falling to {{opower_later}}, when they spread from the first 10 utilities to 111, partly because the first sites were the most receptive.

{{chart_shrink}}

Defaults shrink too, which is the honest part of the chart. A 2022 reanalysis of more than 400 nudge effects found that, after correcting for publication bias, nudges that add information, such as labels and facts, had no detectable average effect, and nudges that change the choice itself, defaults and menus among them, kept a small one (d {{struct_first}} before correction, {{struct_later}} after) with wide uncertainty. Climate field interventions as a group fell from d {{berg_first}} after bias correction to {{berg_later}} in the 32 largest studies, about 2 percentage points.

Evidence: strong. Four independent meta-analyses and one census of government trials agree on the direction; they differ on how much survives.

What happened when 11 messages were tested in 63 countries?

The largest test of climate messages to date ran in 2022, online. A team of more than 200 researchers tested 11 interventions chosen by experts, from a letter to a future generation to a summary of the scientific consensus, against a control group that read a passage from Dickens. This page re-estimates the effects from the {{n_tour}} participants in the authors' public data. The message that did most for belief in climate change, {{belief_name}}, raised it by {{belief_top}} points on a 100-point scale; the one that did most for support of climate policy, {{policy_name}}, raised that by {{policy_top}}. A message designed to stir negative emotion raised willingness to share climate information on social media by {{share_top}} percentage points, from a base of about {{share_ctrl}} percent.

Then came the one costly act the study measured. Participants could complete up to eight pages of a tedious screening task, and each page they completed led the researchers to plant one tree. In the control group about {{wept_any}} percent did at least one page, and the average was {{wept_ctrl}}.

{{chart_tournament}}

None of the 11 messages raised the number of pages. Six lowered it by an amount the data can tell from zero, and the four largest drops, {{wept_lo}} to {{wept_hi}} pages, are up to {{wept_pct}} percent of the control average. The negative-emotion message that did most for sharing did most harm to effort. Part of the loss may be fatigue, since several of these messages took minutes to read, but the short ones did not raise effort either.

Evidence: moderate. The sample is very large and the analysis was preregistered, but every outcome is online and immediate; the tree task measures effort, not emissions.

What a default does not do

The case against reading the hero chart as a verdict is strong, and it starts with what the long bars count. Each is one choice at one counter, form or shop. A diner served lentils at lunch can order steak at dinner; most food trials cannot see it. One cafeteria study that looked found no rebound at dinner in the same cafeteria, and a Swiss campus study found a small rise in meat sales the day after meat-free days, but no trial has followed people's whole diet. A 2025 review of rigorous trials aimed at cutting meat found an average effect of 0.07 standard deviations on what people ate, and 0.01 after correcting for publication bias; only two choice-design studies met its standard, largely because so few measure eating rather than choosing.

Electricity has its own version of the problem. A green tariff moves a customer's contract, but critics argue that it often reallocates renewable power that already exists rather than causing new plants to be built. And a default can cost the people least able to notice it: in a Swiss study, about half of the lowest-income customers on a green default said they would rather have the cheaper tariff.

The sharpest risk is political. In online experiments, offering people a green-energy default lowered their support for a carbon tax from 70.3 percent to 55.0 percent, as if the small step had paid for the large one. The same researchers found a cheap fix: telling people plainly that the default's effect is small, and that most emissions come from elsewhere, brought support for the tax back to 72 percent without reducing support for the default. Those results come from one group and hypothetical votes. They are still the best evidence on the question, and they shape the kit below.

Evidence: moderate. Compensation and crowding out are each measured in a handful of studies, mostly short or hypothetical; the direction is consistent, the size is not settled.

A kit built on less, not none

What would a compromise message look like if it were built from this record rather than from intuition? It would change the choice more than the person, offer a smaller step rather than demand a total one, swap rather than subtract, and say honestly how large the step is. The table records what was tested and how much it moved; it is not a ranking of what anyone ought to do. Grade A means several field trials or one very large program on real behavior; B, one good field study; C, surveys, online tasks or a single small trial. Two words often blamed for putting people off, "sacrifice" and "eco", have no field test in either direction, so they get no grade.

{{kit_table}}

Read down the grades and a pattern appears. The A rows change the setting: what is offered, what is preselected, what is made cheap or easy, what is shown at the moment of use. The C rows are the ones people argue about most, the wording, the framing, the tone. Persuasion is not useless; it may be what makes a default acceptable to the people who must approve it. But in the record so far, the strongest message is not a sentence. It is the box already ticked.

Method

Field trials. A trial enters the hero chart when it meets four rules. It measures a real choice in the field. Its outcome is the share of people or purchases that took the lower-carbon option. It prints both shares. Its primary text was opened and read. The two shares come from the paper's table or text, which the build file names for each row. The effect is the paper's own printed estimate. The build stops when the difference of the two shares is more than 0.5 points from that estimate. Where a paper reports a model estimate, the chart shows the printed shares, and the number is the printed estimate.

Replication pairs. Each bar in the second chart is a later or larger-sample estimate divided by the first estimate, both in the paper's own unit: percentage points, percent of electricity use, or Cohen's d, a difference measured in standard deviations. The values are from the table or page that the build file names.

Tournament. The data are the authors' deposit on Zenodo. The fetch script checks the file against the checksum that Zenodo publishes. Rows with no condition label are dropped, as the authors' code does. Belief and policy support are each participant's mean over the answered items, 0 to 100. Sharing is 1 if willing and 0 if not; people who do not use social media are excluded. The tree task is pages completed, 0 to 8. Each effect is an ordinary least squares estimate against the control group, with country fixed effects and standard errors clustered by country. The build stops when any tree-task effect is more than 0.06 pages from the paper's own estimate in its supplementary Table S4.

Grades and dates. Grades are this page's judgment, defined in the kit section. The literature was searched to {{lit_through}}.

Limits

The eleven rows are a selection, not a census. They are the trials that met the rules above, and other trials of the same kinds give other numbers. Baselines differ: a share of 2 percent and a share of 50 percent cannot move the same way. Several food rows compare weeks or sites, not randomized people. One row rests on the research team's published summary, because the journal article was not open to read. The page does not convert effects to tonnes of carbon dioxide. A share of choices does not convert to tonnes without assumptions about what people did instead. The tournament's outcomes are online. The kit's examples are illustrations of the tested asks, not the wording that the trials used, unless the trial is named for that wording.
