A neuron in a textbook has a round body, a few branches at the top and a tail that leaves the frame. It is a diagram, and everyone knows it is a diagram. The interesting question is what the honest version would look like, and the answer is that nobody can draw one — not because the data is secret but because no single file contains it.

There is a public archive of reconstructed neurons: {{n_total}} morphologies traced by hand from real tissue. This page counts every one of them, and opens the files themselves from {{sample_archives}} of the contributing archives. The archive records, for each reconstruction, which parts of the cell it contains, whether its widths were measured, whether it is three-dimensional, whether its axon was followed to the end, and whether anyone corrected for the tissue shrinking under the microscope. Requiring all five leaves {{n_complete}} files out of {{n_total}}, which is {{pc_complete}}% of them. That is the archive's count. Opened, {{opened_pass}} of the {{n_complete}} pass this page's own test for a measured width, {{opened_single}} hold a single width for the entire arbor despite the flag that says otherwise, and one of them is drawn below at three scales.

Why the diagram is schematic

Start with the reason the textbook picture exists, which is not laziness. A whole-brain reconstruction of a single neuron reaches about {{span_extent}} from end to end. The thinnest process such a cell carries is about {{span_thinnest}}, a figure from electron microscopy, because visible light cannot resolve anything that small. Drawing both at once means holding a range of {{span_range}} to one in a single picture: {{span_orders}} orders of magnitude.

Every scale one cell holds at once. The band is about what a figure can render between its largest and smallest mark, which is assumed rather than measured: three orders of magnitude. The cell needs {{span_orders}}.

A figure printed at a thousand pixels across would render that thinnest process {{span_px}} of a pixel wide. Showing it as a single pixel would take a canvas {{span_px_needed}} pixels across. The schematic is not a casual lie about a neuron; it is what is left when a shape that spans {{span_orders}} orders of magnitude is forced onto something that holds three.

The two neurons nobody can combine

So a reconstruction has to choose, and the choice is made by how the tissue was prepared rather than by anyone's preference. A cell recorded in a slice sits in a piece of brain a third of a millimetre thick; its widths can be measured, and its axon is cut off by the blade. A cell traced through a whole brain keeps its axon, and nobody measured its widths.

Two real files at one scale, {{pair_upp}} per pixel. The large cell is {{ml_archive}}: {{ml_axon}} of axon reaching {{ml_reach}} from the soma, a hairline because the file holds one width. Right: the box opened, at {{pair_own_upp}} per pixel, with the {{allen_distinct}} recorded widths drawn at their true size, dendrite in blue and axon in brown; where a width is thinner than a pixel, a fainter hairline carries the shape.

The large cell carries {{ml_axon}} of axon. The cell in the box carries {{allen_axon}}, and it is the kind of picture that a search for a cortical neuron returns. Neither is wrong. Each is missing the half the other has, and the halves cannot be added, because they come from different animals prepared in different ways.

Is it a gradient?

The obvious next claim is that the further an arbor reaches, the coarser its recorded thickness becomes — a smooth trade rather than two camps. Pooled across every sampled cell, the rank correlation between reach and coarseness is {{grad_pooled}}, which looks like strong support. It is not, because reach and method are the same variable in that sample: almost every far-reaching cell is whole-brain and almost every near one is a slice, so the correlation measures which archive a file came from.

The test that separates them is to run it inside each archive, where the method is held constant. An archive qualifies with at least {{grad_min_cells}} sampled cells and a {{grad_spread}}-fold spread in reach, because a set of cells that all reach the same distance cannot show a gradient either way. A gradient counts as surviving if the median within-archive correlation exceeds {{grad_rho_rule}} and at least {{grad_share_rule}}% of qualifying archives exceed it. That rule was fixed before the answer was known.

{{grad_cells}} sampled reconstructions from {{grad_all_archives}} archives. The verdict: {{grad_verdict}}. Median within-archive correlation {{grad_within}} across {{grad_archives}} qualifying archives, against a pooled figure of {{grad_pooled}}.

{{grad_verdict}}. What the data supports is the weaker and more specific statement: two ways of preparing tissue, each of which destroys what the other keeps.

Who corrects

The {{n_complete}} reconstructions that meet every requirement come from {{n_labs}} laboratories. {{complete_species}}. {{top_doi_n}} of them — {{top_doi_pc}}% — carry a single DOI, {{top_doi}}, which is the paper that states the shrinkage correction those files were corrected with. The corrected corner of the archive is not a slow accumulation of good practice across the field. It is a few groups that correct as a matter of protocol, and their cells.

The file holds {{drawn_widths_file}} distinct widths: {{drawn_axon_distinct}} on the axon, {{drawn_dend_distinct}} on the dendrites, of which {{drawn_widths_shared}} appear on both, so the neurites between them hold {{drawn_widths_union}}; the last is the soma's own, {{drawn_soma_width}}, which is drawn as the disc in every panel but belongs to neither compartment and is not part of the width test. So even in the corrected corner of the archive the axon's width is a floor and the dendrite's is a measurement, which is why panel B shows dendrite in ink and axon as a hairline. The cell was chosen by rule rather than by eye: the largest single-paper group among the {{n_complete}}, less the {{drawn_rule_fail}} file in it that fails the width test when opened, and then the cell nearest that group's median reach and axon length. End to end it spans {{drawn_extent}}; the whole-brain cell in the second figure spans {{ml_x_drawn}} times that.

The {{n_complete}} also contain {{proj_n}} cells that look like the thing this page says no file holds: {{proj_species}} pyramidal cells from {{proj_region}}, filled {{proj_protocol}}, with {{proj_axon_min}} to {{proj_axon_max}} of axon reaching {{proj_reach_min}} to {{proj_reach_max}} from the soma and {{proj_distinct_min}} to {{proj_distinct_max}} recorded widths, flagged in the archive as corrected for shrinkage and as having a complete axon. All {{proj_n}} come from one paper, and that paper contains no statement about shrinkage or a correction; on completeness it says that some axons might have been incompletely traced, and that in most cases the callosal axon was lost inside the fibre tract. The flags are the archive's curation fields, not the authors' claims, and they are the reason every count on this page is read from the files as well as from the metadata.

That is what the question on this page looks like when it is made concrete. Not whether a picture is accurate, but how far you have to go before the number under it was measured by someone, on purpose, and written down.

The same thing happens to numbers

A figure loses its provenance in the same way a picture does. Two examples turned up in the course of checking the sources for this page, and neither is about neuroscience going wrong; both are about a number outliving the measurement it came from.

The membrane area of a dendrite is roughly double what its traced skeleton suggests, because spines carry much of it. The factor is often attributed to a 2001 paper on hippocampal synapse counts, which contains no such factor: it is the source for where spines are, not how much membrane they hold. The measured factor is {{spine_f}}, from a 2016 study of human cortical cells, and it applies only beyond sixty micrometres from the soma, because the dendrite is nearly bare closer in. So the familiar shortcut — double it — is wrong twice: wrong in its source, and wrong to apply it uniformly.

The synaptic delay everyone quotes as half a millisecond comes from a 1965 paper on the frog neuromuscular junction. That paper reports a minimum of {{katz_min}}, at twenty degrees, in a low-calcium solution chosen to make single events visible; the typical delay in the same preparation is {{katz_modal}}. A fast central synapse at body temperature releases in about a hundred and fifty microseconds. Three different quantities, routinely quoted as one.

Limits

The five requirements are read from the archive's own metadata, which means they are the depositors' claims about their files rather than measurements of them. That distinction is not decorative: a reconstruction is flagged as having a measured width if its widths are not all identical, and that test passes a file holding three values. Every width statement on this page is therefore computed from the files, using a stricter rule that is stated in the code and labelled assumed in the payload. Applied to the {{n_complete}} themselves, that rule fails {{opened_fail}} of them ({{opened_fails_by}}), and {{opened_single}} of those hold one width for the whole arbor and carry the flag anyway.

Nor can you always tell what a file's numbers mean. An SWC file has seven columns and none of them is a unit: the convention is micrometres, and there is no field in which a file could say otherwise. Of the {{units_total}} archives sampled here, {{units_ok}} can be confirmed to be in micrometres and {{units_rejected_n}} cannot: {{units_rejected}}. One of them describes an arbor reaching {{units_worst}} µm from its soma, which is further than the brain it came from; the other gives its cells a soma about the width of a mitochondrion. Neither file is corrupt. They are almost certainly written in nanometres or in voxels, correctly, by people who knew what they meant. The test used here is the only yardstick inside a file: a soma is between {{units_soma_min}} and {{units_soma_max}} µm across in essentially every neuron, so an archive whose somata are not somata is not in micrometres. That leaves {{units_kept}} of {{sample_n}} sampled reconstructions to measure from, and the {{units_dropped}} dropped are counted and named rather than quietly discarded.

A measured width is not the same as a reliable one. When the same eight cells were reconstructed by two competent light-microscopy pipelines, the widths differed by about a factor of two on visually matched segments: {{diam_pipelines}}. The field's own tracing benchmark excluded diameter altogether, as too subjective at the resolutions used for whole-arbor reconstruction. And three experts tracing one dendrite agreed on {{iou}} of it by area, while one person re-tracing after a fortnight agreed with themselves on 87.5%. There is no single true morphology to draw, and this page's widths inherit all of that.

Every count here is a snapshot. The census was taken on {{retrieved}}, and the archive grows continuously: it passed {{n_total}} reconstructions that day, and the figure on the day you read this is larger. Re-running fetch_data.py takes a fresh census and every number on the page moves with it.

The sample of files is drawn from {{sample_archives}} archives with a fixed seed, taking up to a fixed number of cells from each archive's first page of records. It is not a uniform random sample of the archive, and archives differ in what they contain, so the medians here describe the sample rather than the whole.

This page does not claim that textbook diagrams have been audited for accuracy. No such study was found. What is shown instead is arithmetic: the range a true-proportion drawing must span, against the range a figure can render.

Nor is the contrast between the two cells in the second figure a measurement of how much axon slicing removes. They are different cells from different animals. The published figure for that is {{truncation}}, and it covers local axon only; a long-range projection is lost entirely.

Depth is the one thing no figure here uses. Reconstructions made in slices are distributed without a shrinkage correction, and tissue prepared this way loses roughly half its thickness — {{zshrink}} in the preparation these files come from. The archive that supplies the widths handled this by leaving depth-derived measurements out of its own analysis rather than by correcting the coordinates. So every drawing on this page is a flat projection, and the code that reads them returns two columns rather than three. The cell drawn from the complete set is also shown as the archive standardises it, with the soma at the origin and the axes rotated onto the principal components of the coordinates, which is not the orientation of the tissue; the depositor's own file is not distributed for that archive.

The code

Two public sources, no key: the NeuroMorpho.Org API and the Allen Institute API. fetch_data.py takes the census and the sample and records a hash of each; swclib.py parses the reconstructions and holds the rule about depth; build_neuron.py computes the payload and builds the interactive; fig_neuron.py draws the five figures; update_page.py writes this page from the payload; test_neuron.py checks the failure modes, including the parser against a fixture whose every quantity was worked out by hand. The archive holds the scripts, the frozen census, the measurements of the {{n_complete}} files that were opened, and the three reconstructions the figures draw; it does not hold the fifteen hundred files the sample was computed from, which the fetch script will pull again.
