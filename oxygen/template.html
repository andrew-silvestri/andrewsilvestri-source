How a 1990 smog rule tainted wells and cleared the way for corn ethanol

In 1990 Congress required the gasoline sold in America's smoggiest cities to contain oxygen. The rule's sponsors hoped it would help corn ethanol. Refiners chose MTBE instead, a chemical that leaked from underground tanks into groundwater and shut half of Santa Monica's water supply. States banned or limited it, and in 2005 Congress traded the oxygen rule for a mandate for renewable fuel. Ethanol took MTBE's place by 2006; later laws did much of the rest.

First order: the oxygen arrived as MTBE

The rule set an oxygen content; it did not name a chemical. Two were practical. Ethanol, distilled mostly from corn, carries more oxygen per gallon, but it picks up water in pipelines, so it was usually shipped separately and blended at terminals. MTBE (methyl tertiary-butyl ether), made from methanol and isobutylene, could be shipped through existing pipelines with the gasoline, and its lower volatility made the emission limits easier to meet. EPA said refiners also chose it for economic reasons. In {{y_brp}} the federal panel on oxygenates found MTBE in over {{brp_mtbe}} per cent of reformulated gasoline and ethanol in about {{brp_eth}} per cent. Reformulated gasoline was about a third of U.S. gasoline from 1996 on.

MTBE was not new. Refiners had used it at low levels since 1979, to replace the octane that lead had supplied. The oxygen programmes multiplied its use. EIA put U.S. demand for MTBE at {{mtbe_a}} thousand barrels a day in {{y_a}} and {{mtbe_b}} thousand in {{y_b}}, and MTBE in gasoline at {{mtbe_c}} thousand by {{y_c}}. EIA named the oxygen programmes as the cause. The chart shows refinery and blender use of MTBE by month. In 1993 and early 1994, use is high only in winter, when the carbon monoxide programme applies. From late 1994, when refiners began to make reformulated gasoline, the summer low disappears. The annual average reaches its peak in {{peak_y}}, at {{peak_kbd}} thousand barrels a day, about {{peak_share}} per cent of all U.S. gasoline.

Second order: MTBE in the water

The properties that made MTBE easy to blend made it hard to contain. It dissolves in water much more readily than the other parts of gasoline, holds less to soil and breaks down slowly, so a leak spreads further and lasts longer. Small amounts can give water a turpentine-like taste and smell. The 1999 panel found that the major source of groundwater contamination appeared to be releases from underground gasoline storage systems. In 1996 Santa Monica, California, found MTBE in its Charnock and Arcadia well fields and shut them down; they supplied half of the city's drinking water.

The evidence that the fuel rule, and not only the leaks, put MTBE in the water is a comparison. The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) sampled groundwater, the sources of public water supplies and drinking water from 1993 to 2002. It divided the samples into areas of high and low MTBE use in gasoline. MTBE was detected {{usgs_lo}} to {{usgs_hi}} times as often in high-use areas. In the USGS's words, detection was "{{q_usgs}}". The USGS also states a limit: high-use areas are more densely populated, and population density accounts for part of the difference. Most detections were at low concentrations.

States acted before Congress did. California ordered MTBE out of its gasoline in 1999. EPA's list of state actions gives {{nbans}} states with a ban or a limit by {{y_lastban}}; {{ncomplete}} of them banned MTBE completely, and the rest set limits, mostly at trace levels. EIA described the bans as a response to water contamination. Water suppliers sued the oil companies that made and sold MTBE. Third order: the trade that brought ethanol

The bans came first. California, New York and Connecticut, three large markets for reformulated gasoline, banned MTBE from the start of 2004, and their refiners used ethanol to meet the oxygen rule. On the chart below, ethanol use passes MTBE use in {{cross}}.

In Congress, the clause's farm-state sponsors faced a problem: without the oxygen requirement, ethanol stood to lose much of its market in reformulated gasoline. In 2000 Senator Tom Daschle, a co-author of the 1990 clause, called MTBE in groundwater an "{{q_unanticipated}}". He proposed to let states waive the oxygen rule and to create a national renewable fuels standard. He described the waiver as "{{q_daschle}}". Bills on those lines failed for five years; one failed in 2003 largely over a proposal to shield MTBE makers from liability. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 ended the oxygen requirement for reformulated gasoline and set renewable fuel volumes of {{rfs06}} billion gallons in 2006, increasing to {{rfs12}} billion in 2012. It gave MTBE no shield from liability.

The national requirement ended on {{repeal}}. EIA had reported that refiners intended to remove MTBE that year because of state bans, continuing liability and the larger exposure they expected once the requirement was gone. MTBE use decreased from {{sw_jan}} thousand barrels a day in January 2006 to {{sw_may}} thousand in May. EIA had estimated that about {{eia2006}} thousand barrels a day of additional ethanol was necessary to replace it. The chart shows MTBE and ethanol by month. What would have happened anyway

A chain like this is easy to tell backwards. Four objections deserve weight.

MTBE was already in gasoline. About {{mtbe_a}} thousand barrels a day was in use in {{y_a}}, for octane. The rule more than tripled that amount and put most of it in the cities it covered; it did not introduce the chemical.

The leaks were the path. Without leaking tanks there would have been little contamination, and federal rules required old tanks to be upgraded by the end of 1998. But tanks leaked gasoline before 1990 too. What the rule changed was how much MTBE the gasoline carried.

Ethanol had other sponsors. Ethanol blends have been exempt from part of the federal gasoline tax since 1978, and the 1990 clause itself was sold as a market for ethanol. In 1994 EPA tried to reserve 30 per cent of the oxygen in reformulated gasoline for renewable fuels; a federal appeals court struck the rule down in 1995. In 2006 and 2007 ethanol use was {{act06}} and {{act07}} billion gallons, above the 2005 law's volumes of {{rfs06}} and {{rfs07}} billion, and EPA's full rules for the mandate took effect only in September 2007. So in those years the mandate was not what held ethanol up. The much larger mandate of the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 called for 36 billion gallons of renewable fuel by 2022, up to 15 billion of it from conventional fuels such as corn ethanol, and that law does not mention MTBE. Most U.S. gasoline is now E10, gasoline with 10 per cent ethanol, and the share has stayed near one tenth since {{y_tenth}}. The MTBE episode set the timing and produced the first mandate; it does not explain the whole of today's volume.

The air benefit was small. A National Research Council committee found in 1999 that the oxygenates in reformulated gasoline had "{{q_nrc}}". Later work found that federal gasoline content rules, reformulated gasoline included, did not measurably improve ozone, while California's rules, which targeted the compounds most likely to form ozone, did. Reformulated gasoline also cut benzene and other toxics, and the 2005 law kept those cuts when it removed the oxygen requirement.

Beyond the third order the evidence is contested. More ethanol means more corn. One study of the 2007 expansion of the mandate estimated that it raised corn prices by about 30 per cent and added about 2.1 million hectares of cropland between 2008 and 2016, so that corn ethanol's carbon intensity was no less than gasoline's and likely at least 24 per cent higher. Other biofuel modellers argued in 2022 that the study overestimated those emissions; its authors replied that the critique was unsupported. This page does not take a side.

Method

Data. MTBE use is EIA's refinery and blender net input of MTBE (series MMTRIUS1), in thousand barrels a month, divided by the days in each month. EIA stops reporting the series after {{mtbe_last}}. Ethanol use is EIA's fuel ethanol consumption from the Monthly Energy Review, Table 10.3 (series ENTCPUS), treated the same way. The share of gasoline is ethanol consumption divided by finished motor gasoline product supplied (MGFUPUS2). That total includes the blended ethanol. The corn share is USDA's corn use for fuel alcohol divided by corn production, by marketing year (September to August). The last two years in USDA's file are an estimate and a projection, so the chart stops at {{corn_last}}. The state count uses each state's phaseout date in EPA's 2007 table. A state counts from the end of the year in which its ban took effect.

Checks. The build compares the series with figures that the sources printed. MTBE net input in 1997 must be below EIA's figure of {{mtbe_c}} thousand barrels a day for all MTBE in gasoline, and within 15 per cent of it. It is {{chk_1997}} thousand, because net input does not count MTBE blended elsewhere. EPA's table must give 25 states and seven complete bans. Ethanol must be about one tenth of gasoline in the latest full year. It is {{eth_share}} per cent in {{y_last}}.

Evidence ratings. The ratings are this page's judgement. Strong: a statute, an official record or a study design connects the cause to the effect. Moderate: the effect is real, but other causes share it. Contested: published studies disagree.

Limits

This page traces one chain. It does not model what would have happened without the rule. National totals hide regional detail: California, New York and the Midwest moved at different times. Net input understates MTBE use by about an eighth (12 per cent in 1997). The corn share is gross: about a third of the grain, by weight, returns as distillers' grains for animal feed, and this page does not subtract it. The health effects of MTBE at the levels found in most water are not settled, and this page makes no claim about them.
