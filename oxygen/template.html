A 1990 smog rule tainted wells and cleared the way for corn ethanol

On 4 May 2000 Senator Tom Daschle introduced a bill in the Senate to undo part of his own work. Ten years earlier he had co-written the clause of the 1990 Clean Air Act amendments that required the gasoline of America's smoggiest cities to contain oxygen, a rule its sponsors hoped would help corn ethanol. Refiners met it instead with MTBE, a gasoline additive that leaked from underground tanks into groundwater and shut half of Santa Monica's water supply. Daschle's bill would have let states waive the oxygen rule in return for a national mandate for renewable fuel. It was one of the bills on those lines that failed for five years, until Congress made the trade in 2005 by ending the rule outright. By 2006 ethanol had taken MTBE's place, and after a 2007 law raised the mandate it became about one gallon in ten of U.S. gasoline. But how much of that one gallon in ten does the 1990 rule explain? Its timing and its first mandate; the volume has other causes.

First order: the oxygen arrived as MTBE

The rule set an oxygen content and left the chemical to the refiners, who had two practical choices. Ethanol, distilled mostly from corn, carries more oxygen per gallon, but it picks up water in pipelines, so it was usually shipped separately and blended at terminals. MTBE (methyl tertiary-butyl ether), made from methanol and isobutylene, could travel through existing pipelines with the gasoline, and its lower volatility made the emission limits easier to meet; EPA said refiners also chose it for economic reasons. In {{y_brp}} the federal panel on oxygenates found MTBE in most reformulated gasoline (over {{brp_mtbe}} per cent) and ethanol in little of it (about {{brp_eth}} per cent). Reformulated gasoline was about a third of U.S. gasoline from 1996 on. A rule its sponsors hoped would help corn ethanol had, for the most part, made a market for MTBE.

MTBE was not new: refiners had used it at low levels since 1979, to replace the octane that lead had supplied, and the oxygen programmes multiplied its use. EIA put U.S. demand for MTBE at {{mtbe_a}} thousand barrels a day in {{y_a}} and {{mtbe_b}} thousand in {{y_b}}, and MTBE in gasoline at {{mtbe_c}} thousand by {{y_c}}, more than three times the starting level, and EIA named the oxygen programmes as the cause. The chart below shows refinery and blender use of MTBE by month, and the programmes can be seen arriving in it. In 1993 and early 1994, use is high only in winter, when the carbon monoxide programme applies; from late 1994, when refiners began to make reformulated gasoline, the summer low disappears. The annual average reaches its peak in {{peak_y}}, at {{peak_kbd}} thousand barrels a day, about {{peak_share}} per cent of all U.S. gasoline. That is roughly one gallon in thirty, against one in ten for ethanol today.

Second order: MTBE reached the water

The properties that made MTBE easy to blend made it hard to contain. It dissolves in water much more readily than the other parts of gasoline, clings less to soil and breaks down slowly, so a leak spreads further and lasts longer. Small amounts can give water a turpentine-like taste and smell. The 1999 panel found that the major source of groundwater contamination appeared to be releases from underground gasoline storage systems. Santa Monica, California, learned what that meant in 1996, when it found MTBE in its Charnock and Arcadia well fields. It shut them down, and with them half of the city's drinking water.

But how do we know that the fuel rule, and not only the leaks, put MTBE in the water? The evidence is a comparison. The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) sampled groundwater, the sources of public water supplies and drinking water from 1993 to 2002, and divided the samples into areas of high and low MTBE use in gasoline. MTBE was detected {{usgs_lo}} to {{usgs_hi}} times as often in high-use areas. In the USGS's words, detection was "{{q_usgs}}". The survey also states its own limit: in the groundwater samples, high-use areas were more densely populated, and the USGS judged that population density probably accounts for part of the difference; most detections were at low concentrations. Still, part of a difference is not the whole of it.

States moved before Congress did, beginning with California, which ordered MTBE out of its gasoline in 1999. EPA's list of state actions gives {{nbans}} states with a ban or a limit by {{y_lastban}} — half the country — and {{ncomplete}} of them banned MTBE completely, while the rest set limits, mostly at trace levels. EIA described the bans as a response to water contamination. The chart below counts the states with a ban or a limit in effect at the end of each year. Water suppliers sued the oil companies that made and sold MTBE.

Third order: the trade that brought ethanol

The bans came first, and they moved the market before any federal law did. California and New York, the two largest states to ban MTBE, and Connecticut banned it at the end of 2003, and the gasoline sold there moved to ethanol to meet the oxygen rule. On the chart below, ethanol use passes MTBE use in {{cross}} and stays above it.

In Congress, the clause's farm-state sponsors faced a problem: without the oxygen requirement, ethanol stood to lose much of its market in reformulated gasoline. Daschle's answer, in the bill of May 2000, was a trade. Introducing it, he called MTBE in groundwater an "{{q_unanticipated}}" and proposed to let states waive the oxygen rule in return for a national renewable fuels standard. He described the waiver as "{{q_daschle}}". Bills on those lines failed for five years; one failed in 2003 largely over a proposal to shield MTBE makers from liability. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 ended the oxygen requirement for reformulated gasoline and set renewable fuel volumes of {{rfs06}} billion gallons in 2006, increasing to {{rfs12}} billion in 2012. It gave MTBE no shield from liability. Fifteen years after requiring oxygen in the hope of selling ethanol, Congress dropped the oxygen and required the ethanol.

The national requirement ended on {{repeal}}, but refiners did not wait for the date. EIA had reported that they intended to remove MTBE that year because of state bans, continuing liability and the larger exposure they expected once the requirement was gone, and it had estimated that about {{eia2006}} thousand barrels a day of additional ethanol might be needed to replace the MTBE then used in reformulated gasoline. MTBE use decreased from {{sw_jan}} thousand barrels a day in January 2006 to {{sw_may}} thousand in May. In four months nearly all of what remained was gone. What would have happened anyway?

A chain like this is easy to tell backwards, starting from the result and finding every link inevitable. Four objections deserve weight.

MTBE was already in gasoline. About {{mtbe_a}} thousand barrels a day was in use in {{y_a}}, mostly for octane. The rule did not introduce the chemical; it more than tripled the amount and put most of it in the cities it covered.

The leaks were the path. Without leaking tanks there would have been little contamination, and federal rules required old tanks to be upgraded, replaced or closed by the end of 1998. But tanks leaked gasoline before 1990 too. What the rule changed was how much MTBE that gasoline carried.

Ethanol had other sponsors. A 1978 law exempted ethanol blends from the federal gasoline tax, and the 1990 clause itself was sold as a market for ethanol. In 1994 EPA tried to reserve 30 per cent of the oxygen in reformulated gasoline for renewable fuels; a federal appeals court struck the rule down in 1995. Was it the 2005 mandate that lifted ethanol? Not at first. In 2006 and 2007 ethanol use ({{act06}} and {{act07}} billion gallons) ran above the 2005 law's volumes of {{rfs06}} and {{rfs07}} billion, and EPA's full rules for the mandate took effect only in September 2007. So in those years the mandate was not what held ethanol up.

The mandate that did came two years later. The Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 called for 36 billion gallons of renewable fuel by 2022 (nearly five times the 2005 law's goal for 2012), up to 15 billion of it from conventional fuels such as corn ethanol, and that law does not mention MTBE. Most U.S. gasoline is now E10, gasoline with 10 per cent ethanol, and in EIA's monthly data ethanol's share of gasoline has stayed near one tenth since {{y_tenth}}. The MTBE episode set the timing and produced the first mandate. It does not explain the whole of today's volume.

The air benefit was small. A National Research Council committee found in 1999 that the oxygenates in reformulated gasoline had "{{q_nrc}}" — the air quality the rule was written to improve. Later work found that federal gasoline content rules, reformulated gasoline included, did not measurably improve ozone, while California's rules, which targeted the compounds most likely to form ozone, did. Reformulated gasoline also cut benzene and other toxics, and the 2005 law kept those cuts when it removed the oxygen requirement.

Beyond the third order the evidence is contested, because more ethanol means more corn. One study of the 2007 expansion of the mandate estimated that it raised corn prices by about 30 per cent and added about 2.1 million hectares of cropland between 2008 and 2016, so that corn ethanol's carbon intensity was no less than gasoline's and likely at least 24 per cent higher. Other researchers argued in 2022 that the study overestimated those emissions; its authors replied that the critique was unsupported. This page does not take a side. A rule written about smog in 1990 had become, by 2022, an argument about cropland.

Method

Data. MTBE use is EIA's refinery and blender net input of MTBE (series MMTRIUS1), in thousand barrels a month, divided by the days in each month; EIA stops reporting the series after {{mtbe_last}}. Ethanol use is EIA's fuel ethanol consumption from the Monthly Energy Review, Table 10.3 (series ENTCPUS), treated the same way. The share of gasoline is ethanol consumption divided by finished motor gasoline product supplied (MGFUPUS2), a total that includes the blended ethanol. The corn share is USDA's corn use for fuel alcohol divided by corn production, by marketing year (September to August). The last two years in USDA's file are an estimate and a projection, so the chart stops at {{corn_last}}. The state count uses each state's phaseout date in EPA's 2007 table, and a state counts from the end of the year in which its ban took effect.

Checks. The build compares the series with figures that the sources printed. MTBE net input in 1997 must be below EIA's figure of {{mtbe_c}} thousand barrels a day for all MTBE in gasoline, and within 15 per cent of it. It is {{chk_1997}} thousand, short because net input does not count MTBE blended elsewhere. EPA's table must give 25 states and seven complete bans. Ethanol must be about one tenth of gasoline in the latest full year; it is {{eth_share}} per cent in {{y_last}}.

Evidence ratings. The ratings are this page's judgement. Strong: a statute, an official record or a study design connects the cause to the effect. Moderate: the effect is real, but other causes share it. Contested: published studies disagree.

Limits

This page traces one chain and does not model what would have happened without the rule. National totals hide regional detail: California, New York and the Midwest moved at different times. Net input understates MTBE use by about an eighth (12 per cent in 1997). The corn share is gross: about a third of the grain, by weight, returns as distillers' grains for animal feed, and this page does not subtract it. The health effects of MTBE at the levels found in most water are not settled, and this page makes no claim about them.
