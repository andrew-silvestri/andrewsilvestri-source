Three in four jobless Americans get no unemployment check

Unemployment insurance is the part of the American safety net built for the moment a job ends. In an average week of {{last}}, about {{us_last}} of every 100 unemployed people were claiming it. In the 1950s the figure was about {{us_50s}}. Where a worker lives now matters a great deal: in {{lo_name}} the figure was {{lo_val}}, in {{hi_name}} {{hi_val}}. Most of the people it misses never apply, and many of those would not qualify under their state’s rules.

Seventy-five years of one ratio

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) calls the measure the recipiency rate. It divides the number of people claiming regular state unemployment insurance in an average week by the number of unemployed people that the government’s household survey counts. The chart shows it for the country and for {{lo_name}}, the lowest state in {{last}}.

Data: DOL Unemployment Insurance Chartbook, charts A.12 and A.13, {{us_first}} to {{prev}}; {{last}} computed by this page from DOL’s monthly state claims reports and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) unemployment counts (see Method). Regular state programs only.

A falling ratio can have three causes. Fewer of the unemployed may qualify, fewer of those who qualify may apply, or more of those who apply may be turned down. Each is real, and none tells the whole story.

Fifty-one programs, not one

Unemployment insurance is a federal-state system. Washington sets broad rules and collects a small payroll tax; each state and the District of Columbia decides who qualifies, how much a claimant receives each week and for how many weeks. Most states pay up to 26 weeks. As of spring 2025, by the count of a Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis study, Florida, Kentucky and North Carolina paid at most 12, and eight more states paid fewer than 26.

{{strip}}

Most of the low states are in the South. North Carolina made the largest cut. In July 2013, with its trust fund close to insolvency, it reduced the top weekly benefit from ${{nc_wba0}} to ${{nc_wba1}} and the most weeks from 26 to 20, now as few as 12. Among its short-term unemployed, the share receiving any benefit fell from {{nc_from}} per cent to {{nc_to}} per cent in the three years after the change, while the share in comparable states held steady. The economists who measured this, Gordon Dahl and Matthew Knepper, describe the drop as partly mechanical, because benefits ended sooner, and possibly partly people leaving the program earlier as payments shrank.

Not who is jobless, but who claims

The program is meant for people who lose a job through no fault of their own and who earned enough before. People who quit, or who are looking for work for the first time or after time away, usually do not qualify. If more of the unemployed were in those groups today, the ratio would fall with no change in the program. That is not what the survey shows.

{{comp}}

Asking the unemployed directly gives the same picture from the other side. In a BLS survey in 2022, {{bls_applied}} per cent of unemployed people who had worked in the past year had applied for benefits, and {{bls_received}} per cent of those who applied received them. Of those who did not apply, {{bls_inelig}} per cent said they believed they were not eligible. The Minneapolis Fed study estimated that {{cn_elig}} per cent of the unemployed met the non-financial rules in 2024. By its estimate, eligibility fell {{cn_drop}} points from 2000 while the recipiency rate fell more than 9; shorter maximum durations accounted for {{cn_weeks}} points of the fall in eligibility and changes in who was unemployed for {{cn_mix}}.

The case for a narrow program

There are serious arguments on the other side. The best established is that benefits lengthen joblessness. In Austria, where a rule gives longer benefits to workers with longer work histories, extending benefits from 20 to 30 weeks lowered the rate at which people found jobs in their first 20 weeks of search by {{ccw}} per cent. Whether that adds up to a large effect across a whole economy is disputed. One group of economists credited the end of the long federal extensions in December 2013 with {{hmm}} million people finding work in 2014; another study, using a different method, found that the extensions of the Great Recession raised the unemployment rate by at most {{crck}} points. Dahl and Knepper also found that employment grew {{dk_emp}} per cent faster in the states that cut benefits, though starting salaries there were {{dk_sal}} per cent lower.

Cost is the second argument. Employers pay for the program through payroll taxes, and the state funds that hold the money are thin: on 1 January 2026, {{solv_now}} states met DOL’s recommended solvency standard, against {{solv_2020}} at the start of 2020. A state that pays fewer claims can keep employer taxes lower. The third is measurement. The ratio sets an administrative count against a survey estimate, and it is not a take-up rate: its denominator includes many people the program was never designed to pay.

Against that, the money does what it is meant to do for those who receive it. Jonathan Gruber found that consumption fell by an average of {{gruber}} per cent for workers who became unemployed, and that without unemployment insurance the fall would have been over three times as large. Peter Ganong and Pascal Noel found that spending drops sharply when benefits run out.

Against other rich countries

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) models what a benefit system pays a single person without children who earned two thirds of the average wage. In {{oecd_year}}, in the first month out of work, U.S. unemployment insurance replaced {{us_m1}} per cent of that person’s previous net income, against an OECD average of {{oecd_m1}} per cent with all benefits counted. After a year, U.S. unemployment insurance paid nothing; across the OECD it still replaced {{oecd_m12_ui}} per cent on average, and {{oecd_m12_all}} per cent with social assistance and housing benefits, against {{us_m12_all}} per cent in the United States. These are rules applied to a model household, not payments to real people, and they assume the person claims.

Method

The measure. The recipiency rate is DOL’s measure. The numerator is the number of continued weeks claimed in regular state unemployment insurance programs in a year, divided by 52. A continued week claimed is a week for which a person asks for a payment after the first. The denominator is the annual average number of unemployed people: the BLS Current Population Survey for the nation, and the BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics for a state. The numerator does not include Extended Benefits, the federal pandemic programs, or the programs for federal employees and former service members.

The years. The national figures for {{us_first}} to {{prev}} and the state figures for 1976 to {{prev}} are DOL’s, from its Unemployment Insurance Chartbook. The build computes the same measure from the raw files: the continued weeks claimed in DOL’s monthly ETA 5159 reports and BLS’s monthly unemployment counts. It agrees with DOL to within {{tol}} points for every year from {{repro_us0}} to {{repro_us1}} for the nation, and for every state from {{repro_st0}} to {{repro_st1}}. The build stops if it does not.

{{last}}. BLS did not collect its household survey for October 2025, during the lapse in federal appropriations. DOL’s published {{last}} figures agree with the raw files only if October counts as zero unemployed people in a twelve-month average. This makes each of DOL’s {{last}} figures 12/11 of the average of the months that BLS published. This page uses the average of the {{n_months}} published months, as BLS does for its own annual average. The national figure is thus {{us_last1}}, not DOL’s {{us_dol}}. The build checks every state’s DOL figure against this explanation.

Other figures. Job losers per 100 unemployed people and claimants per 100 job losers use the BLS survey’s count of the unemployed by reason, not seasonally adjusted, as annual averages. The share of residents uses the Census Bureau’s population estimates for July {{last}}, for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The replacement rates are OECD’s, for a single person without children at 67 per cent of the average wage. The survey results on applications, the eligibility estimates and the research results are as their authors published them.

Limits

The two halves of the ratio come from different sources. Claims are administrative counts by state of the claim; the unemployed are a survey estimate by state of residence. A claimant who works part time and claims a partial week is counted as employed by the survey. In 2020, five states and the District of Columbia recorded more claimants than unemployed people, for these reasons. The ratio counts weeks claimed, not weeks paid. It also leaves out the federal programs, which in 2020 and 2021 paid many people that regular state programs did not, so it understates how many jobless people received some payment in those years. DOL computed its state figures before 2020 with BLS state counts that BLS has since revised; with the current counts, the 2016 to 2019 figures differ by up to about 1.3 points. Claimants per 100 job losers is a ratio of two counts, not a share: some claimants are not job losers in the survey’s terms. Finally, the ratio describes how far the program reaches. It does not say how far it should.
