Three in four jobless Americans are not claiming unemployment insurance

Unemployment insurance is the part of the American safety net built for the moment a job ends. In an average week of {{last}}, about {{us_last}} of every 100 unemployed people claimed it. In the 1950s the figure was about {{us_50s}}. Where a worker lives matters: in {{lo_name}} the figure was {{lo_val}}; in {{hi_name}}, {{hi_val}}. Most of the unemployed who do not claim never apply, and many of those believe they would not qualify under their state’s rules.

Seventy-five years of one ratio

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) calls this ratio the recipiency rate. It divides the number of people who claim regular state unemployment insurance in an average week by the number of unemployed people that the government’s household survey counts.

A falling ratio can have three causes. Fewer of the unemployed may qualify, fewer of those who qualify may apply, or states may turn down more of those who apply. The sections below give evidence on the first two.

Fifty-one programs, not one

Unemployment insurance is a federal-state system. The federal government sets broad rules and collects a payroll tax; each state and the District of Columbia decides who qualifies, how much a claimant receives each week and for how many weeks. Most states pay up to 26 weeks. A count in a Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis study found three states, Florida and North Carolina among them, paying at most 12 weeks in spring 2025, and eight more paying fewer than 26.

{{cuts}}

North Carolina’s cut was, in the words of the economists Gordon Dahl and Matthew Knepper, far larger than any earlier rollback in the United States. In July 2013, with its trust fund insolvent, the state reduced the maximum weekly benefit from ${{nc_wba0}} to ${{nc_wba1}} and the maximum weeks from 26 to 20. The maximum falls to 12 weeks when the state’s unemployment rate is below {{nc_ur12}} per cent. Among the state’s short-term unemployed, the share receiving any benefit decreased from {{nc_from}} per cent to {{nc_to}} per cent in the three years after the change, while the share in comparable states held steady. Dahl and Knepper describe the decrease as partly mechanical, because benefits ended sooner. They say it may also be partly because people left the program earlier as payments became smaller.

Not who is unemployed, but who claims

The program is for people who lose a job through no fault of their own and who earned enough before they lost it. People who quit, or who look for work for the first time or after time away, usually do not qualify. If more of the unemployed were in those groups than in the past, the ratio would fall with no change in the program. The household survey shows little change of this kind.

{{comp}}

A survey of unemployed people points the same way. In a BLS survey in 2022, {{bls_applied}} per cent of unemployed people who had worked in the past year had applied for benefits, and {{bls_received}} per cent of those who applied received them. Of those who did not apply, {{bls_inelig}} per cent said they believed they were not eligible. The Minneapolis Fed study estimated that {{cn_elig}} per cent of the unemployed met the main rules a survey can check, why the job ended and how long the person was out of work, in 2024. By the study’s estimate, that share decreased by {{cn_drop}} points from 2000, while the recipiency rate decreased by more than 9. Fewer maximum weeks accounted for {{cn_weeks}} points of the decrease in eligibility. Changes in who was unemployed, chiefly a smaller share out of work for 26 weeks or less, accounted for {{cn_mix}}.

What the research finds about the program

Three arguments favour a narrower program. The best established is that longer benefits lengthen unemployment. In Austria, where a rule gives longer benefits to workers with longer work histories, extending benefits from 20 to 30 weeks lowered the rate at which people found jobs in their first 20 weeks of search by {{ccw}} per cent. Whether that adds up to a large effect across a whole economy is disputed. One group of economists credited the end of the federal extensions in December 2013 with {{hmm}} million people finding work in 2014; another study, using a different method, found that the extensions of the Great Recession raised the unemployment rate by at most {{crck}} points. Dahl and Knepper also found that, within the same firm, establishments in the states that cut benefits grew employment {{dk_emp}} per cent faster than those in other states. Their starting salaries were {{dk_sal}} per cent lower.

Cost is the second argument. Unemployment insurance taxes are paid mostly by employers, on the wages they pay, and many state trust funds hold less than DOL recommends: on 1 January 2026, {{solv_now}} states met DOL’s recommended solvency standard, against {{solv_2020}} at the start of 2020. Paying fewer claims lets a state keep those taxes lower. The third argument is about measurement. The ratio sets an administrative count against a survey estimate, and it is not a take-up rate: its denominator includes many people the program was never designed to pay.

Research has also measured what benefits do for those who receive them. Jonathan Gruber found that food spending fell by an average of {{gruber}} per cent for people who became unemployed in the United States from 1968 to 1987, and that without unemployment insurance the fall would have been over three times as large. Peter Ganong and Pascal Noel found that spending drops sharply when benefits run out.

Method

The measure. The recipiency rate is DOL’s measure. The numerator is the number of continued weeks claimed in regular state unemployment insurance programs in a year, divided by 52. A continued week claimed is a week of unemployment for which a person who has already filed a claim asks for benefits, or for credit for the unpaid waiting week, whether or not it is paid. The denominator is the annual average number of unemployed people: the BLS Current Population Survey for the nation, and the BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics for a state. The numerator does not include Extended Benefits, the federal pandemic programs, or the programs for federal employees and former service members.

The years. The national figures for {{us_first}} to {{prev}} and the state figures for 1976 to {{prev}} are DOL’s, from its Unemployment Insurance Chartbook. The build computes the same measure from the raw files: the continued weeks claimed in DOL’s monthly ETA 5159 reports and BLS’s monthly unemployment counts. The result agrees with DOL to within {{tol}} points for every year from {{repro_us0}} to {{repro_us1}} for the nation, and for every state from {{repro_st0}} to {{repro_st1}}. If a figure differs by more than {{tol}} points, the build stops.

{{last}}. BLS did not collect its household survey for October 2025, during the lapse in federal appropriations. DOL’s published {{last}} figures agree with the raw files only if October counts as zero unemployed people in a twelve-month average. Each of DOL’s {{last}} figures is thus 12/11 of the figure computed with the average of the months that BLS published. This page uses the average of the {{n_months}} published months, as BLS does for its own annual average. This page’s national figure is {{us_last1}}. DOL’s figure is {{us_dol}}. The build checks every state’s DOL figure against this explanation.

Other figures. Job losers per 100 unemployed people and claimants per 100 job losers use the household survey’s count of the unemployed by reason, not seasonally adjusted, as annual averages. The share of residents uses the Census Bureau’s population estimates for July {{last}}, for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The survey results on applications, the eligibility estimates and the research results are as their authors published them.

Limits

The numerator and the denominator come from different sources. Claims are administrative counts by the state where the claim is filed; the unemployed are a survey estimate by state of residence. The ratio counts weeks claimed, not weeks paid. The survey counts a claimant who works part time and claims a partial week as employed. In 2020, for these reasons, five states and the District of Columbia recorded more claimants than unemployed people. The ratio also leaves out the federal programs, which in 2020 and 2021 paid many people that regular state programs did not, so it understates how many unemployed people received some payment in those years. DOL’s state figures before 2020 cannot be reproduced exactly from BLS’s current state counts; recomputed, the 2016 to 2019 figures differ from DOL’s by up to about 1.3 points, and the page shows DOL’s. BLS has changed the household survey’s methods over the decades, so a comparison of 1970 with {{last}} is approximate. Claimants per 100 job losers is a ratio of two counts, not a share: some claimants are not job losers in the survey’s terms. Finally, the ratio describes how far the program reaches. It does not say how far it should.
