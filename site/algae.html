Algae fix carbon by the billion tonnes and keep almost none of it

Algae are the largest carbon fixers in the sea, and the sea does nearly half of the photosynthesis on Earth. Fixing carbon is not the same as removing it. Followed tonne by tonne from the sunlit surface to the deep ocean and a century beyond, almost everything algae take up goes back to the air, and the engineered routes that keep the most of it exist so far as models and closed companies.

Data: Global Carbon Budget 2025 (through 2024); ocean retention from Siegel and colleagues (2021); route figures from papers published 1998 to 2026. This page does not update.

  1. Ocean algae fix about 48.5 billion tonnes of carbon a year, 4.6 times what fossil fuels emit, but that flow was in balance before industry and removes almost none of the added carbon.
  2. Of 100 tonnes of carbon fixed by farmed seaweed and sunk in deep water, about 44 tonnes are still out of the air after a century in the central case, against about 3.5 tonnes if the same crop is sunk on a continental shelf.
  3. Algae turned into fuel or feed remove nothing: the carbon goes back to the air, and the energy used leaves a net emission.

How much carbon do algae fix?

Satellite and ship measurements put the ocean's net primary production, the carbon that phytoplankton and seaweeds fix each year beyond what they respire, at about 48.5 billion tonnes, in the estimate of Field and colleagues1 (1998). That is 46 percent of all photosynthesis on Earth, and seaweeds supply only about 1 billion tonnes of it; newer satellite models give a lower total2, 38.8 to 42.1 billion tonnes. Fossil fuels and cement released 10.5 billion tonnes of carbon in 2024, according to the Global Carbon Budget3. So the ocean's algae fix about 4.6 times the fossil flow. On those two numbers alone, the sea looks like the obvious place to put the excess.

But fixation is not removal. Most of that carbon is eaten, respired or decomposed within days to months, in the sunlit top hundred meters, and returns to the water as dissolved CO2. The ocean does take up added carbon: 3.2 billion tonnes a year on average over 2015 to 2024. The IPCC's 2021 assessment attributes that uptake4 mainly to chemistry, CO2 dissolving into seawater and reacting with carbonate, and judges the contribution of biology to it minimal. The biological pump was running long before industry, and it was in balance: the carbon it sent down each year was matched by old carbon coming back up. A flow in balance removes nothing.

Where does the fixed carbon go?

Some of it sinks. Dead cells, fecal pellets and animals that migrate between the surface and the depths carry carbon below a hundred meters, and published estimates of this export range5 from 4 to 10 billion tonnes a year. Far less goes deeper. The RECCAP2 synthesis6 puts the flux past 1,000 meters near 0.65 billion tonnes, about 2 tonnes of every 100 fixed. Burial in sediment, the only exit that lasts for millennia, takes 0.02 to 0.2 billion tonnes a year: less than half a tonne of each 100. Counting carbon that is respired in slowly mixed water, 2 to 5 tonnes of each 100 stay out of contact with the air for a century. That is a gross flow, and the return of older carbon cancels it.

Natural seaweed is no different in kind. Kelp forests shed blades and whole plants, and some drift off the continental shelf. Karen Filbee-Dexter and colleagues7 estimated in 2024 that 1 to 14 tonnes of each 100 fixed by the world's brown-seaweed forests stay out of the air for a century, depending on how deep the carbon goes before it is respired. Those forests are part of the same balanced cycle. Only a change, more forest or a deeper pump, would count against the carbon people add.

The engineered routes try to change the arithmetic. Each sets out to fix extra carbon and send it where the return flow cannot reach it quickly. The funnel below follows 100 tonnes of fixed carbon down each route to the net share still out of the air 100 years later.

Depth decides how long carbon stays

The number that matters most on any route is the depth at which the carbon is released. In 2021 the oceanographer David Siegel8 of the University of California, Santa Barbara, and three colleagues used an ocean circulation model to release CO2 at every grid cell of the ocean and to time its return to the surface. Averaged over the ocean, CO2 released at 208 meters is only 7 percent still out of the air after 100 years. At 530 meters the share is 30 percent; at 1,022 meters, 68 percent; at 2,100 meters, 89 percent.

Line: mean over the ocean's area at each model depth. Band: the 10th to 90th percentile of grid cells. Computed by this page's code from the published model output of Siegel and colleagues (2021), released into the public domain.

How reliable is this? It is a model, not a measurement, but one fitted to the observed spread of radiocarbon and other tracers through the ocean, and its authors give the result cell by cell. This page's code reproduces their printed check: at 208 meters after 50 years, a plain mean over grid cells of 14 percent, against 15 percent in the paper. Location matters as much as depth. Water near the Antarctic Polar Front and in the western North Atlantic returns to the surface sooner than water in the North Pacific or the Indian Ocean. The grade: a reasonable estimate, good to within a few percentage points on average and much less certain for any one site.

What happens to sunk seaweed?

Consider a seaweed farm that grows 100 tonnes of carbon and sinks the crop. First, the seaweed took its carbon from seawater, not from the air, and the water refills that deficit from the atmosphere only partly before it mixes away from the surface. DeAngelo and colleagues used a removal fraction9 of 0.5 in their 2023 cost study; ocean-model runs by Berger and colleagues10 give 0.40 to 0.79. Take 0.55, and the 100 tonnes become 55 tonnes actually drawn from the air. Second, if 0.90 of the crop reaches 1,000 meters, 49.5 tonnes remain. Third, released near 2,100 meters (an assumed depth), 0.89 of that stays for a century. The product is about 44 tonnes, and the published inputs give a range of 22 to 74. Sunk on a continental shelf at 208 meters, the same crop keeps about 3.5 tonnes.

Two effects can cut the figure further. Bach and colleagues showed in 202111, using floating sargassum as a natural analogue, that seaweed takes nutrients that plankton downstream would have used, and that shell-building organisms living on the weed release CO2 as they calcify; together these can remove most of the benefit in some waters. The record so far is thin. Running Tide, a company in Portland, Maine12, sold carbon removal to buyers including Microsoft and Stripe, then sank about 19,000 tonnes of lime-coated wood chips, not seaweed, off Iceland in 2023, and checked the result with its own monitoring. It closed in June 2024. No carbon registry offers a method for crediting sunk seaweed.

The costs are model costs. DeAngelo's study put the best case at $480 a tonne of CO2, $830 where nutrients limit growth, and typical runs at $1,120 to $2,090. The National Academies' 2022 research strategy suggested a cost near $100, before the cost of measuring what is removed.

Iron: many blooms, one deep export

Iron fertilization pulls a different lever. In the Southern Ocean and parts of the Pacific, phytoplankton run short of iron rather than nitrogen or phosphorus, so a few tonnes of dissolved iron can start a bloom across hundreds of square kilometers. Between 1993 and 2009, 13 open-ocean experiments did exactly that, and nearly all of them produced a bloom. Only one, the EIFEX experiment13 of 2004, reported that at least half of the bloom's carbon sank below 1,000 meters, a figure inferred from changes in the water column rather than caught in sediment traps. The LOHAFEX experiment14 of 2009 added 4 tonnes of iron, doubled production, and exported no more carbon than the water around it, because grazing animals ate the bloom.

Models add a further discount. In a 2010 study by Andreas Oschlies15 and colleagues, only about a fifth (0.22) of the extra carbon exported by one year of fertilization was still out of the atmosphere a century later, after the fertilized water starved plankton downstream and part of the carbon outgassed. Applied to the experiments' average export, that leaves about 2 tonnes of each 100 fixed, with a range of 0 to 30. Published cost estimates run from under $50 to over $1,000 a tonne of CO2. Under the London Protocol16, the parties resolved in 2008 that ocean fertilization other than legitimate research should not be allowed. In 2012 a company poured about 100 tonnes of iron sulfate into the sea off Haida Gwaii, Canada, and no independent measurement of what it exported exists.

Ponds: reuse is not removal

Microalgae in ponds and closed reactors are the route most often sold as carbon capture, usually fed with CO2 from a power plant or factory stack. Each tonne of dry algae holds the carbon of about 1.8 tonnes of CO2. But that CO2 came from a fossil source, and when the algae become fuel or animal feed it returns to the air within months. The IPCC's 2022 mitigation report states17 that using captured fossil CO2 in products removes nothing from the atmosphere. And the pond runs on energy: in the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's 2016 design case, mixing18, harvesting, drying and CO2 delivery emit about 0.36 tonnes of CO2 for each tonne fixed. Per 100 tonnes fixed, the fuel route ends near −36 tonnes, a net emission, with a range of −67 to −5.

Burying the algae changes the sign. Brilliant Planet, a London company19, grew algae in seawater ponds in the Moroccan desert and planned to dry and bury them; it entered administration in November 2024 without delivering verified removals. An independent 2026 analysis by Leonard and colleagues20 found that ponds fed only by air cannot grow fast enough to be net negative, but ponds fed with carbon-rich seawater could remove carbon at $181 to $661 a tonne of CO2. The funnel's central figure for burial, 38 tonnes of each 100 (range 8 to 82), assumes that three quarters of the carbon is in the end drawn from the air and that the burial pit holds.

What the evidence supports

The case for algae deserves its strongest form. The National Academies called seaweed cultivation21 a compelling ocean strategy for carbon removal, and the physics behind it is sound: carbon placed deep enough, far from where deep water rises, stays out of the air for centuries. Every engineered route, though, depends on how much carbon the sea later pulls from the air, which cannot yet be measured at sea and is taken from models. The record holds one iron experiment with deep export, one seaweed company that sank wood, and one burial company in administration.

On the published numbers, the best route, sunk seaweed, keeps about 44 tonnes of each 100 it fixes for a century, and even its high case leaves a quarter behind. No engineered route has a verified tonne. Model costs run from under $50 to over $2,090 a tonne of CO2, and the routes that keep the most sit in the middle and upper part of that range; buyers paid $320 a tonne on average across publicly priced carbon-removal orders in 202422, and $646 for direct air capture through Frontier. A claim of algae carbon removal that does not state the depth, the share drawn from the air and the time span is a claim about fixation. Fixing carbon is the easy part; keeping it is the whole problem.

Method

The page uses tonnes of carbon (t C) for flows and US dollars per tonne of CO2 for costs. One tonne of carbon is 3.67 tonnes of CO2. Each route starts with 100 t C fixed. The script then applies published fractions in sequence. The fractions are in params.py, each with its source.

Natural routes: the deep figure is the flux at 1,000 meters divided by net primary production. The 100-year figure is gross. The net figure is zero, because the natural cycle was in balance before industry (IPCC 2021, Cross-Chapter Box 5.3).

Sunk seaweed: net = 100 × share drawn from the air × share reaching 1,000 meters × share retained for 100 years at the release depth. The retained share comes from the Siegel and colleagues (2021) model output. The script reads only the needed rows of the public file, with HTTP range requests, and averages each depth level by grid-cell area. The high case assumes 0.95 retained below 3,000 meters.

Iron: net = extra export × the model ratio of air uptake to export at 100 years (Oschlies and colleagues, 2010), less 3 to 9 percent for nitrous oxide. Pond burial: net = 100 × share harvested × share drawn from the air × share kept in the pit − energy and materials (Leonard and colleagues, 2026). Pond fuel: net = −100 × energy emissions per tonne fixed.

The build checks two printed figures before it writes: the Global Carbon Budget ocean sink for 2015 to 2024 (3.2 billion tonnes a year against 3.2 printed) and the Siegel 208-meter check above.

Limits

Low, central and high values are the spread of published estimates. They are not statistical confidence intervals. The central depth for sunk seaweed (2,100 meters) is a choice. The air-drawn share for pond burial is borrowed from seaweed studies. The iron central case is an order of magnitude only. All costs are model estimates with no stated dollar year. Running Tide's and Brilliant Planet's figures are company statements or public register entries; none was independently verified.
