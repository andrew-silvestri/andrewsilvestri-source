What utilities report as lost gas

What U.S. gas distribution operators report each year as lost and unaccounted for, how that figure is reported, and how much of its year-to-year spread measurement alone could produce.

Every year each U.S. gas distribution operator tells the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration what share of its gas it cannot account for: the gas it bought or produced, less the gas its customers and the company used and appropriate adjustments, as a percentage of that use1, over the twelve months to June 30. The figure is lost and unaccounted-for gas, and PHMSA's own report to Congress on it says what it is2: "LAUF gas is a combination of measurement inaccuracy and unknown leaks, but it is impossible to know the portion attributable to each."

This page reads 22,723 of those reports, 2010 to 2025: natural gas systems from 2015, every system before, when the reports do not say which gas a system carries, and asks two things: how the figure is reported, and how much of its movement from one year to the next measurement alone could produce on a system that loses nothing. It follows What a meter's error is worth, which asks the same question of a pipeline's meters.

What it finds

Close to a third of reports are zero or below, every year. From 2010 to 2025, between 28.7 and 33.2 per cent of reports were at or below zero. Until 2017 the form did not allow a negative figure, and PHMSA's report to Congress said so: "PHMSA instructs companies that calculate a negative value for percent of LAUF gas to report zero." From the 2018 reports it does, and PHMSA changed the denominator from the gas taken in to the gas consumed at the same time, to match the Energy Information Administration3. The share at or below zero did not change when the rule did. Exact zeros fell from 29.6 to 15.1 per cent of reports while negatives rose from none to 17.1, and of the 91 negative reports in 2018, 34 came from systems that had reported exactly zero the year before. A negative figure cannot be gas physically lost: more gas was accounted for going out than coming in.

The rest of the figure is heaped and repeated. Among non-zero reports, 24.3 to 31.7 per cent are whole numbers, and 18.7 to 34.6 per cent of systems report exactly the figure they reported the year before. So the spread below is shown twice: on every report, and on the reports that are not zero, not a whole number and not a repeat of the year before.

The middle half of systems reported between 0 and 3.10 per cent in 2025, and the median was 1. On reports that are not zero, whole or repeated, the middle half is 0.05 to 3.23 per cent and the median 1.20. In Texas, 117 reports, the middle half ran from 0 to 7 per cent; in Oklahoma, 66 reports, from 0.38 to 8.70.

No state total is shown. A report carries a percentage and no volume, so a state figure has to weight systems by something, and by services the largest operator in Oklahoma holds 81 per cent of the state's: its figure would be the state's. The distribution of systems needs no weight, and it describes systems, not gas.

A system's figure tends to stay where it was. Between one year and the next the rank correlation runs from 0.56 to 0.72. From 2024 to 2025, the middle half of systems moved between −0.7 and +1.0 points; one system in ten moved up by more than 4.2, and one in ten down by more than 3.6. Many of those changes are exact repeats, a change of zero; on the 690 systems whose figure was not zero, whole or repeated in either year, the middle half moved −0.7 to +1.2, with one in ten up by more than 3.8 and one in ten down by more than 2.8.

Measurement errors of the kind modelled here, normal and symmetric, can move the figure as much as that in the middle, but not in the tails. The model behind this page takes a system that loses nothing, measures what comes in at a city-gate station and what goes out through 2,000 customer meters, and draws each error as a part that stays with the system and a part redrawn each year: the station, the customer meters' shared drift and their own scatter, the meters that do not correct for temperature, and the billing periods that do not line up with the reporting year. At its default assumptions, the middle half of systems moves between −0.6 and +0.6 points from one year to the next, against −0.7 to +1.2 reported on the systems above. With every uncertainty scaled by 1.5, the scale whose middle half is closest to the reported one, the middle half is −1.0 to +1.0, but one in ten moves up by more than 1.8, against 3.8 reported. Double every uncertainty and the tails reach −2.4 and +2.4 while the middle half widens to −1.3 to +1.3; triple them and the tails reach −3.6 and +3.7, with a middle half of −1.9 to +1.9. No single scale of these errors gives both the middle and the tails reported. A system can have errors of another shape - a station meter that fails for part of a year, a regulator off its set point, a wrong correction factor - and errors like those could produce long tails; the model does not include them, so this is a statement about the errors modelled, not about measurement in general.

That leaves the tails to something the model does not contain, and this page does not say what. PHMSA's report puts it plainly: "LAUF gas is not a valid proxy for either unknown leak volume or methane emissions." It also lists what the measurement itself does to the figure: "Volume meters have a tendency to drift away from accurate measurement over time."

The two states this series looks at treat the figure differently. Texas presumes that all of it is gas actually lost unless a utility shows otherwise in a rate case, and for ratemaking disallows the expense of lost gas4 above 5.0 per cent of the gas metered in for a distribution system, unless the Commission allows more on the facts of a case; on PHMSA's basis, gas consumed, that is 5.26 per cent. It is a limit on what a rate can recover, not on what a utility may report. Oklahoma's rule requires gas to be measured5 "in order that the ‘lost and unaccounted for’ gas in distribution may be accurately determined", and sets no percentage.

Method

The reports. PHMSA's gas distribution annual reports, Form 7100.1-1, Part G, field PERCENT_UNACC_GAS, from the bulk file of 2010 to 2025 reports. The form defines the figure as "Unaccounted for gas as a percent of total consumption" and the reports from 2018 on divide by consumption. Each report covers one operator in one state, by the state of operation, not the office address. From 2015 the reports say which gas a system carries, and only natural gas systems are kept (763 propane, landfill, synthetic and other reports are left out); before 2015 every report is kept.

Reports that are not zero, whole or repeated. Reports that are not exactly zero, not a whole number, and not identical to the same system's report the year before. The rule is mechanical; a zero, a whole number or a repeat can each be a real figure. It is used to show how much the reporting pattern shapes the distribution, not to correct it or to judge any report. Percentiles are the nearest reported value at or below the rank (numpy's "lower" method), so every figure printed is a figure someone reported.

Persistence. Systems are matched across consecutive years by operator and state. The rank correlation uses average ranks for ties, because the figures are heaped.

Measurement alone. A system with no loss: receipts measured at a city-gate station, deliveries through 2,000 customer meters, reported figure (in − out) / out. Each error is a relative error on a volume, normal, split into a part that persists from year to year and a part redrawn each year. The defaults, every one an assumption: city-gate standard uncertainty 0.5 per cent, half of it persistent; the fleet's shared error 0.5 per cent between systems and its drift 0.2 per cent a year; each meter's own error 1.0 per cent, which averages away over many meters; 70 per cent of meters without temperature correction, at a mean gas temperature of 55 °F that varies by 1.5 °F from year to year; billing-period mismatch 0.5 per cent. An uncorrected meter reads actual volume and bills it as if at 60 °F, so it misstates standard volume by the ratio of absolute temperatures. The sweep scales every uncertainty together; 200,000 systems are drawn for each setting.

Beside EIA. EIA's Natural Gas Annual6 Table A1 gives losses and unaccounted for by state, over the whole state's system and calendar year, from a different form. It is not the same quantity as PHMSA's, and the page does not set one against the other.

Limits

The reports are the operators' own figures, filed on a form; nothing here checks one. The model's inputs are assumptions with defaults, not measurements of any system; its result depends on them, which is why the simulator lets each be moved and why the page reports a range of scales rather than one. The errors are drawn as normal and symmetric; a real meter fleet can be skewed, and the model has nothing for theft, unmetered use, estimated reads, accounting adjustments or leaks, all of which can be in a reported figure. Pressure correction is left out, as are the two-year effects of a billing lag. No operator is named on this page or in its figures; the downloadable table carries PHMSA's operator names as PHMSA publishes them.
