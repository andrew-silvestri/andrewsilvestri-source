The night shift breaks sleep into pieces and takes the evening

Picture an American street at three in the morning and count the dark windows. Nearly all of them are dark: in the government's time diaries for 2025, 95 percent of people aged 15 and over were asleep at that hour. The lit ones belong partly to the 5.1 million wage and salary workers, 3.4 percent of the total, who usually work a night shift. The common belief is that the shift steals their sleep. The diaries say something more specific. On workdays, night workers report about as much time asleep as day workers do. They get it in pieces, at hours when most of the country is awake, and the hours they give up are the evening ones that families and friends share.

  2. In the government's time diaries, night workers on workdays report about as much sleep as day workers, but more often in pieces and at hours when about two in three people are awake.
  3. Years of night work are associated with more heart disease and diabetes, the cancer evidence is contested, and how much of the risk the shift itself causes is not settled.

Who works at night

Night work is concentrated in jobs that cannot stop or that run cheaper when roads and power lines are quiet. In BLS's 2024–25 tables, 3.4 percent of wage and salary workers usually worked a regular night shift1, and the share reached 11.7 percent in transportation and material moving and 7.9 percent in production occupations. Hospital nurses are in the count too, but they sit inside the broad professional group, where the share is diluted. About 61 percent of night workers are men. That is roughly three in five, against a little over half of all wage and salary workers.

Why do they do it? The survey asked each night worker for the main reason, and the answer most often given was not pay. It was personal preference, at 30.7 percent, ahead of the nature of the job (19.8 percent) and better pay (17.5 percent). The split by sex is wide. Nearly half of women on nights (47.6 percent) gave preference as the reason, against about one in five men (20.0 percent), while pay was named by 22.6 percent of men and 9.4 percent of women. Better arrangements for family or child care, the tag-team schedule in which parents hand children over at the door, accounted for 16.5 percent. The night shift is, for a large minority, a choice.

How do we know what a night worker's day looks like?

The numbers in the clock come from the American Time Use Survey, which the Census Bureau runs for BLS2. Each respondent recounts one randomly chosen day, activity by activity, from 4 a.m. to 4 a.m., with who was present. In 2024 and 2025 the same respondents also answered the Leave and Job Flexibilities Module, which asked which shift they usually work. Joining the two gives what neither publishes: the diary days of 190 usual night workers, 122 of them on a day they worked. The build reproduces BLS's own figures before it uses the files: the 3.4 percent share of night workers, the reasons above, and the share of the country asleep at 3 a.m.3 Standard errors come from the survey's 160 replicate weights.

That makes the country's clock well measured and the night workers' clock a small sample, and the table below gives the uncertainty. A second caution concerns the word asleep. The diaries record what people say they were doing, and time in bed trying to sleep is counted as sleep. Measured sleep is shorter. A national health survey found that 44.0 percent of night workers usually slept six hours or less4, against 28.8 percent of day workers. Wrist-worn monitors on Danish night warehouse workers5 recorded 5 hours 39 minutes of sleep after night shifts and 7 hours 24 minutes on recovery days. So the diaries are best read for when people sleep and how the sleep is broken, not for its exact length.

Read that way, the workday difference in reported sleep is small: night workers report 21 minutes more than day workers, with a standard error of 16, which is no difference the sample can detect. The difference in shape is large. Night workers' longest sleep is about an hour shorter, and they are about four times as likely to sleep in two or more blocks, typically a main sleep after the shift and a nap before the next one.

The body clock rarely follows

The timing matters because the body keeps its own schedule. In 170 day-active adults, salivary melatonin, the hormone that marks the biological night, rose at 20:50 on average6 and fell at 08:21. Core body temperature reaches its daily low7 around 05:00, close to the middle of a normal night's sleep, and the early-morning hours from about 02:00 to 07:00 are when sleepiness peaks and attention fails most8. A night worker is at work through that low point and in bed during the hours the body treats as day, which is why day sleep is shorter and lighter.

Does the clock move to fit the job? Rarely, and not fully. A 2008 review of six studies9 of permanent night workers found that fewer than 3 percent adjusted their melatonin rhythm completely and fewer than one in four adjusted enough to benefit. A 2013 study of police officers10 working seven consecutive nights found that 7 of 17 adapted while the rest barely moved. Where workers are cut off from daylight, as on North Sea oil platforms11, the clock does shift. For most night workers, the drive home in morning sunlight resets it. In a laboratory, ten adults12 whose meals and sleep were moved against their clocks showed higher blood glucose despite more insulin, 17 percent lower leptin, a hormone that signals fullness, and in 3 of 8 people a post-meal glucose response in the prediabetic range. The experiment was small and lasted ten days. It shows a mechanism, not a disease.

What years of nights are associated with

The long-term evidence comes mostly from large cohorts of nurses who reported how many years they had worked rotating night shifts, at least three nights a month. In the Nurses' Health Study, ten or more years of rotating nights were associated with an 18 percent higher risk of coronary heart disease13. Fifteen or more years were associated with a 23 percent higher risk of death from cardiovascular disease14. Twenty or more years went with a 58 percent higher risk of type 2 diabetes15, falling to 24 percent once body weight was taken into account, which suggests that much of the link runs through weight. A 2012 meta-analysis of 34 studies16 and about two million people found a 23 percent higher risk of heart attack among shift workers, but no higher death rate.

Cancer is the contested case. In 2019 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified night shift work as probably carcinogenic to humans, Group 2A17, on limited evidence in humans and stronger evidence from animals and laboratory systems. But when ten prospective studies of 1.4 million women were pooled in 2016, the relative risk of breast cancer for night work was 0.9918, no excess at all, even after 20 or 30 years. Studies that ask women with and without cancer to recall their working lives find higher risks than studies that follow women forward19 in time, which is the pattern recall bias would produce.

How much of any of this does the shift cause? The studies are observational, and two biases push in opposite directions. Workers who fall ill tend to leave night work20, which hides harm; and people with less education and higher body weight are more likely to end up on shifts in the first place, which a 2021 genetic study found could inflate it21. The fairest reading of the cohort evidence is a modest excess risk, on the order of a fifth, that appears after a decade or more, with cause still unproven.

Accidents and the drive home

The acute risks are clearer. Pooling studies of workplace incidents, Simon Folkard and Emanuele Lombardi found22 risk 27.9 percent higher on night shifts than on morning shifts, 36 percent higher on the fourth consecutive night than on the first, and 27.5 percent higher on 12-hour shifts than on 8-hour ones. The commute home is the other exposure. In a 2016 test on a closed track23, 16 night workers drove an instrumented car twice, once after a night's sleep and once after a night shift. After sleep there were no near-crashes. After the shift, 6 of the 16 drives had at least one, and 7 were stopped early for safety.

Evenings and family

The clock above shows where the social cost falls. Between 18:00 and 22:00, the hours when most people are awake and off work, night workers on workdays are asleep or at work 49 percent of the time, against 18 percent for day workers. Their total time with family or friends on a workday is about the same as day workers'. Days off do not repay the evenings: on them night workers report 111 fewer minutes with family and friends than day workers do on theirs, nearly two hours, with a standard error of 44. They also report 10.8 hours of sleep on days off, against 9.7 for day workers, which looks like recovery.

Studies of marriages find costs in particular groups rather than across the board. In a sample of 3,476 American couples24 followed for about five years, men with children who were married less than five years and worked fixed nights were some six times as likely to separate or divorce as those on days; among women with children married more than five years, the odds tripled. A later study of newlyweds25 found that wives' night shifts raised divorce risk, but among couples without children. A 2014 review found adverse associations26 with children's outcomes in 21 of 23 studies.

What it pays

No federal law requires a private employer27 in the United States to pay more for nights. The federal government pays its own General Schedule employees a 10 percent differential for regularly scheduled work28 between 18:00 and 06:00, and many employers do something similar. In 13 European countries29, workers who receive a shift premium are paid 5 to 16 percent of gross wages for it. What workers say they would need is higher. In a Belgian choice experiment with union-recruited respondents, the median worker required 36 percent more an hour to accept a fixed night shift over a day job. The survey's sample was not representative, but the gap between what is paid and what is asked is wide.

Who it can suit

People differ in when their clocks run, and the evidence suggests the mismatch, not the night as such, does the harm. Among 64,615 nurses30, early risers with ten or more years of shift work had no significant excess of diabetes, while late risers had their highest risk on day schedules. Preference also counts for something: the largest group of night workers chose the shift. A laboratory schedule that combined bright light during the shift, dark glasses on the way home and a dark bedroom moved young volunteers' melatonin onset31 to about 03:20, close to a night worker's needs, but it took discipline that few workplaces enforce. The diaries cannot say whether a night worker sleeps well. They show when, and with whom the evenings are spent. That much the shift decides.

Method

Data. BLS American Time Use Survey public-use files for 2024 and 2025 (respondent, activity, who, summary and roster files) and the Leave and Job Flexibilities Module, 2024–25, with its weights. No diaries were collected in October 2025. The module covers 6,593 employed wage and salary respondents; 190 usually work a night shift and 3,086 usual day workers kept a diary on a day they worked. A workday is a diary day with any time coded as working (activity codes 0501xx). Asleep is activity code 0101xx. Family and friends follow BLS's own definitions; the build reproduces BLS's per-respondent totals from the who-codes exactly. Work time is excluded from time with family or friends.

Clock. The diary day starts at 04:00. A person counts as asleep at a time if a sleep activity starts at or before that time and ends after it. The rings use ten-minute steps. The everyone ring uses the diary weights of all respondents in both years, each year scaled to one average day. The night and day rings use the module weights. The body-clock arc and dot are published averages for day-active adults, not values from these diaries.

Checks. The build stops unless it reproduces, to the published decimal, BLS's share of night workers (3.4 percent), the number of night workers (5,144 thousand), the reasons in Table 8, and Table A-3's shares of people asleep at 03:00 and 07:00 and working at 10:00 in 2025. Standard errors use the 160 replicate weights by successive-difference replication.

Limits

The night sample is 190 people, 122 of them on a workday and 68 on a day off, so the night figures carry standard errors of several points and are averages over shifts that start at different hours. One diary day per person shows a typical day, not a person's week. Reported sleep includes time in bed. The health evidence comes from observational studies, mostly of rotating shifts among nurses, and it cannot separate the shift from the people who take it. This page summarises evidence and is not medical advice.
