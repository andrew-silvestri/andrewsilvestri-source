How a 1990 smog rule ended with corn in America's gasoline

In 1990 Congress told refiners to add oxygen to the gasoline sold in America's smoggiest cities. The rule's sponsors expected it to help ethanol made from corn. Refiners used MTBE, a cheaper chemical made from natural gas and refinery streams, instead. MTBE leaked from underground tanks into groundwater, and 25 states banned it. In 2005 Congress ended the oxygen rule and, in the same law, required a rising volume of renewable fuel. Ethanol replaced MTBE in 2006 and is now about one gallon in ten of U.S. gasoline; about a third of the American corn crop goes to make it. Each link in this chain has its own evidence, and the last one has other causes as well.

  2. MTBE leaked from fuel tanks into groundwater: where gasoline carried much of it, wells and water supplies were 4.9 to 7.5 times as likely to contain it, and 25 states banned or limited it.
  3. When Congress ended the oxygen rule in 2005 and mandated renewable fuel, refiners swapped MTBE for corn ethanol within months; ethanol is now about one gallon in ten of U.S. gasoline, though later mandates and tax credits did much of that work.

The chain, one order at a time

A first-order effect is what a policy does directly. The second order is what that effect does, and the third order follows from the second. Each step below states its claim, the mechanism and how strong the evidence is: strong when official records or a study design tie cause to effect, moderate when other causes share the result, contested when serious researchers disagree.

The policy · 1990

The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 required gasoline in the areas with the worst ozone smog to contain at least 2.0 per cent oxygen by weight from 1995. Winter gasoline in areas with too much carbon monoxide had to contain 2.7 per cent from 19921. Oxygen helps fuel burn more completely. The Senate sponsors' press release said the amendment would2 “significantly increase the market share for grain-based alternative fuels”, such as ethanol.

First order: the oxygen arrived as MTBE

The rule set an oxygen content; it did not name a chemical. Two were practical. Ethanol, distilled mostly from corn, carries more oxygen per gallon, but it picks up water in pipelines, so it had to be trucked to terminals and blended there. MTBE (methyl tertiary-butyl ether) could travel with the gasoline, added octane and cost refiners less. They chose it for most of the new fuel. In 1999 the federal panel on oxygenates3 found MTBE in over 85 per cent of reformulated gasoline and ethanol in about 8 per cent. Reformulated gasoline was about a third of U.S. gasoline from 1996 on4.

MTBE was not new. Refiners used it from the early 1980s to replace the octane that lead had supplied. The rule increased its use: EIA reported that U.S. demand for MTBE went from 83 thousand barrels a day in 1990 to 161 thousand in 1994, and that MTBE in gasoline reached 269 thousand barrels a day by 1997. EIA identified the oxygen programmes as the cause5. The chart shows refinery and blender use of MTBE by month. Use is high each winter in 1993 and 1994, when only the carbon monoxide programme applied. From 1995, when reformulated gasoline started, the summer low disappears: use stays high all year, because reformulated gasoline is sold in every season. It reaches its peak in6 1999, at 260 thousand barrels a day, about 3.1 per cent of all U.S. gasoline.

Evidence: strong. The requirement is in the statute, the increases in the monthly data occur on the programmes' start dates, and EIA attributed the growth in MTBE demand to the programmes.

Second order: MTBE in the water

The properties that made MTBE easy to blend made it hard to contain. It dissolves in water much more readily than the other parts of gasoline, holds less to soil and breaks down slowly, so a leak spreads further and lasts longer. Small amounts give water a taste and smell of turpentine. The 1999 panel found that the major source of groundwater contamination appeared to be leaking underground storage tanks at service stations. Santa Monica, California, closed the wells that supplied about half of its water in 19967.

The evidence that the fuel rule, and not only the leaks, put MTBE in the water is a comparison. The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) sampled groundwater, the sources of public water supplies, and drinking water from 1993 to 2002. It divided the samples into areas of high and low MTBE use in gasoline. MTBE was found 4.9 to 7.5 times as often in high-use areas8. In the USGS's words, detections were "about 5 to 7.5 times higher in areas of relatively high MTBE use". The USGS also notes a limit: high-use areas are more densely populated, and population density accounts for part of the difference. Most detections were at low concentrations.

States acted before Congress did. California ordered MTBE out of its gasoline in 1999. The chart shows how many states had a ban or a limit in effect at the end of each year, from EPA's list of state actions9.

Evidence: strong. The USGS comparison ties detections to use, the panel identified the path, and the state laws name MTBE in water as their reason.

Third order: the trade that brought ethanol

By 2000 the rule's farm-state sponsors had a problem. Without the oxygen requirement, refiners had no reason to blend ethanol into reformulated gasoline; with it, they kept using MTBE where states allowed. Senator Tom Daschle, who wrote the 1990 clause with Senator Bob Dole, called MTBE in groundwater an "unanticipated development" and proposed to let states drop the oxygen rule in exchange for a national renewable fuel standard, which he called10 "a fair tradeoff for the establishment of a renewable fuels standard". Bills on those lines failed for five years11. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 finally ended the oxygen requirement and set renewable fuel volumes of 4.0 billion gallons in 2006, increasing to 7.5 billion in 201212. It gave MTBE makers no protection from lawsuits.

The oxygen rule ended on13 May 5, 2006. EIA had reported that refiners intended to remove MTBE in 200614 because of state bans, existing lawsuits and the larger exposure they expected once the oxygen requirement was gone. MTBE use decreased from 114 thousand barrels a day in January 2006 to 7 thousand in May. EIA had estimated that about 130 thousand barrels a day of additional ethanol would be necessary to replace it. The chart shows the two oxygenates by month. The bars below show the share of each year's corn crop that went to fuel ethanol.

Evidence: strong for the switch, moderate for today's level. The legislative record and EIA's reports tie the 2006 switch to the bans, the lawsuits and the repeal. The growth after 2007 has other causes, described below.

What would have happened anyway

A chain like this is easy to tell backwards. Four objections deserve weight.

MTBE was already in gasoline. About 83 thousand barrels a day was in use in 1990, for octane. The rule more than tripled that amount and put most of it in the cities it covered; it did not introduce the chemical.

The leaks were the path. Without leaking tanks there would have been little contamination, and tank upgrades were due by 1998. But the tanks leaked gasoline before 1990 too. What the rule changed was how much MTBE the gasoline carried.

Ethanol had other sponsors. A federal tax break for ethanol dates from 197815, and the 1990 clause itself was sold as a market for it. In 2006 and 2007 ethanol use was 5.5 and 6.9 billion gallons, above the mandate of 4.0 and 4.7 billion, so in those years the 2005 mandate was not what held ethanol up. High oil prices, the blenders' tax credit and the replacement of MTBE did. The much larger mandate that holds ethanol near one gallon in ten today came in the Energy Independence and Security Act of 200716, argued on energy security, not on MTBE. The MTBE episode set the timing and created the first mandate; it does not explain the whole of today's volume.

The air benefit was small. A National Research Council committee found in 199917 that the oxygenates in reformulated gasoline had "little impact on improving ozone air quality". Later work found that the federal reformulated gasoline programme as a whole did little for ozone, while California's tighter recipe did more18. Reformulated gasoline also cut benzene and other toxics, and that part of the programme stayed after the oxygen rule went.

The fourth order is contested. More ethanol demand means more corn. One study of the renewable fuel mandate estimated19 that it raised corn prices by about 30 per cent and increased U.S. cropland by about 2.1 million hectares between 2008 and 2016, enough that corn ethanol may emit no less carbon than gasoline. Researchers at Argonne National Laboratory and elsewhere dispute20 the land and carbon estimates. This page shows that branch as contested and does not take a side.

Method

Data. MTBE use is EIA's refinery and blender net input of MTBE (series MMTRIUS1), in thousand barrels a month, divided by the days in each month. Ethanol use is EIA's fuel ethanol consumption21 from the Monthly Energy Review, Table 10.3 (series ENTCPUS), treated the same way. The share of gasoline is ethanol consumption divided by finished motor gasoline product supplied (MGFUPUS2), which includes the ethanol blended into it. The corn share is USDA's corn use for fuel alcohol22 divided by corn production, by marketing year (September to August). The latest two years in USDA's file are an estimate and a projection; the chart stops at 2024/25. The state count uses the phaseout date of each state in EPA's 2007 table. A state counts from the end of the year its ban took effect.

Checks. The build compares the series with figures that the sources printed. MTBE net input in 1997 must be below, and within 15 per cent of, EIA's figure of 269 thousand barrels a day for all MTBE in gasoline; it is 236 thousand, because net input does not count MTBE blended elsewhere. EPA's table must give 25 states. Ethanol must be about one tenth of gasoline in the latest full year; it is 10.5 per cent in 2025.

Evidence ratings. The ratings are this page's judgement. Strong means a statute, an official record or a study design ties the cause to the effect. Moderate means the effect is real but other causes share it. Contested means published studies disagree.

Limits

This page traces one chain and does not model what would have happened without the rule. National totals hide regional detail: California, New York and the Midwest moved at different times. Net input understates MTBE use by about a tenth. The share of corn going to ethanol is gross; about a third of the grain returns as distillers' grains23 for animal feed, which this page does not subtract. The health effects of MTBE at the levels found in most water are not settled, and this page makes no claim about them.
