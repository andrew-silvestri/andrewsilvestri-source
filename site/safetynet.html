Three in four jobless Americans get no unemployment check

Unemployment insurance is the part of the American safety net built for the moment a job ends. In an average week of 2025, about 26 of every 100 unemployed people were claiming it. In the 1950s the figure was about 49. Where a worker lives now matters a great deal: in Florida the figure was 8, in Minnesota 46. Most of the people it misses never apply, and many of those would not qualify under their state’s rules.

  1. In an average week of 2025, about 26 of every 100 unemployed Americans were claiming state unemployment insurance; in the 1950s about 49 of every 100 were claiming it.
  2. Job losers, the group the program is built for, have made up close to half of the unemployed since 1970; claimants were about as many as job losers in 1970, and about 55 for every 100 of them in 2025, though the share of job losers had not changed much.
  3. In 21 states, home to 39 per cent of Americans, fewer than one in five unemployed people were claiming; Florida was lowest at 8 in 100 and Minnesota highest at 46, about six times as many.

Seventy-five years of one ratio

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) calls the measure the recipiency rate1. It divides the number of people claiming regular state unemployment insurance in an average week by the number of unemployed people that the government’s household survey counts. The chart shows it for the country and for Florida, the lowest state in 2025.

The 1950s: about half. From 1950 to 1959, an average of 49 of every 100 unemployed people claimed state unemployment insurance in a typical week. The highest year was 1958, at 55.

1980 to 1984: a step down. The figure decreased from 44 in 1980 to 29 in 1984. Rebecca Blank and David Card found2 that fewer of the eligible unemployed claimed after 1980, which by itself took about 6 points off the share.

2007 to 2019: Florida. In 2007, 37 of every 100 unemployed people in Florida claimed. From January 2012 the state paid at most 20 weeks instead of 26, on a scale that falls as unemployment falls. By 2019 the figure was 11.

2020 to 2025: a spike, then back. When millions were laid off at once in 2020, the national figure rose to 78. In 2025 it was 26 nationally and 8 in Florida.

Data: DOL Unemployment Insurance Chartbook, charts A.12 and A.13, 1950 to 2024; 2025 computed by this page from DOL’s monthly state claims reports and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) unemployment counts (see Method). Regular state programs only.

A falling ratio can have three causes. Fewer of the unemployed may qualify, fewer of those who qualify may apply, or more of those who apply may be turned down. Each is real, and none tells the whole story.

Fifty-one programs, not one

Unemployment insurance is a federal-state system. Washington sets broad rules and collects a small payroll tax; each state and the District of Columbia decides who qualifies, how much a claimant receives each week and for how many weeks. Most states pay up to 26 weeks. As of spring 2025, by the count of a Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis study3, Florida, Kentucky and North Carolina paid at most 12, and eight more states paid fewer than 26.

51 programs. Each bar counts the states, with the District of Columbia, whose figure for 2025 falls in a step of 5. The middle state had 21 claimants per 100 unemployed people; the national figure, 26, is higher because large states such as California and New York are above the middle.

Under one in five. In 21 states, fewer than 20 of every 100 unemployed people were claiming. They are home to 39 per cent of the country’s residents.

Six to one. Florida was lowest, at 7.6; Minnesota was highest, at 45.7.

Most of the low states are in the South. North Carolina made the largest cut. In July 2013, with its trust fund close to insolvency, it reduced the top weekly benefit from $535 to $350 and the most weeks from 26 to 20, now as few as 12. Among its short-term unemployed, the share receiving any benefit fell from 30 per cent to 10 per cent in the three years after the change, while the share in comparable states held steady. The economists who measured this, Gordon Dahl and Matthew Knepper4, describe the drop as partly mechanical, because benefits ended sooner, and possibly partly people leaving the program earlier as payments shrank.

Not who is jobless, but who claims

The program is meant for people who lose a job through no fault of their own and who earned enough before. People who quit, or who are looking for work for the first time or after time away, usually do not qualify. If more of the unemployed were in those groups today, the ratio would fall with no change in the program. That is not what the survey shows.

1967 to 1979: close together. Job losers, the group the program is for, were 44 of every 100 unemployed people in 1970. Claimants were 44: about 100 for every 100 job losers.

1980 to 2025: apart. Outside recession years, job losers stayed between 38 and 62 of every 100 unemployed people. Claimants fell. In 2025, job losers were 47 and claimants 26: about 55 for every 100 job losers.

Asking the unemployed directly gives the same picture from the other side. In a BLS survey in 20225, 26 per cent of unemployed people who had worked in the past year had applied for benefits, and 55 per cent of those who applied received them. Of those who did not apply, 55 per cent said they believed they were not eligible. The Minneapolis Fed study estimated that 46.1 per cent of the unemployed met the non-financial rules in 2024. By its estimate, eligibility fell 5.4 points from 2000 while the recipiency rate fell more than 9; shorter maximum durations accounted for 2.6 points of the fall in eligibility and changes in who was unemployed for 2.1.

The case for a narrow program

There are serious arguments on the other side. The best established is that benefits lengthen joblessness. In Austria, where a rule gives longer benefits6 to workers with longer work histories, extending benefits from 20 to 30 weeks lowered the rate at which people found jobs in their first 20 weeks of search by 5 to 9 per cent. Whether that adds up to a large effect across a whole economy is disputed. One group of economists credited7 the end of the long federal extensions in December 2013 with 2.1 million people finding work in 2014; another study, using a different method8, found that the extensions of the Great Recession raised the unemployment rate by at most 0.3 points. Dahl and Knepper also found that employment grew 1.5–2.4 per cent faster in the states that cut benefits, though starting salaries there were 1.8–7.2 per cent lower.

Cost is the second argument. Employers pay for the program through payroll taxes, and the state funds that hold the money are thin: on 1 January 2026, 18 states met DOL’s recommended solvency standard9, against 31 at the start of 2020. A state that pays fewer claims can keep employer taxes lower. The third is measurement. The ratio sets an administrative count against a survey estimate, and it is not a take-up rate: its denominator includes many people the program was never designed to pay.

Against that, the money does what it is meant to do for those who receive it. Jonathan Gruber found10 that consumption fell by an average of 7 per cent for workers who became unemployed, and that without unemployment insurance the fall would have been over three times as large. Peter Ganong and Pascal Noel found11 that spending drops sharply when benefits run out.

Against other rich countries

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) models what a benefit system pays12 a single person without children who earned two thirds of the average wage. In 2025, in the first month out of work, U.S. unemployment insurance replaced 55 per cent of that person’s previous net income, against an OECD average of 67 per cent with all benefits counted. After a year, U.S. unemployment insurance paid nothing; across the OECD it still replaced 34 per cent on average, and 49 per cent with social assistance and housing benefits, against 9 per cent in the United States. These are rules applied to a model household, not payments to real people, and they assume the person claims.

Method

The measure. The recipiency rate is DOL’s measure. The numerator is the number of continued weeks claimed in regular state unemployment insurance programs in a year, divided by 52. A continued week claimed is a week for which a person asks for a payment after the first. The denominator is the annual average number of unemployed people: the BLS Current Population Survey for the nation, and the BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics for a state13. The numerator does not include Extended Benefits, the federal pandemic programs, or the programs for federal employees and former service members.

The years. The national figures for 1950 to 2024 and the state figures for 1976 to 2024 are DOL’s, from its Unemployment Insurance Chartbook. The build computes the same measure from the raw files: the continued weeks claimed in DOL’s monthly ETA 5159 reports14 and BLS’s monthly unemployment counts. It agrees with DOL to within 0.05 points for every year from 2006 to 2024 for the nation, and for every state from 2020 to 2024. The build stops if it does not.

2025. BLS did not collect its household survey for October 2025, during the lapse in federal appropriations15. DOL’s published 2025 figures agree with the raw files only if October counts as zero unemployed people in a twelve-month average. This makes each of DOL’s 2025 figures 12/11 of the average of the months that BLS published. This page uses the average of the eleven published months, as BLS does for its own annual average. The national figure is thus 26.0, not DOL’s 28.4. The build checks every state’s DOL figure against this explanation.

Other figures. Job losers per 100 unemployed people and claimants per 100 job losers use the BLS survey’s count of the unemployed by reason, not seasonally adjusted, as annual averages. The share of residents uses the Census Bureau’s population estimates for July16 2025, for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The replacement rates are OECD’s, for a single person without children at 67 per cent of the average wage. The survey results on applications, the eligibility estimates and the research results are as their authors published them.

Limits

The two halves of the ratio come from different sources. Claims are administrative counts by state of the claim; the unemployed are a survey estimate by state of residence. A claimant who works part time and claims a partial week is counted as employed by the survey. In 2020, five states and the District of Columbia recorded more claimants than unemployed people, for these reasons. The ratio counts weeks claimed, not weeks paid. It also leaves out the federal programs, which in 2020 and 2021 paid many people that regular state programs did not, so it understates how many jobless people received some payment in those years. DOL computed its state figures before 2020 with BLS state counts that BLS has since revised; with the current counts, the 2016 to 2019 figures differ by up to about 1.3 points. Claimants per 100 job losers is a ratio of two counts, not a share: some claimants are not job losers in the survey’s terms. Finally, the ratio describes how far the program reaches. It does not say how far it should.
