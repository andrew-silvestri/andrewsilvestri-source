Per kilogram, a resting body puts out more power than the Sun

In 1932 Max Kleiber, an animal scientist at the University of California, found that the resting heat of mammals1 rises with body mass to about the three-quarter power. The rule became one of biology's few laws, and it has since been stretched to cells, cities, economies and stars, as if one law of size ran through all of them. This page tests that idea on one pair of axes: mass, and power per kilogram. It uses public data for 2,881 species, 509 stars, 379 US cities and the energy use of every country. Some patterns survive the test. The three-quarter law survives only inside each group of living things, and its best-known extensions, to cities and to stars, do not.

This data does not update.

  1. Across 2,881 species, from bacteria to an elephant, the resting power of each major group averages between 0.3 and 9 watts per kilogram, where one three-quarter-power law would spread them about 30,000-fold.
  2. A resting human body gives off about 6,100 times more power per kilogram than the Sun, and heavier stars give off more per kilogram, the opposite of the tilt in animals.
  3. Fuel lifts the average person to about 28 times the power of their own body, and larger US metros produce more per person while, in published fits, using about the same energy per person.

Why does life stay between 0.3 and 9 watts a kilogram?

The reference unit on this page is a resting adult: about 83 watts for a 70 kg body, or 1.2 watts per kilogram (W/kg). That is the heat of a dim incandescent bulb. In 2019 the ecologist Ian Hatton and four colleagues2 collected 8,098 measurements of resting metabolism, from bacteria and single-celled algae to trees and elephants, and published them with their code. When this page reruns their method, the average for each major group (birds, mammals, invertebrates, cold-blooded vertebrates, plants, protists) lies between 0.3 and 9 W/kg. Birds are at the top, at 8.7 W/kg; mammals average 4.3; fish, amphibians and reptiles 0.29; plants 0.31. Across about 18 orders of magnitude of eukaryote mass, a single three-quarter-power law would spread those averages about 30,000-fold. They spread about 30-fold.

Anastassia Makarieva and colleagues reported3 the same band in 2008 from a different compilation of 3,006 species: group averages of 0.3 to 9 W/kg. The tilt that Kleiber saw is real, but it lives inside each group. Fitted on Hatton's data, the exponents inside the groups run from 0.66 to 0.90, and the exponent for all eukaryotes pooled is 0.95 (95 percent interval 0.94 to 0.95), close to one. So a bigger species within a group uses less power per kilogram, while the groups themselves sit side by side on a nearly flat floor. Why each group tilts is still argued. Geoffrey West, James Brown and Brian Enquist derived three quarters in 19974 from the geometry of branching supply networks; later work found the exponent curved5 and varied from group to group. The tilt is a fitted law. The single line is not.

How firm is the band? It is well measured for what it measures, which is resting animals and plants, and it moves when that choice moves. Field metabolic rates of active animals6 run 1.5 to 7 times their resting rates, and a growing bacterium can use power at ten to twenty times its starved rate. Temperature is the other lever. Hatton's method adjusts cold-blooded species to 20 °C; at 37 °C, body temperature for a mammal, the same groups average 1 to 16 W/kg and the range narrows to about 10-fold. Individual species spread wider than the averages: the middle 98 percent of them lie between 0.04 and 23 W/kg. What survives every one of these choices is the comparison. Life's spread is tens to hundreds of times, where a single law of size would demand tens of thousands.

Evidence: strong for the band of resting rates; the exact width depends on temperature and activity.

The Sun runs cold

The arithmetic for the Sun is short enough to show in full. The International Astronomical Union fixes7 the Sun's power output, as a nominal value, at 3.828 × 1026 watts. Its mass, from the Union's solar mass parameter divided by the gravitational constant, is 1.988 × 1030 kilograms. Divide the first by the second and the Sun gives off 1.93 × 10−4 W/kg, or about 0.2 milliwatts per kilogram. A resting adult gives off 1.2 W/kg, so per kilogram the body puts out about 6,100 times more power than the Sun. The Sun is enormous because it is heavy, not because each kilogram works hard. The slowest group of animals in Hatton's table, fish, amphibians and reptiles at 0.29 W/kg, does more than a thousand times better.

Stars also tilt the other way. Zeki Eker, an astronomer8 at Akdeniz University in Turkey, and colleagues measured the masses and radii of 509 main-sequence stars in eclipsing binary pairs, from 0.18 to 31 times the Sun's mass. From their radii and temperatures, luminosity rises roughly as mass to the power 4, so power per kilogram rises as mass to the power 3. The lightest stars in the sample give off about 0.000003 W/kg; the heaviest give off 1.5 W/kg, about as much as a resting body. The reason is physical, not biological9. A heavier star squeezes its core hotter and denser, fusion runs much faster at higher temperature, and the energy leaks out faster too. An animal's tilt is set by how it supplies its cells; a star's tilt is set by gravity and the rate of fusion.

Eric Chaisson, an astrophysicist at Harvard10, has made a long table of such numbers, which he calls free energy rate density, and argues that it rises from galaxies to stars to plants to animals to society. The arithmetic of each entry is sound. As a law it is weak11: each value depends on where the boundary is drawn and which mass is used, and the entries are chosen rather than sampled. It is an ordering, and an analogy, not a fitted law.

Evidence: strong for the numbers; the opposite tilt has a known physical cause. A common law with life: none.

Fuel takes people out of the band

A body at rest uses about 83 watts. In 2025 the world used about 20,258 kilowatt-hours of primary energy per person, according to Our World in Data's compilation of the Energy Institute's statistics12. Spread over the hours of a year, that is a steady 2,300 watts for every person alive, about 28 times the power of the body. The figure is about 8,600 watts in the United States, roughly 100 times the body, and about 850 watts in India, 10 times. Counted per kilogram of the people who use it, the average person with fuel runs at 33 W/kg and the average American at 120 W/kg, above the band of every group of living things. No other species takes a step of this size away from the floor that the rest of life shares.

Evidence: strong; national energy statistics, divided by population and by a 70 kg body.

Cities are not big organisms

The most quoted extension of Kleiber's law is to cities. In 2007 Luís Bettencourt, Geoffrey West and colleagues reported13 that a city's economic output grows faster than its population, with exponents near 1.1 to 1.3, while the length of its roads and cables grows slower, near 0.8. The second finding invited the analogy with animals: a big city as a large organism, saving on its networks as an elephant does. The first finding is the opposite of an animal's tilt. The chart below repeats the economic test on current US data. Summing the Bureau of Economic Analysis's county GDP14 into 379 of the Census Bureau's 387 metropolitan areas for 2023 gives an exponent of 1.11 (95 percent interval 1.08 to 1.13). A metro ten times larger produces about 28 percent more per person.

Energy is where the organism analogy should show, and it does not. In Bettencourt's own table, household electricity in German cities scales with an exponent of 1.00 (interval 0.94 to 1.06), and in Chinese cities with 1.05. Michail Fragkias and colleagues found15 that carbon dioxide emissions of US metros scale at about 0.93, and at about 1 once density and income are held constant. A city ten times larger uses about ten times the energy, not the five or six times an organism's tilt would predict. The networks that deliver the energy are leaner in big cities; the people at the ends of them are not.

The exponents themselves are softer than animal exponents. Elsa Arcaute and colleagues16 at University College London redrew the boundaries of cities in England and Wales thousands of ways and found that most measures scaled close to one, and that the exponents that did differ from one moved with the boundary. This page's own fit has the same weakness: one year, one definition of a metro, and GDP counted where people work. It shows a gain in output with size in the United States in 2023. It does not show a law of cities.

Evidence: moderate for output rising with size in US data; strong that per-person energy does not fall the way an organism's would.

Method

Life. The data are Hatton and colleagues' Metabolism.csv (8,098 rows: mass in grams, resting or basal power in watts, temperature). The method follows their own R code. The power of cold-blooded groups and bacteria is corrected to 20 °C with an Arrhenius factor (activation energy 0.6 eV); birds and mammals are not corrected. Rows without a temperature in those groups are dropped. Each species keeps its lowest power and the mass of that row, except plants, which are not aggregated. The band is the range of the geometric means of power per kilogram of the six eukaryote groups. Exponents are ordinary least-squares fits of log power on log mass; the interval for the pooled exponent is from 1,000 bootstrap samples. The 37 °C variant changes only the reference temperature. The resting human is the 70 kg row in the same table.

People with fuel. Our World in Data's total energy supply per person (kilowatt-hours a year; updated 2026-06-30), divided by 8,766 hours, gives watts per person. Per kilogram uses a 70 kg body for every country, which is an assumption.

Stars. Eker and colleagues' table on VizieR lists 586 stars; the 509 marked for their calibration are used. Luminosity is radius squared times temperature to the fourth power, in units of the IAU nominal Sun (5,772 K, 3.828 × 1026 W). Mass in kilograms uses the IAU solar mass parameter divided by the CODATA 2018 gravitational constant17.

Cities. GDP is BEA's county GDP in current dollars (CAGDP1, line 1) for 2023, summed over the counties that the Census Bureau's 2023 population file lists in each metropolitan statistical area. Where BEA reports a Virginia county together with its independent cities, the combined value is used when every part lies in the same metro. 8 metros are left out: five in Connecticut, where BEA has no 2023 values for the planning regions the Census file uses, and three in Virginia. The fit is least squares on logarithms (R² 0.96), with a bootstrap interval. The energy exponents for cities are published fits, not this page's.

Data vintage. Hatton and colleagues, Zenodo record 3145281 (2019); Eker and colleagues (2018), VizieR J/MNRAS/479/5491; Our World in Data energy per person, years to 2025; BEA county GDP and Census metro population estimates, 2023.

Limits

The band is a statement about resting rates. Active animals, growing microbes and plants in full sun all run higher, and the bacteria in the table are mostly at rest. Mass is wet mass; in dry mass, water-rich organisms such as jellyfish would move up. National energy statistics leave out traditional biomass such as firewood, so they understate energy use in the poorest countries, and a 70 kg body for every country is a simplification. The stars are main-sequence stars between 0.18 and 31 solar masses; giants, white dwarfs and brown dwarfs are not in the sample. The city fit is one cross-section of one country, and its exponent depends on how metros are drawn.
