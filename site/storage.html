Big batteries in Texas earn most of their money in rare years, when a handful of days of extreme prices pay for half the year, and 2025 was not one of them

A grid battery makes money by charging when power is cheap and selling it back when it is dear. This page runs one on ERCOT's real prices (ERCOT runs most of the Texas grid) for every year from 2011 to 2025, and lets it know every price in advance, which gives a ceiling no real battery reaches. A battery that can deliver its full power for four hours would have earned at most $208 a year for each kilowatt of that power in 2023, but $66 in the median year. The big years were the ones with a few days of extreme prices: half of 2021's money came in 4 days, during Winter Storm Uri in February. 2025 had no such days: its best day earned $2.33 per kilowatt, and it took 72 days to earn half the year's money. Real batteries earn well under the ceiling; the fleet's income from energy in 2025 was about $14 per kilowatt. Texas now has more than 17,000 megawatts of batteries, so what they can earn swings several times over with the weather and the market's rules.

What a battery is paid for

A battery on the grid fills up when electricity is cheap, often in the middle of a sunny or windy day, and empties when it is dear, usually in the evening as the sun sets. Its profit on a day is what it sold the power for, less what it paid, less a little for the wear of cycling. This page puts a battery of one megawatt and four hours of storage on ERCOT's real-time prices, every hour since 2011, at the average of ERCOT's four trading hubs, and lets it see each month's prices in advance. No real battery knows the future, so every dollar figure here is a ceiling.

2023: $208 per kilowatt. The year this battery would have earned most, drawn one day at a time. Most days it made a little; a few it made a great deal. Its best day, 20 June, was worth $15.51 per kilowatt on its own.

What real batteries earned

ERCOT's independent market monitor reports what the whole battery fleet earned. Its chart for 2021 to 2025, read by eye, puts the fleet's income from selling energy at about $14 per kilowatt in 2025, against this page's ceiling of $45 for a two-hour battery that year, the fleet's average size being about 1.9 hours. Real batteries also sell reserve services, which this page does not model, and they cannot see the future, sit at particular points on the grid rather than at the hub average, and joined the fleet through the year. The model cannot separate those causes. The monitor's own summary of 2025: "Annual revenue per unit of ESR capacity declined significantly in 2025, falling by almost 37% from 2024 after normalization for the larger volume of ESR capacity in the market."

Batteries also change the prices they trade on. The monitor credits the growth of storage with most of the fall in price spikes during the evening solar ramp in 2025: "The increase in solar capacity primarily drove the reduction in daytime price spikes from 2024 to 2025, while growth in energy storage capacity largely drove the decline in price spikes during solar ramp periods." This page does not test that.

Method

Prices. ERCOT's historical settlement point prices2 for its hubs and load zones, real-time (15-minute, averaged to the hour) and day-ahead (hourly), one file a year from 2011, the first full year of its current market, to 17 October 2026. The battery trades at HB_HUBAVG, the average of the four trading hubs. The daylight-saving days have 23 and 25 hours, as ERCOT settled them; one 15-minute price ERCOT's 2013 file leaves out (3 November 2013, 02:00) is filled from the other three in that hour. Checked against an independent record of the same market: ERCOT's day-ahead North hub price in 2015 agrees with the on-peak trades EIA publishes3 for that hub to 2.4 per cent over the year.

The battery. A linear program4 that maximises the month's profit with every price known in advance: one megawatt, four hours of storage, 86 per cent round trip5, a $2 per megawatt hour cost on every megawatt hour through it, and no charging and discharging in the same hour. Months are solved one at a time with the charge carried over, so the battery places no value on charge left at a month's end. Checked against a price pattern whose best answer is known in closed form; it is reproduced exactly.

Days. A day's profit is the cash of its hours: sales less purchases less the cycling cost. "Days for half" sorts the days that made money from best to worst and counts how many it takes to reach half of their total. The day-ahead prices give a similar picture, with ceilings $27 to $219 per kilowatt in the same years.

Duration. The same years with one, two and eight hours of storage, real-time, at the hub average. Each extra hour adds less than the one before:

Limits

Perfect foresight is a ceiling, not a forecast: a real operator bids without knowing tomorrow's prices. The model trades energy only, at hourly average prices, so it misses the reserve markets, where batteries made most of their money until 2024, and the extra spread inside each hour. Its "four-hour" battery delivers 3.71 hours at full power, because its losses come out of the stored energy. For scale, NREL's Annual Technology Baseline6 puts the cost of owning a four-hour battery at about $242 per kilowatt a year (capital over twenty years at an assumed eight per cent, plus fixed operations). That is a cost reference, not a verdict, since a real battery has other income. This is an explainer, not investment advice.

The code

Python: the fetch script, which records where and when each ERCOT file came from; the price reader; the dispatch model; the payload builder; the page generator; and the tests. The archive carries the payload, not ERCOT's price files, which the fetch script downloads again. Also the Julia and Octave ports of the dispatch and a workbook of the same arithmetic. 233 KB.
