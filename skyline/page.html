{{h1}}

Imagine a mapmaker who asks Wikidata, the free database kept alongside Wikipedia and a source for maps, apps and research, for every building of {{floor}} meters or more in {{n_all}} big cities, and draws whatever comes back. The answer is {{cand}} records. By Wikidata's own records, {{gone}} of those buildings have been demolished or destroyed, among them the old 270 Park Avenue in New York, whose demolition it dates to {{park_year}}; another {{unbuilt}} were never finished or are still going up. That is {{wd_out}} of the {{cand}}, about one in twenty, that the mapmaker would draw and a visitor would not find standing. The database says so itself, in records that any search that does not check passes straight over. Of the rest, {{noosm}} could not be matched to a standing building in OpenStreetMap, the map drawn from the ground and from aerial imagery; many of those stand, but they are left out rather than risk drawing a tower that is not there. So how many tall buildings does a city have? That depends on what counts as a building, where a city is taken to end and how closely two volunteer databases must agree, and this page gives each of those choices with its count. The towers that pass are below as an instrument, for the {{n_full}} cities with at least {{min_ring}} of them, and for {{kept_thin}}, which fall short but stay in the list, marked as thin. Turn a wheel of a city's towers, and the towers in front of you play a chord that grows louder the taller they are.

How does a record become a standing tower?

Three heights do three jobs. The search asked for everything of {{pull_floor}} meters or more within {{radius}} kilometers of each city's center, a margin below the cut so that it missed nothing near it. A building must be {{floor}} meters or more to count or to go on a wheel, and the table of cities further down counts the buildings of {{min_h}} meters or more, the usual sense of a high-rise. From the {{items}} records that the search returned:

{{funnel_rows}}
StepRecords

Each step, in plain words:

  • Not a building. This step removes bridges, statues, monuments and similar items ({{n_notb}}), then television towers, radio masts, observation towers, and clock and church towers ({{n_tower}}). Wikidata files these under "building", though no one would call them buildings, which is why the Eiffel Tower, the CN Tower and Tokyo Skytree are not on the wheels.
  • Not in the city. Wikidata must place the tower inside the city; a tower it places only in the city's county or state must stand within {{core_km}} kilometers of the city's center. So La Défense is not in Paris, Ramat Gan is not in Tel Aviv, and Yokohama is not in Tokyo ({{n_outside}}).
  • Gone or unbuilt, by Wikidata's own record. The record has a demolition date, or it records the building as destroyed, proposed, unfinished, under construction, on hold or cancelled ({{wd_out}}).
  • Not matched in OpenStreetMap. A tower stays only if OpenStreetMap does one of two things: links one of its buildings to the Wikidata record without marking it as under construction, or maps a building within {{near_m}} meters of the record's position that matches the tower's height (or its number of floors at 3.5 meters each) or its name ({{noosm}}).
  • The same building twice. Two records for one building count as one ({{n_dup}}).

What is measured and what is invented

Measured. Each tower's height and position come from Wikidata, and whether it stands comes from Wikidata and OpenStreetMap together. The towers sit around the wheel in their true compass order, seen from the middle of the city's towers (a point moved toward the tallest). How far can the heights be trusted? OpenStreetMap gives its own height for {{osm_h}} of the towers on the wheels, and for {{osm_off}} of them it differs from Wikidata's by more than ten per cent. The wheels use Wikidata's.

Rearranged. The spacing around the wheel. The towers keep their true compass order, clockwise from north, but are spaced evenly. Seen from the middle, a city's towers cluster: half of them fall within an arc of {{arc_lo}} degrees in {{arc_lo_city}} and {{arc_hi}} in {{arc_hi_city}}, so a wheel at true angles would be crowded on one side and empty on the other. A transit map takes the same liberty with distance, for the same reason. One consequence deserves a plain statement. A wheel has no empty horizon, so a turn changes the chord and never silences it.

Invented. The sound, all of it. A city has no pitch in any sense, so the mapping is a choice, and the table below gives it in full. It is deterministic: the same city at the same bearing always makes the same sound.

How the bars become sound

In short, the taller the towers in front of you, the louder the chord. At most seven notes sound at once, and filters shape the sound so that it can never turn shrill. The details:

Property of the barProperty of the soundWhy
Position across the viewWhich note of the city's chord, lowest on the left; the lowest note is the root an octave downThe view has ninety-six bars, which is too many to play: neighboring bars on almost the same pitch would beat against each other. So the bars share the chord's nine or ten notes, and each note takes the loudest bar that feeds it.
Height, as a share of this city's tallestLoudness of that note, as the share to the power 1.6; and the level of the whole chord, set by its loudest noteThe exponent makes the peaks stand out in the ear as they do in the picture. A turn toward the tallest towers makes the chord swell. A tower half the height of the city's tallest is about ten decibels quieter when you face it. Every city's tallest tower is equally loud: its lower height does not make {{shortest_city}} quieter than {{tallest_city}}.
How many bars are litAt most seven notes sound at once, the loudest sevenA bearing that lights every note gives a chord nobody wrote, and density is most of what makes a tone like this harsh.
Which note it isHigher notes are made quieter; every fourth voice is a triangle wave, the rest are sine wavesA choice, so that the chord sounds warm, not buzzy.
EverythingA filter that cuts 12 decibels above 1.8 kHz, another that removes everything above 2.4 kHz, and a limiter, all before the level is setSo that no city at any bearing can pile its notes into a shrill tone.

Every key is a choice

Each city's key is a choice with a reason, and the reasons come in three kinds, which the app marks: a piece of music tied to the city, in its usual key; a national anthem, which belongs to the country rather than the city; or the scale family of the city's musical tradition, with the root chosen. None of them is a measurement, and the table below does not pretend otherwise.

Which notes of each mode sound is also a choice. Each mode has its own voicing, with the note that gives the mode its character placed an octave up, where it colors the chord without clashing with the root. No two notes that sound together are ever closer than three semitones. A line under the bars in the app names the notes sounding at each moment, so the instrument can be read as well as heard.

Limits

Standing is judged by agreement between two databases that volunteers edit, not by anyone looking. Many of the {{noosm}} towers that OpenStreetMap could not confirm do stand, among them Meiji University's Liberty Tower in Tokyo; OpenStreetMap maps them, but without a link, a height or a name that the match could use. The error can run the other way too, since a tower not yet built could pass if Wikidata does not mark it and a building of similar height stands at its position. The located-in test misses towers that Wikidata places nowhere useful. Within {{core_km}} kilometers of the center it accepts towers placed only in the city's county or state, and beyond that distance it rejects them. No row of the first table removes more records than "not in the city" does, so a city's count turns on where that line is drawn.

Heights are Wikidata's, and some are wrong. It gives the Empire State Building an architectural height of 453 meters, where Wikipedia gives 443.2 meters to the tip and 381 to the roof, so Wikidata's figure sits about ten meters above even the tip and 72 above the roof. On the wheel, that changes how loud one tower sounds next to New York's tallest, not which is tallest. Both databases change daily, and the searches behind this page ran on {{fetched}}. Their answers are kept. The page can be rebuilt from exactly what it was built from.
