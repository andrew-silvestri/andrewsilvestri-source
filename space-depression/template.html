No one has measured mood where a Mars crew would live

Everything known about depression in spaceflight comes from people who could see Earth out of the window. The validated mood measurements taken in flight cover about {{n_measured}} people, all in Earth orbit, none on a stay much longer than six months. A crew bound for Mars would spend two to three years away, with each message taking up to {{hi_min}} minutes to arrive. NASA rates the risk of a behavioural or psychiatric problem on that mission red, its highest band, and it has not one case diagnosed in flight to count. The rating is an extrapolation, and the map below shows how far it reaches.

What have the flights shown?

Since {{first_year}}, {{n_people}} people have made {{n_rides}} trips to orbit or beyond and spent about {{py_total}} person-years there, by the count in Jonathan McDowell's public tables. Most trips are short: {{pct_under30}} percent lasted under a month. Only {{n_gt180}} lasted more than six months, and {{n_ge365}} a year or more. The longest, {{longest_name}}'s stay on the Mir station from {{longest_year}}, ran {{longest_days}} days. That is less than half of NASA's reference mission to Mars.

Mood in flight has mostly been measured by asking. Nick Kanas, a psychiatrist at the University of California, San Francisco, and colleagues gave weekly questionnaires, drawn from a standard scale called the Profile of Mood States, to {{kanas_us}} American astronauts and {{kanas_ru}} Russian cosmonauts on joint Shuttle and Mir missions. They then repeated the study with {{kanas07}} crew members on the International Space Station. Neither study found mood falling in the second half of a mission or in any single quarter. What both found was displacement: crews directed tension and negative feeling at mission control rather than at each other. Those {{n_measured}} person-missions are close to the whole published record of validated mood scores taken in flight.

What NASA counts instead is events. Its 2025 evidence report records {{iss_n}} behavioural health instances across the first 40 expeditions to the space station, a rate of {{iss_rate}} per person-year, and notes that its medical model listed no astronaut diagnosed with anxiety or depression in flight as of {{nodx_year}}. Sleep is the clearer signal. Laura Barger of Harvard Medical School and colleagues found that station crews slept {{barger_h}} hours a night on average, and {{barger_hyp}} percent of the crew members who answered the question took sleep medication.

Evidence: thin. The questionnaire studies are careful but small, every one was in Earth orbit, and the crews knew that what they reported could affect their next flight.

What do the rehearsals on the ground show?

The longest sealed rehearsal for Mars ran {{m500_days}} days in Moscow. Six men lived in a mock spacecraft, staged a landing and worked through a delay on every message to mission control. Mathias Basner of the University of Pennsylvania and colleagues tracked them each week. One crew member reported at least one symptom of depression in {{m500_pct}} percent of mission weeks, at mild to moderate levels in more than a tenth of them. The crew reported conflicts with mission control {{m500_ctrl}} times as often as conflicts with each other. One man's sleep-wake cycle drifted to an average of {{m500_period}} hours, out of step with the habitat's 24-hour day.

The largest clinical sample comes from Antarctica. Lawrence Palinkas and colleagues interviewed {{pal_n}} people at the end of winter at McMurdo and South Pole stations between 1994 and 1997. Thirty-nine of them, {{pal_any}} percent, met the criteria for a psychiatric disorder; weighted for the low share of civilian staff who took part, the figure was {{pal_w}} percent. Mood disorders were the most common diagnoses. Those are crews who could not leave for months, which is the part of a Mars mission that Antarctica copies best.

The folklore of isolated crews says morale bottoms out just past the halfway mark, in the "third quarter". The evidence for it is mixed. Mars-500 and the Kanas studies did not find it, and a 2019 review of {{bell_n}} analog studies by Suzanne Bell and colleagues found that team mood did not consistently follow it. Yet at Concordia, the French-Italian station on the high Antarctic plateau, Gro Mjeldheim Sandal's team followed {{sandal_n}} people through two winters of about {{sandal_mo}} months. Several of their measures, coping effort most clearly, reached a low at midwinter, which was the third quarter of the stay. The authors called it "{{q_hib}}".

Evidence: moderate for the ground, weak as a stand-in for Mars. The rehearsals copy isolation and confinement. They do not copy weightlessness, deep-space radiation or the knowledge that no rescue is possible.

What changes on the way to Mars?

Distance changes three things that Earth orbit never tested. The first is length. NASA's 2009 reference architecture gives short-stay round trips of {{dra_s1}} to {{dra_s2}} days and long-stay missions of {{dra_l1}} to {{dra_l2}} days; its worked long-stay example is 210 days out, 496 at Mars and 210 back, {{dra_916}} days in all. The second is delay. Between 2033 and mid-2036, a signal takes from {{lo_min}} minutes to reach Mars at the closest approach to {{hi_min}} minutes when the planet sits behind the Sun. A call home becomes an exchange of letters. The third is silence: when the Sun lies between the planets, NASA expects communication blackouts that can last weeks. From the Moon, by contrast, a signal takes about {{moon_s}} seconds.

Time beyond Earth orbit is easier to compare in person-years: one person spending one year somewhere. Everyone who has ever gone beyond low Earth orbit, {{n_people_moon}} people on {{n_moon}} flights to the Moon, logged {{moon_pd}} person-days there, or {{py_moon}} person-years. NASA's current plan assumes no fewer than four crew members on the Martian surface. Four people on the {{dra_916}}-day mission would log 4 × {{dra_916}} = {{crew_pd}} person-days, and {{crew_pd}} divided by 365.25 is {{py_min}} person-years. One mission would add about {{x_min}} times as much time beyond Earth orbit as all of human history to date.

Evidence: strong for the distances and the delays, which follow from orbital mechanics. There is no evidence yet on what they do to mood.

How does NASA rate a risk it has not observed?

NASA scores each human health risk by likelihood and consequence, each on a scale of 1 to 5. In its current risk record, a long stay in Earth orbit scores {{rr_leo_long}} for problems during the mission. Both kinds of Mars mission score {{rr_mars}}, and the evidence report calls a Mars surface mission a red, or high, risk. Of the five spaceflight hazards it weighs, the board that sets the ratings names isolation and confinement as the main reason. The Mars likelihood is projected from the space station's event rate, a rate the record itself calls {{q_underest}}.

{{table_rating}}

Two readings fit a record of zero diagnoses. The first is selection. Astronauts are screened, trained and supported, with private conferences with psychologists during flight. If they carried the general adult rate, {{nsduh}} percent of American adults with a major depressive episode in 2025, about {{expected}} episodes would be expected across {{py_total}} person-years. That figure is only illustrative, since it treats a yearly prevalence in the whole population as a rate for a screened few. The second reading is silence. NASA's evidence report says the true rate is probably higher than the count because of astronauts' reluctance to report symptoms that could cost them a flight. Both can be true at once.

Spaceflight also gives something back. When E. C. Ihle and colleagues at the University of California, San Francisco, sent an anonymous survey to {{ihle_sent}} astronauts and cosmonauts, all {{ihle_back}} who replied reported a positive reaction to being in space, most strongly to the sight of Earth. But only {{ihle_pct}} percent replied, and the people who enjoyed a flight may be the likeliest to answer. The first crew to go will also be the first data.

Method

Flights. One dot is one person on one flight, from Jonathan McDowell's astronaut tables (rides and missions, table dated {{table_date}}). Suborbital hops are left out. A flight counts as beyond low Earth orbit when McDowell's mission table flags it as deep space; these are the Apollo flights and Artemis II. A flight still under way on the table's date counts up to that date ({{n_ongoing}} flights). Person-years are person-days divided by 365.25.

Delay. The one-way signal time is the light time that JPL Horizons gives from the centre of Earth to Mars, daily from 1 January 2033 to 30 June 2036, and to the Moon, hourly through 2033. The build checks that light time equals the distance divided by the speed of light. A real spacecraft adds relay and processing time, so these are lower bounds.

Mood measurements. A person counts as measured in flight if a peer-reviewed paper reports a standard mood questionnaire given to them during the flight: {{kanas_us}} astronauts and {{kanas_ru}} cosmonauts in Kanas 2001 and {{kanas07}} crew members in Kanas 2007. The papers do not name their subjects, so a person in both is counted twice. Diaries, stress ratings and cognitive tests do not count.

Ground rehearsals. The lengths are from each source: Mars-500 in days; CHAPEA from NASA's start and end dates, counting both; Concordia and the HI-SEAS missions in months, converted at 30.4 days a month.

Evidence ratings. The ratings are this page's judgement. Strong: a measurement or a physical law settles it. Moderate: real data, but from a setting that differs from the one in question. Thin: well-designed studies with too few people or the wrong setting to settle it.

Limits

This page summarises evidence; it gives no medical advice. The flight counts include every crewed flight to orbit, so they mix week-long trips with year-long stays, and a dot's position says nothing about that person's health. The in-flight mood count leaves out results that are unpublished, held in NASA's controlled-access archives, or published only in Russian. The ground rehearsals used different questionnaires and cannot be pooled. NASA's ratings are a board's judgement from the evidence it holds, not a measured rate.
