Texas batteries wait for scarcity years, and {{last_year}} was not one

Imagine a battery in Texas that never guesses wrong. It knows ERCOT's real prices for the coming month before it acts, charges when power is cheapest and sells it back when power is dearest, and it trades this way through every year from 2011 to {{last_year}} (ERCOT's grid serves about 90 per cent of Texas's electricity demand). A Texas battery's best years are made by a few days of scarcity, and its headline value by one assumption, that it knows the prices in advance.

How does a battery earn?

A battery on the grid charges when electricity is cheap, often in the middle of a sunny or windy day, and discharges when it is dear, usually in the evening as the sun sets. Its daily profit is its sales, less its purchases, less a small cost for the wear of cycling. The model puts a battery of {{mw}} megawatt with four hours of storage on ERCOT's real-time prices for every hour since 2011, trading at the average of ERCOT's four trading hubs and seeing the coming month's prices before it acts. No real battery knows the future. So every dollar figure here is a ceiling for trading energy at the hub, not an estimate of what any battery took home.

Is the ceiling high? For scale, ownership of a battery like this costs about ${{atb}} per kilowatt a year (NREL's Annual Technology Baseline), which is more than the ceiling in any year since 2011. That does not make every Texas battery a loss, because real batteries also earn payments to stand ready as reserves, which this page does not model. It does mean that on this cost reference, trading energy alone would not have paid for ownership in any year, even with perfect foresight.

What did real batteries earn?

ERCOT's independent market monitor reports what the whole battery fleet earned (it calls batteries ESRs, energy storage resources), but only as a chart for 2021 to 2025, with no table behind it. Read by eye, the chart puts the fleet's energy income at about ${{f_energy_last}} per kilowatt in {{fleet_last}}. That is an estimate read off a picture, not a measurement. This page's ceiling that year for a two-hour battery, about the fleet's average size ({{fleet_hours}} hours), is ${{ceil2_last}}. So real batteries got roughly a {{fleet_frac}} of what perfect foresight at the hub allows.

Why the gap? Part of it is that real batteries cannot see the future. Part is that they sit at their own points on the grid, and some joined the fleet during the year. Part is that they keep charge back for reserve payments, which pay for standing ready rather than for trading. The monitor on {{fleet_last}}: "{{q_fleet}}"

Batteries also move the prices they trade on. In the monitor's words: "{{q_ramp}}" That is the market monitor's finding; this page does not test it. If it holds, the batteries are competing away the very spikes they earn from.

Method

Prices. The data is ERCOT's historical settlement point prices for its hubs and load zones: real-time (15-minute, averaged to the hour) and day-ahead (hourly). There is one file for each year from 2011, the first full year of ERCOT's current market, to {{through_long}}. The battery trades at HB_HUBAVG, the average of the four trading hubs, and the daylight-saving days have 23 and 25 hours, as ERCOT settled them. ERCOT's 2013 file does not include one 15-minute price (3 November 2013, the first 02:00 hour), so the model fills it from the other three in that hour. How do we know the files are read correctly? The check uses an independent record of the same market, the on-peak trades EIA publishes for the North hub: ERCOT's day-ahead North hub price in 2015 agrees with them to {{ice_pct}} per cent over the year.

The battery. The model is an optimisation, a linear program with one yes-or-no choice per hour, that maximises profit over 30 days at a time and knows every price in that window in advance. The battery has {{mw}} megawatt of power and four hours of storage. Its round-trip efficiency, the share of the energy put in that it gives back, is {{rte}} per cent. Its cycling cost is ${{cdeg_leg}} per megawatt hour charged and the same discharged, and it cannot charge and discharge in the same hour. Each year starts half full, and each 30-day window carries its charge into the next, so the battery places no value on charge left at the end of a window. A test with a price pattern whose best answer is known in closed form gives that answer exactly.

Days. The profit of a day is the cash of its hours: sales, less purchases, less the cycling cost. "Days for half" sorts the days that made money from best to worst and counts how many of those days it takes to reach half of their total. "Share from best 10 days" is the share of the same total that comes from the ten best days. Does the choice of market change the answer? On day-ahead prices the ceilings are ${{dam_lo}} to ${{dam_hi}} per kilowatt, and the five best years are the same five{{dam_note}}.

Duration. The model also runs the same years with one, two and eight hours of storage, on real-time prices at the hub average. Each extra hour of storage adds less than the one before:

And real batteries settle at their own grid nodes, which can be further apart still.

Limits

Perfect foresight gives a ceiling, not a forecast: a real operator bids without knowledge of tomorrow's prices. The model trades energy only, at hourly average prices, so it does not include the extra spread inside each hour or the reserve markets, where batteries earned most of their money until 2024 (the monitor: "{{q_as}}"). Its four hours of storage give about {{ac_hours}} usable hours at full power, because its losses come from the stored energy.

The ownership cost above is NREL's capital and fixed operations cost for a four-hour battery, with the capital spread over twenty years at an assumed eight per cent. NREL gives these batteries a 15-year life, and over 15 years the same arithmetic gives about ${{atb15}}. Either figure is a cost reference, not a verdict, because a real battery has other income. This is an explainer, not investment advice.
