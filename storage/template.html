What a battery is paid for

A battery on the grid fills up when electricity is cheap, often in the middle of a sunny or windy day, and empties when it is dear, usually in the evening as the sun sets. Its profit on a day is what it sold the power for, less what it paid, less a little for the wear of cycling. This page puts a battery of {{mw}} megawatt with four hours of storage on ERCOT's real-time prices, every hour since 2011, at the average of ERCOT's four trading hubs, and lets it see the coming month's prices before it acts. No real battery knows the future, so every dollar figure here is a ceiling for trading energy at the hub. For scale, owning a battery like this costs about ${{atb}} per kilowatt a year (NREL's Annual Technology Baseline), more than the ceiling in any year since 2011; real batteries also earn from being paid to stand ready as reserves, which this page does not model.

What real batteries earned

ERCOT's independent market monitor reports what the whole battery fleet earned, calling batteries ESRs, energy storage resources. Its chart for 2021 to 2025 has no table; read by eye, it puts the fleet's income from selling energy at about ${{f_energy_last}} per kilowatt in {{fleet_last}}. This page's ceiling for a two-hour battery that year, about the fleet's average size ({{fleet_hours}} hours), is ${{ceil2_last}}, so real batteries took in roughly a {{fleet_frac}} of what perfect foresight at the hub allows. Some of the gap is that they cannot see the future; some, that they sit at their own points on the grid and joined the fleet through the year; some, that they hold charge back to be paid as reserves. Method

Prices. ERCOT's historical settlement point prices for its hubs and load zones, real-time (15-minute, averaged to the hour) and day-ahead (hourly), one file a year from 2011, the first full year of its current market, to {{through_long}}. The battery trades at HB_HUBAVG, the average of the four trading hubs. The daylight-saving days have 23 and 25 hours, as ERCOT settled them; one 15-minute price ERCOT's 2013 file leaves out (3 November 2013, the first 02:00 hour) is filled from the other three in that hour. Checked against an independent record of the same market: ERCOT's day-ahead North hub price in 2015 agrees with the on-peak trades EIA publishes for that hub to {{ice_pct}} per cent over the year.

The battery. An optimisation, a linear program with one yes-or-no choice per hour, that maximises profit over 30 days at a time with every price in that window known in advance: {{mw}} megawatt, four hours of storage, {{rte}} per cent round-trip efficiency (the share of the energy put in that comes back out), a cycling cost of ${{cdeg_leg}} per megawatt hour charged and the same discharged, and no charging and discharging in the same hour. Each year starts half full, and each 30-day window carries its charge into the next, so the battery places no value on charge left at a window's end. Checked against a price pattern whose best answer is known in closed form; it is reproduced exactly.

Days. A day's profit is the cash of its hours: sales less purchases less the cycling cost. "Days for half" sorts the days that made money from best to worst and counts how many it takes to reach half of their total; "share from best 10 days" is the same total's share in its ten best days. On day-ahead prices the ceilings run ${{dam_lo}} to ${{dam_hi}} per kilowatt, and the five best years are the same five{{dam_note}}.

Duration. The same years with one, two and eight hours of storage, real-time, at the hub average. Each extra hour of storage adds less than the one before:

Location. The same four-hour battery at each hub and load zone: in {{z_hi_year}}, from ${{z1_lo}} ({{z1_lo_name}}) to ${{z1_hi}} ({{z1_hi_name}}); in {{z_lo_year}}, from ${{z2_lo}} ({{z2_lo_name}}) to ${{z2_hi}} ({{z2_hi_name}}). The hub average sits inside both ranges, so a battery at a well-placed zone could earn more than this page's ceiling. Real batteries settle at their own grid nodes, which can be further apart still.

Limits

Perfect foresight is a ceiling, not a forecast: a real operator bids without knowing tomorrow's prices. The model trades energy only, at hourly average prices, so it misses the reserve markets, where batteries earned most of their money until 2024 (the monitor: "{{q_as}}"), and the extra spread inside each hour. Its four hours of storage give about {{ac_hours}} usable hours at full power, because its losses come out of the stored energy. The ownership cost above is NREL's capital and fixed operations cost for a four-hour battery, with the capital spread over twenty years at an assumed eight per cent; NREL gives these batteries a 15-year life, and over 15 years the same arithmetic gives about ${{atb15}}. Either is a cost reference, not a verdict, since a real battery has other income. This is an explainer, not investment advice.
