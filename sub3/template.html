Most runners on pace for a sub-3 marathon at halfway still miss it

On 29 September 2024, {{y24_n}} runners passed halfway in the Berlin Marathon between 1:28:30 and 1:29:59. They were on schedule to finish in under three hours, the line that many amateur marathoners treat as the mark of a serious runner. Half of them made it. A year later, on a morning about {{dt}} °C warmer, {{y25_n}} runners passed the same point in the same 90 seconds, and fewer than one in five made it. Over all {{years_n}} races from {{first}} to {{last}}, {{band_pct}} percent of the runners who reached halfway in that window finished under 3:00. The ones who missed lost almost all of their time after 25 km.

A schedule that holds for half a race

The pattern is not a quirk of one year. In {{years_less1}} of the {{years_n}} races, most of the runners who passed halfway in 1:28:30 to 1:29:59 finished slower than 3:00; in the other, 2024, it was an even split. Their success rate ranged from {{yr_lo}} percent in {{yr_lo_y}} to {{yr_hi}} percent in {{yr_hi_y}}, and it has been {{recent_pct}} percent since 2019. Look from the other end and the picture is the same. Of the {{near_n}} runners with a halfway split who finished between 3:00:00 and 3:04:59, {{near_pct}} percent had passed halfway under 1:30. Most near misses were on schedule at halfway.

Many of these runners were aiming at 3:00 on purpose. In 9.5 million marathon finishes, Eric Allen and colleagues found 51.4 percent more runners in the minute before three hours than in the minute after it, the mark of a target that people push for. So the halfway group is full of people with the same plan. About one in three carried it out.

What three hours asks for

The marathon is 42.195 km, so three hours means {{pace3}} a kilometre ({{mile3}} a mile), or {{split5}} for every 5 km, held from the start to the finish. In Berlin, a flat course on which the men's world record was broken repeatedly from 2003 to 2022, {{all_pct}} percent of the {{all_n}} finishers since {{first}} did it: {{m_pct}} percent of men and {{w_pct}} percent of women. Jack Daniels and Jimmy Gilbert's running formula, which many coaches use, gives a 3:00 marathon the same fitness as a {{half3}} half marathon run on its own. That is about three minutes faster than the halfway split of the runners on this page.

Physiology says why the gap is needed. Michael Joyner's model of the marathon divides the oxygen a runner can use for the whole race (their maximum, VO2max, times the fraction of it they can hold) by the oxygen each kilometre costs. Elite marathoners hold about 75 to 85 percent of VO2max; in a 1983 laboratory study of 28 finishers of one marathon, the slowest held about 60. But the model describes a rested runner, and nobody runs the last 10 km rested.

Why the second half is different

Two things change as the race goes on, and laboratory work on runners near this level measures both. The first is what physiologists call durability. In 2025 Michele Zanini and colleagues at Loughborough University tested 14 well-trained male marathoners, whose recent marathons averaged 2:47, fresh and again after 90 and 120 minutes of running at an average of 14.1 km/h, almost exactly 3:00 pace. Their speed at the lactate threshold, the point at which lactate starts to build up in the blood, fell from 14.0 km/h fresh to 13.5 after 90 minutes and 13.0 after two hours. In race data from 82,303 marathoners, Barry Smyth and colleagues found that heart rate began to drift up against pace at about 25 km on average.

The second is fuel, and the arithmetic is worth doing once. Running costs about 1 kilocalorie per kilogram of body weight per kilometre, so a 70 kg runner uses about 2,950 kcal over a marathon, whatever the pace. At 3:00 pace a trained runner works hard enough for carbohydrate to supply about three quarters of that, the share measured at 72 percent of VO2max in trained cyclists. Three quarters of 2,950 is about 2,200 kcal, and at about 4 kcal a gram that is about 550 grams of carbohydrate. Benjamin Rapoport's model of marathon fuel puts the stores of a lean 70 kg male runner at about 400 grams (88 in the liver, about 310 in the leg muscles). These are estimates, not measurements, and they change with body size, diet and pace. But the shortfall is about 150 grams, and without carbohydrate taken during the race the stores run low in its last third.

The estimate lines up with race records. In 4.2 million of them, Smyth found that about a third of runners hit the wall, defined as slowing by at least a quarter for 5 km or more. On average it began at about 29.5 km. Sports nutrition guidelines advise loading with carbohydrate for a day or two before the race and taking up to 90 grams an hour during events longer than about 2.5 hours. The direct evidence at this level is thin: in the nearest controlled test, seven runners on a treadmill in 1995 finished a marathon about four minutes faster with a sugar drink than with water.

Heat takes it out of the second half

Berlin is run in September, usually on the last weekend, and the weather on race morning varies more than the course does. The chart gives, for each race, the share of the halfway group who finished under 3:00, with the hottest race mornings in a separate colour. The temperatures are hourly readings from the German weather service (DWD) at Berlin-Tempelhof, averaged from 09:00 to 13:00.

{{chart_weather}}

Across the {{years_n}} races, the warmer the morning, the fewer of these runners made it: about {{slope}} percentage points fewer for each degree, a correlation of {{r_temp}}. At {{t24}} °C in 2024, {{y24_pct}} percent made it; at {{t25}} °C in 2025, the warmest race morning of the {{years_n}}, {{y25_pct}} percent did. But the Berlin figures are {{years_n}} points, not an experiment, and the field has grown and changed over the same years; the lowest rate of all, in {{yr_lo_y}}, came on a morning that was not hot. Larger studies agree on the direction. In 1.8 million finishes at six major marathons, Berlin among them, Nour El Helou and colleagues found that air temperature was the weather factor that mattered most, and that runners were fastest at roughly 4 to 10 °C. Matthew Ely and colleagues found that slower runners lost more in the heat than the leaders did, a finding El Helou's group did not reproduce across all finishers.

What the numbers do not show

An expert would object to one thing first. The runners who missed may have been less fit than those who made it, and a better schedule would not have saved them. Faster runners pace more evenly in the large studies of marathon splits, so some of the link runs from fitness to pacing and not the other way. Of the runners in the halfway window who had already finished an earlier Berlin under 3:00, only {{rep_pct}} percent did it again (in {{rep_n}} races), little better than the rest; but a former sub-3 runner who now passes halfway in 1:29 may be past their best. And in Strava data from more than 25,000 runners, Smyth and Daniel Muniz-Pumares estimated each runner's sustainable speed from training: a start faster than that speed raised the share who slowed badly, from 17 to 23 percent.

The evidence has three gaps. The data counts finishers only, so the runners who passed halfway at 1:29 and then dropped out are missing, and the true success rate is lower than {{band_pct}} percent. It cannot say which runners were aiming at 3:00. And experiments on pacing are few: a 2025 review of 24 trials found that no imposed strategy, even pacing included, beat letting athletes pace themselves. This page's reading is therefore narrow. A halfway split on a 3:00 schedule leaves no room for the fade that almost every runner has, and the margin that protects against it is fitness, not a faster first half: a runner who can race a half marathon in about {{half3}} passes halfway in 1:29 with something in hand.

For most amateurs the next line is a qualifying standard. To enter the Boston Marathon in 2027 a man aged 18 to 34 needed 2:55:00 and a woman 3:25:00, and so many applied that runners had to beat their standard by 5 minutes 17 seconds. At the top, Sabastian Sawe of Kenya ran 1:59:30 in London on 26 April 2026, the first marathon under two hours in a record-eligible race, at {{wr_pace}} a kilometre; on the same course that day Tigst Assefa of Ethiopia ran 2:15:41, a record for a women-only race. Most modern men's world records have been run with a second half as fast as the first, or faster. At that level the fade has been trained out of the race.

Method

Data. The results are the Berlin Marathon file that Aldo Seffrin, Beat Knechtle and colleagues published on Zenodo in 2026 under a CC BY 4.0 licence: {{rows}} official results from {{first}} to {{last}}, with net times and splits every 5 km and at halfway. There was no race in 2020. A finisher is a result with a finish time ({{all_n}}); the file holds almost no other results, so runners who dropped out are not in it. The halfway window is 1:28:30 to 1:29:59; an even second half from there finishes between 2:57:00 and 2:59:58. The build reads the file only after it matches the checksum that the Zenodo record publishes, and it never writes a runner's name.

Calculations. The second-half slowdown is the second-half time minus the first-half time, divided by the first-half time. The section paces in the table use runners with every 5-km split. The check on earlier sub-3 finishers links a runner across years by name, nationality and sex; a name that appears twice in one year is left out of the linking. The weather series is the mean of DWD's five hourly air temperatures at station 433, Berlin-Tempelhof, from 09:00 to 13:00 local time; DWD's station file gives these readings in UTC, and the build converts them. The race dates are from the race's records. The VDOT figures use Daniels and Gilbert's published equations for the oxygen cost of running speed and the fraction of VO2max held for a given time.

Limits

Berlin is one flat race, usually run in mild weather, and its rates are likely higher than those of a hilly or hot course. The data counts finishers only. The runners in the halfway window were not all aiming at 3:00, and the data cannot say who was. Shoes, field size and the share of fast runners changed over the years; this page does not separate these from the weather, so the weather correlation is a description, not an estimate of cause. The fuel arithmetic uses typical values from laboratory studies of a few dozen people, and the physiology studies cited are small. This page is an evidence summary, not a training plan or medical advice.
