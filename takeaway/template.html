When the pipe is full

Permian gas production set against the pipeline capacity that carries it out of the basin, from public data, and what venting and flaring did as the gap closed and reopened.

Most of the gas in the Permian Basin comes up with oil. In {{csgd_year}}, {{csgd_pct}} per cent of the gas the Texas Railroad Commission recorded in the basin's three districts came from oil wells, and an oil well does not stop producing gas because the gas has nowhere to go. When the pipelines out of the basin are full, the price at the Waha hub can fall below zero, and gas that cannot be moved is burned or released at the lease. The Commission's own account of flaring says as much: in existing fields, flaring "may be necessary because existing pipelines may have reached capacity".

This page asks how close production runs to the capacity that carries it out, and what happens to venting and flaring as that gap closes and reopens around each new pipe. It uses only public data: EIA's estimate of the region's production, a dated list of pipeline capacity built from EIA's project tracker and dated reports of when lines started, and the venting and flaring Texas operators report to the Commission.

What it finds

Pipe has been added in steps, and most of what is still to come is only expected. EIA, quoting PointLogic, put the basin's takeaway capacity at {{anchor_lo}} to {{anchor_hi}} Bcf/d in {{anchor_month}}. Since then {{n_conf}} additions are in service, {{conf_total}} Bcf/d between them, the largest Matterhorn Express at {{matterhorn}} Bcf/d. Another {{exp_total}} Bcf/d is expected by {{exp_last}}, {{exp_by}} Bcf/d of it by the end of {{exp_by_year}}. None of the expected lines is counted as in service until an opened source says it is.

Production and capacity cannot be drawn on one axis. EIA's production figure is marketed gas, measured before the natural gas liquids are taken out and before gas is burned in the field and at processing plants, and it already exceeded the capacity estimate in {{anchor_month}} ({{P_anchor}} against {{anchor_lo}} to {{anchor_hi}} Bcf/d). What can be compared is the change. From a month when the pipes are taken to be full, the pipe added since, less the share of the production added since that has to leave by pipe, is the headroom gained or lost.

Taking {{t0_long}} as that month, {{cap_t0}} Bcf/d of pipe has been added and production has risen {{dP_t0}} Bcf/d, of which {{kdP_t0}} has to leave by pipe at the default share, k = {{k_def}}. That leaves {{H_last}} Bcf/d of headroom gained by {{last_long}}. It was not a steady gain. After Matterhorn the headroom stood at {{H_after_m}} Bcf/d; production growth then ate it, and at the default k it was below zero in {{n_neg}} months ({{neg_months}}) and below zero at the top of k's range in {{n_neg_hi}}. The {{last_long}} figure includes the full {{hb1}} Bcf/d of Hugh Brinson's first phase, which entered commercial service in August and was expected to reach that by September 1; it is counted from September.

Whether {{t0_long}} really was a full month decides how to read that line, and two things outside the model were set down beforehand as the test of it. If the pipes were full, production should have been held back before Matterhorn and released after it. It was not: production grew {{pin_before}} Bcf/d over the three months to {{t0_long}} and {{pin_after}} Bcf/d over the three after Matterhorn opened. If the pipes were full, Texas operators should have vented or flared more before than after. They did: {{fc_before}} per cent of gross withdrawals over {{fc_before_span}} against {{fc_after}} per cent over {{fc_after_span}} ({{fc_missing}} are not yet in the series). One test for and one against, so the reference month is not established. The line is best read as the pipe added less the gas added since {{t0_long}}, not as spare capacity.

Venting and flaring fell after the two earlier pipes the data can test. Across Texas, the share of gross withdrawals vented or flared averaged {{c1_before}} per cent in the {{window_word}} months before Gulf Coast Express started in {{c1_start}} and {{c1_after}} per cent in the {{window_word}} after; around Permian Highway and Whistler in {{c2_year}} it went from {{c2_before}} to {{c2_after}} per cent. Each fall is larger than every one of the {{placebo_n}} before-and-after differences at months clear of any new pipe, but those months all fall between {{pl_first}} and {{pl_last}}, years when the share was rising, so the comparison leans towards making the falls look unusual. Neither is evidence that the pipe caused it: the 2019 window runs into the price collapse and shut-ins of 2020, and the 2021 window opens on that year and takes in the February 2021 winter storm. The months around Matterhorn cannot be tested this way yet, because the monthly series ends in {{vf_last_long}}.

Prices are not drawn here, because no free daily series for Waha exists. The counts that are published measure different things and are left apart. EIA, from Natural Gas Intelligence data, put Waha "below zero for {{eia_pct}}% of trading days in {{eia_year}}". Reuters counted daily averages below zero {{r_2024}} times in 2024, {{r_2025}} times in 2025 and {{r_2026}} times in 2026 by {{r_date}}, and attributed that last run to "pipeline constraints from spring maintenance", which a capacity list like this one does not contain.

Method

Production. EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, {{steo_release}} release, Table 5a, series NGMPPM: marketed natural gas, Permian region, monthly, history to {{last_long}}. The region is drawn by county, {{n_tx_counties}} in Texas and {{n_nm_counties}} in New Mexico. Its forecast months are not used. The Railroad Commission's figures for the Permian, which it defines as districts 7C, 08 and 8A, are Texas only and measured at the lease before any gas is taken out; they were checked against EIA month by month over {{rec_n}} months ({{rec_first}} to {{rec_last}}), and the ratio of the two held between {{rec_min}} and {{rec_max}} (mean {{rec_mean}}). The two are different measures with a stable relation, so EIA's is used for every comparison with capacity, because the pipes carry New Mexico gas too.

Capacity. A line is counted when it starts in the basin and a cited source describes it as carrying gas out of the Permian. Lines inside the basin, lines that start downstream of it, and laterals are not. Capacity and dates are read from EIA's pipeline projects tracker; where a newer report gives a date, it is used and the source named. Where sources disagree on the month a line started, the later month is used. A line is "in service" only where an opened source says it is; every other date is an expectation and is shown as one.

The share k. Not every unit of marketed gas leaves by pipe: the liquids are taken out, and some gas is burned in the field and at the plants. EIA gives both by state. For {{k_year}}, dry gas less lease and plant fuel was {{k_both_net}} of marketed production across Texas and New Mexico; Texas alone {{k_tx_net}}, and dry gas alone {{k_both_dry}}. The default is the first, and the band in the headroom figure runs between the other two. These are statewide ratios standing in for the Permian, and both known errors push the true value lower: associated gas is wetter than the states' average, and gas burned in the basin by power plants and industry is not removed. A k that is too high makes the headroom look smaller, so the default leans towards finding the pipes full.

The reference month. EIA reported on {{eia_t0_date}} that Waha had been below zero "every day since July 26", so {{t0_long}} falls wholly inside that run; September does not, because Matterhorn was expected to begin service that month. The app moves it a month either way.

Venting and flaring. EIA's monthly series for Texas, which it takes from state agency data, vented and flared over gross withdrawals, {{vf_first_long}} to {{vf_last_long}}. It is statewide, not the three Permian districts alone. Before-and-after windows are {{window_word}} months each, so each pair covers the same calendar months; starts less than {{window_word}} months apart are taken together, and a group is tested only if both its windows are complete and hold no other start.

Limits

The production figure is an estimate, and EIA revises its latest months. The capacity anchor is a third-party estimate whose definition is not published, and the additions follow a narrower rule than it does; the anchor cancels out of the headroom, the rule does not. Capacity is nameplate and enters as a full step, while lines ramp up over months and maintenance takes capacity out. The reference month is an inference from prices, and one of the two tests set for it went against it. The venting and flaring series is statewide. Several in-service dates rest on trade press reporting what the pipelines' owners said, because no primary filing could be opened. The page forecasts nothing, and it names no company as the cause of anything.
