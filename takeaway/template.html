Each new Permian pipeline buys the basin only a year or so

Imagine an oil well in West Texas on a day when every pipe out of the Permian is full. It was drilled for oil, but gas comes up with the oil whether or not there is a pipe to carry it away, as it does from oil wells across West Texas and New Mexico. At the Waha hub, where the basin's gas is traded, the price has fallen below zero, so the well's owner pays to have the gas taken away — and keeps pumping, because the oil is what pays. Pipelines out of the basin arrive every few years, each about the size of one or two years' growth. So each new one holds only a year to a year and a half of the basin's gas growth, and then the well is back where it started.

Gas grows every year, pipe in steps

That well is the basin in miniature: gas grows every year, and pipe arrives in steps. The chart sets the two side by side, both measured from {{t0_long}}, the month EIA reported Waha's price below zero "every day since July 26". One is pipe capacity out of the basin, rising a step each time a line enters service. The other is the gas that needs a pipe out, which is production times k, the share not removed as liquids or burned as fuel in the field and at plants. How big is k? That depends on whose ratio is used, so the chart draws the gas twice — once with the statewide figure that EIA's data give ({{k_def}}), and once with the lower share that makes mid-2019, when EIA said the pipes were constrained, as full as {{t0_long}} ({{kfit}}). Where the pipe line runs above the gas lines, there is room; where it runs below them, there is more gas than this accounting can place. Beneath the chart sit the days when Waha's price was below zero and the share of gas vented or flared in the basin's three Texas districts.

Why does the gas keep coming?

Because most of it is a by-product. In {{csgd_year}}, {{csgd_pct}} per cent of the gas the Railroad Commission recorded in the basin's three districts came from oil wells, which are drilled for oil and bring the gas up with it. Reuters put the economics in one sentence: "{{q_losses}}" So a low or even negative gas price does not, by itself, stop the oil. An operator can shut a well in, as many did in 2020, but that stops the oil too. When the pipes are full, then, the gas that cannot be moved is sold at whatever Waha pays, or burned or released at the lease. The Commission's own account of flaring says that in existing fields it "may be necessary because existing pipelines may have reached capacity" — the regulator, in other words, names the same bottleneck this page measures.

And the by-product grows. Since {{pace_from_year}}, the gas that needs a pipe out has grown by {{pace_def}} Bcf/d a year at the default share ({{pace_low}} at the lowest share this page considers), and every calendar year added at least {{yr_min}} Bcf/d except 2020, when operators reduced activity and it added {{yr_2020}}. The four big lines built since 2019 each added {{big_lo}} to {{big_hi}} Bcf/d, which is {{months_lo}} to {{months_hi}} months of that growth. How long each one actually lasted on the chart is another matter, because the lines did not arrive evenly. Gulf Coast Express took {{rec_gcx}} months to fill, over the 2020 slowdown. Permian Highway and Whistler, six months apart, took {{rec_php}} and {{rec_wh}} months; Matterhorn Express {{rec_mh}}.

Full pipes show in the price, not the flare

EIA describes the mechanism: "{{q_mechanism}}" Do the years agree with it? They do. Reuters dates Waha's first below-zero daily average to {{first_neg}}, the year Gulf Coast Express opened, and counts {{n2020}} in 2020. It lists no such day in 2021 or 2022, when the chart shows at least {{room2122}} Bcf/d of room every month. It then counts {{n2023}} in 2023, {{n2024}} in 2024 as the room disappeared, {{n2025}} in 2025 and {{n2026}} in 2026 by {{r_date}} — a count that includes a record run of {{r_run}} days in a row, which Reuters attributed to "pipeline constraints from spring maintenance". A capacity list like this one does not contain maintenance, which is one reason the price can turn before the chart does. Negative prices, in Reuters's description, are ones "{{q_pay}}". That is the well at the top of this page.

Venting and flaring tell a different story after 2019. In the three Texas districts, {{vf2019_bcfd}} Bcf/d was vented or flared in 2019 ({{vf2019}} per cent of the gas produced there). In every year from 2021 to {{vf_lastyear}} the share stayed between {{vf_min_since}} and {{vf_max_since}} per cent, even as gross output at the lease there (the Commission's measure, not EIA's) increased from {{gross2021}} to {{gross_last}} Bcf/d, and even as the below-zero days returned. As the pipes filled again, the share moved only from {{vf2022}} per cent in 2022 to {{vf2023}} in 2023 and {{vf2024}} in 2024. The full pipes of 2024 to 2026 show in the price far more than in flaring. Why? The data cannot say. Reuters offers a view, in its own words: "{{q_flare}}"

Each fall in flaring after a new line can also be tested against ordinary months, using the average of the {{window_word}} months before and after each start. Around Gulf Coast Express, the share fell from {{c1_before}} to {{c1_after}} per cent; around Permian Highway and Whistler, from {{c2_before}} to {{c2_after}}; and around the group that ends with Matterhorn, from {{c3_before}} to {{c3_after}}. Of {{placebo_n}} before-and-after differences at months clear of any start, none fell as far as the 2019 one, while {{c2_rank}} fell further than the 2021 group's and {{c3_rank}} fell further than the Matterhorn group's. Only the first stands out. Even that does not show that a pipe caused a fall: the 2019 window includes the price collapse and shut-ins of 2020, and the 2021 window includes the February 2021 winter storm.

But the sign changes with the choices, so the page does not say which side they are on: whether the basin has a little room or a little too much gas turns on choices such as the share k, which a single headline number would hide. A negative reading needs a word of its own. Pipes cannot carry more than their capacity, so a negative reading means that this accounting has missed a place where the gas went: lines that run above their nameplate, capacity added without a project of its own, more gas burned in the basin, or a smaller share that needs a pipe.

Appendix: the check on 2019

The chart uses the same arithmetic back to {{hero_from_year}}, and there it shows room that the prices did not. In {{ref19_long}}, the month before Gulf Coast Express began taking gas, EIA wrote that takeaway "{{q_eia2019}}". Yet the default reading gives {{ref19_def}} Bcf/d of room that month, and EIA's own Permian ratio (k = {{kp_net}}) still gives {{ref19_kp}}. A share that makes {{ref19_long}} exactly as full as {{t0_long}} is {{kfit}}; it moves to {{kfit_nosm}} without South Mainline, an ambiguous westbound line, and to {{kfit_dbl}} if Double E, which EIA counted as takeaway in 2021, is counted too. That share is too sensitive to print as a measurement. But every version is below the default, and the price evidence leans the same way. With the lower share, the pipes in {{last_long}} are slightly emptier than in {{t0_long}}, not fuller ({{H_fit}} Bcf/d in the table). That is why the page gives no sign.

Method

Production. The source is EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, {{steo_release}} release, Table 5a, series NGMPPM: marketed natural gas, Permian region, monthly, history to {{last_long}}. The page does not use its forecast months, and its latest months are estimates. The region is drawn by county, {{n_tx_counties}} in Texas and {{n_nm_counties}} in New Mexico. The workbook does not reach the months before {{steo_first_long}}, so for those months the chart uses EIA's last Drilling Productivity Report (total gas, the same counties) times {{splice}}. This is the mean ratio of the two over the {{splice_n}} months they share (range {{splice_lo}} to {{splice_hi}}). The ratio increases over those months, so, applied to 2018 and 2019, when more gas was flared, it slightly overstates production in those years and understates the 2019 discrepancy. It is fixed at that value, and the monthly refresh cannot change it. The Railroad Commission's Permian figures (districts 7C, 08 and 8A, Texas only, measured at the lease) were checked against EIA's over {{rec_n}} months, and the ratio stayed between {{rec_min}} and {{rec_max}}.

The share k. Not every unit of marketed gas leaves by pipe: liquids are removed, and some gas is burned in the field and at plants. EIA gives both by state. For {{k_year}}, dry gas less lease and plant fuel was {{k_both_net}} of marketed production across Texas and New Mexico; this is the default. For Texas alone it was {{k_tx_net}}, and for dry gas alone {{k_both_dry}}. These are statewide ratios standing in for the Permian. EIA's Permian-only figures (STEO Table 10b, shale formations, over Table 5a) give {{kp_dry}}, or {{kp_net}} less fuel, but that ratio is biased low, because the formations table does not include conventional gas.

Capacity. A line counts when it starts in the basin and a cited source says that it carries gas out of the Permian; lines inside the basin, lines that start downstream, and laterals do not. Capacity and dates come from EIA's project tracker, by exact name. Where a newer report gives a date, the page uses that date and names the report, and where sources disagree on a start, it uses the later month. A line is in service only where an opened source says so. Before May 2022, the chart counts the lines in the second table, by the same rule; South Mainline and Permian North, westbound with no note in the tracker, are the ambiguous ones. The arithmetic for room uses only changes, so the level of capacity never enters it.

Venting and flaring. The source is the Railroad Commission's Production Data Query dump, extracted {{vf_extract}}. The share is gas reported vented or flared (disposition code 4, which does not separate the two) over gas-well and casinghead production, in districts 7C, 08 and 8A. The page does not use the {{unsettled_word}} most recent months, because late reports still change them. Each before-and-after window is {{window_word}} months, starts less than {{window_word}} months apart are taken together, and a start is a line of at least {{event_min}} Bcf/d.

Limits

The production figure is an estimate, and EIA revises its latest months. Capacity is nameplate and enters as a full step, but lines increase their flow gradually and maintenance removes capacity, so prices can go below zero while the chart still shows room. The reference month is an inference from prices. The share k is a statewide ratio, and the check on 2019 says it is probably too high for this purpose. Venting and flaring are what operators report at the lease; the page does not count flaring at gathering systems and plants (Form R-3). Several in-service dates come from trade press reports of what the pipelines' owners said. The page forecasts nothing and names no company as the cause of anything.
