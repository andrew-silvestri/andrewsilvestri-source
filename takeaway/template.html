Permian gas fills each new pipeline in a year to a year and a half, and then sellers pay to get rid of it

Oil wells in West Texas and New Mexico bring gas up with the oil whether or not there is a pipe to carry it away. Pipelines out of the basin arrive every few years; production fills each one, and when they are full the price at the Waha hub, where the basin's gas is traded, falls below zero.

Gas grows every year; pipe arrives in steps

The chart sets two things against each other, both measured from {{t0_long}}, the month EIA reported Waha's price below zero "every day since July 26". One is pipe capacity out of the basin; each step is a line entering service. The other is the gas that needs a pipe out: production, times the share k of it that is not taken out as liquids or burned as fuel in the field and at plants. That share is uncertain, so the gas is drawn twice: at the statewide figure EIA's data give ({{k_def}}), and at the lower share that makes mid-2019, when EIA said the pipes were constrained, as full as {{t0_long}} ({{kfit}}). Where the pipe line is above the gas lines there is room; where it is below them, there is more gas than this accounting can place. Underneath are the days Waha's price went below zero and the share of gas vented or flared in the basin's three Texas districts.

Why the gas keeps coming

In {{csgd_year}}, {{csgd_pct}} per cent of the gas the Railroad Commission recorded in the basin's three districts came from oil wells. Those wells are drilled for oil, and the gas comes up with it. Reuters put the economics in one sentence: "{{q_losses}}" A low or negative gas price does not, by itself, stop the oil. An operator can shut a well in, as many did in 2020, but that stops the oil too. When the pipes are full, the gas that cannot be moved is sold at whatever Waha pays, or burned or released at the lease; the Commission's own account of flaring says that in existing fields it "may be necessary because existing pipelines may have reached capacity".

Since {{pace_from_year}}, the gas that needs a pipe out has grown by {{pace_def}} Bcf/d a year at the default share, or {{pace_low}} at the lowest share this page considers. Every calendar year added at least {{yr_min}} Bcf/d except 2020, when operators cut back and it added {{yr_2020}}. The four big lines built since 2019 each added {{big_lo}} to {{big_hi}} Bcf/d, which is {{months_lo}} to {{months_hi}} months of that growth. How long each actually lasted on the chart differs, because the lines did not arrive evenly: Gulf Coast Express took {{rec_gcx}} months to be used up, over the 2020 slowdown; Permian Highway and Whistler, six months apart, {{rec_php}} and {{rec_wh}}; Matterhorn Express {{rec_mh}}.

Prices and flaring

EIA describes the mechanism: "{{q_mechanism}}" The years line up with it. Reuters dates Waha's first below-zero daily average to {{first_neg}}, the year before Gulf Coast Express; it lists no such day in 2021 or 2022, the years that hold the chart's widest room; then {{n2023}} in 2023, {{n2024}} in 2024 as the room ran out, {{n2025}} in 2025 and {{n2026}} in 2026 by {{r_date}}, including a record run of {{r_run}} days in a row that it put down to "pipeline constraints from spring maintenance", which a capacity list like this one does not contain. Negative prices, in Reuters's words, "{{q_pay}}".

Venting and flaring tell a different story after 2019. In the three Texas districts, {{vf2019_bcfd}} Bcf/d was vented or flared in 2019, {{vf2019}} per cent of the gas produced there. In every year from 2021 to {{vf_lastyear}} it stayed between {{vf_min_since}} and {{vf_max_since}} per cent, even as output rose from {{gross2021}} to {{gross_last}} Bcf/d and the below-zero days returned. As the pipes refilled from 2022 to 2024 the share moved from {{vf2022}} to {{vf2024}} per cent. So the full pipes of 2024 to 2026 show in the price far more than in flaring. The data cannot say why. Reuters offers a view, in its own words: "{{q_flare}}"

Appendix: the check on 2019

The chart runs back to {{hero_from_year}} on the same arithmetic, and there it shows room the prices did not. In {{ref19_long}}, the month before Gulf Coast Express began taking gas, EIA wrote that takeaway "{{q_eia2019}}". The default reading gives {{ref19_def}} Bcf/d of room that month; with EIA's own Permian ratio (k = {{kp_net}}) it gives {{ref19_kp}}. A share that makes {{ref19_long}} exactly as full as {{t0_long}} is {{kfit}}; it moves to {{kfit_nosm}} without South Mainline, an ambiguous westbound line, and to {{kfit_dbl}} if Double E, which EIA counted as takeaway in 2021, is counted too. That is too sensitive to print as a measurement, but every version is below the default, and the price evidence leans the same way. With the lower share the pipes in {{last_long}} come out slightly emptier than in {{t0_long}} rather than fuller ({{H_fit}} Bcf/d in the table), which is why the page gives no sign.

Method

Production. EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, {{steo_release}} release, Table 5a, series NGMPPM: marketed natural gas, Permian region, monthly, history to {{last_long}}; its forecast months are not used, and its latest months are estimates. The region is drawn by county, {{n_tx_counties}} in Texas and {{n_nm_counties}} in New Mexico. Before {{steo_first_long}}, which the workbook does not reach, the chart uses EIA's last Drilling Productivity Report (total gas, the same counties) times {{splice}}, the mean ratio of the two over the {{splice_n}} months they share (range {{splice_lo}} to {{splice_hi}}). The ratio drifts up over those months, so applying it to 2018 and 2019, when more gas was flared, slightly overstates production then and understates the 2019 discrepancy. The ratio is fixed at that value so the monthly refresh cannot move it. The Railroad Commission's Permian figures (districts 7C, 08 and 8A, Texas only, measured at the lease) were checked against EIA's over {{rec_n}} months; the ratio held between {{rec_min}} and {{rec_max}}.

The share k. Not every unit of marketed gas leaves by pipe: liquids are taken out, and some gas is burned in the field and at plants. EIA gives both by state; for {{k_year}}, dry gas less lease and plant fuel was {{k_both_net}} of marketed production across Texas and New Mexico, the default; Texas alone {{k_tx_net}}, and dry gas alone {{k_both_dry}}. These are statewide ratios standing in for the Permian. EIA's Permian-only figures (STEO Table 10b, shale formations, over Table 5a) give {{kp_dry}}, or {{kp_net}} less fuel; that ratio is biased low, because the formations table leaves out conventional gas.

Capacity. A line counts when it starts in the basin and a cited source describes it as carrying gas out of the Permian; lines inside the basin, lines that start downstream, and laterals do not. Capacity and dates are read from EIA's project tracker by exact name; where a newer report gives a date, it is used and named, and where sources disagree on a start, the later month is used. A line is in service only where an opened source says so. Before May 2022 the chart counts the lines in the second table, by the same rule; South Mainline and Permian North, westbound with no note in the tracker, are the ambiguous ones. The headroom arithmetic uses changes only, so the level of capacity never enters it.

Limits

The production figure is an estimate and EIA revises its latest months. Capacity is nameplate and enters as a full step, while lines ramp up and maintenance takes capacity out, so prices can go below zero while the chart still shows room. The reference month is an inference from prices. The share k is a statewide ratio and the check on 2019 says it is probably too high for this purpose. Venting and flaring are what operators report at the lease; flaring at gathering systems and plants (Form R-3) is not counted. Several in-service dates rest on trade press reporting what the pipelines' owners said. The page forecasts nothing and names no company as the cause of anything.
