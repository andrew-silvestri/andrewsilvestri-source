Customers of ten Texas and Oklahoma gas utilities are paying off Winter Storm Uri's gas costs until as late as {{ok_last_year}}: ${{lo_month}} to ${{hi_month}} a month for a home, and over a third of the total is interest

In February 2021, during Winter Storm Uri, the price utilities paid for gas jumped; the Oklahoma Corporation Commission's order for one utility describes "{{q_100x}}". Passed on at once, the costs would have pushed monthly home bills "{{q_1000}}", the Commission found for Oklahoma Natural Gas. Instead, under laws both states passed in 2021, the costs were financed with bonds, which spread them over many years of bills, with interest, rather than one winter's. Customers of eight Texas utilities and of Oklahoma Natural Gas and Summit Utilities in Oklahoma repay them through a charge on their gas bills. ${{to_util_bn}} billion went to the utilities; customers repay ${{repay_bn}} billion, ${{int_bn}} billion of it interest. ${{paid_bn}} billion has been paid and ${{left_bn}} billion is still to come.

Who pays, and how

In Texas the law sets one charge for everyone: "{{q_tx_uniform}}" of the participating utilities. Future customers pay it too, whether or not they lived there in 2021. It is charged per unit of gas, inside the gas-cost line of the bill, to the utilities' sales customers, the ones who buy their gas from the utility, and it is reset at least once a year so that collections meet the payments: {{tx_steps}}. Customers who buy their gas elsewhere and pay the utility only to deliver it are not charged. One division's tariff on file differs: Atmos Energy's West Texas division shows ${{tx_wt}} per hundred cubic feet from June 2026, almost twice the others. Its filing does not say why, and this page has not found out.

In Oklahoma the charge is a flat amount on each monthly bill, the same in a cold month as a mild one, and each class of customer carries a share set, in both of the Commission's orders, "{{q_alloc}}". Those shares can be reset; Oklahoma Natural Gas's have been ({{ong_a_alloc0}} of the total for its smaller-use homes at issue, {{ong_a_alloc1}} in the March 2026 true-up). Both orders leave out customers who were on the utility's voluntary fixed-price plan in February 2021 and customers in the Low Income Heating Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP). Oklahoma Natural Gas divides homes by yearly use, under or over 50 dekatherms, a measure of the gas's heat rather than its volume; Oklahoma's average home used {{ok_mcf}} thousand cubic feet a year over {{tx_y0}} to {{tx_y1}}.

Method

Payments. Texas: the official statement's expected amortization schedule by tranche (A-1 ${{tx_a1}} at {{tx_c1}}, A-2 ${{tx_a2}} at {{tx_c2}}), with interest computed here on the scheduled balance from the closing date, March 23, 2023, on the bonds' 30/360 basis, first paid September 1, 2023 and then each April 1 and October 1. The check: the weighted average lives this gives, {{tx_wal1}} and {{tx_wal2}} years, are the ones the statement prints, and the balance at December 31, 2025 equals the issuer's report to the dollar. Oklahoma: the Issuance Advice Letters' expected sinking fund schedules, row by row; every interest figure in them is reproduced from the coupon and the balance to under a cent. What customers repay: Texas, principal less the reserve, plus interest, less the interest paid from the proceeds; In Texas the first interest payment, ${{tx_prepaid_m}} million on September 1, 2023, was paid from an account funded out of the proceeds, before any charge was billed, and a reserve of ${{tx_reserve_m}} million, also held back from the proceeds, pays the last instalment; customers repay both through the principal, so neither is counted again. The account held six months of interest, about ${{tx_six_m}} million at the coupons, against the ${{tx_prepaid_m}} million the first payment needed; the statement says unused up-front funds reduce the first reset of the charge, so the Texas total may be up to about ${{tx_unused_m}} million lower. Oklahoma, principal plus interest (the reserve funds, ${{ok_reserve_m}} million together, are not netted, since what was opened does not say how they are used; it would change the total by {{ok_reserve_pct}} per cent). Ongoing costs (trustee, servicer, the issuers' expenses), about ${{ongoing_m}} million a year across the three, are paid through the same charges and are not in the totals above.

A home. Texas: the filed rate in each year, from October, then today's to the last bill, times EIA's residential consumption per residential consumer averaged over {{tx_y0}} to {{tx_y1}} ({{tx_mcf}} thousand cubic feet a year; {{tx_y1}} alone was {{tx_mcf_last}}). The charge is stated at a pressure base of {{p_tx}} pounds per square inch and EIA counts gas at {{q_eia_base}}, so the rate is multiplied by {{p_eia}}/{{p_tx}}: ${{tx_rate_eia}} per thousand cubic feet, which is what CenterPoint's tariff states at {{p_eia}} (${{cnp_1473}} per hundred). Texas Gas Service says its average home pays about ${{tgs_avg}} a month at ${{tx_rate}}. Oklahoma: the filings' own mechanism, a class's share of the scheduled payments and the stated ongoing costs, divided by the class's customers when the charge was set. That is the figure the page gives, because it is how the charges are set; counted instead as today's charge times the months of the term, the totals are ${{onga_check}}, ${{ongb_check}} and ${{suo_check}}, different because the charge has been above and below today's. A home's interest share counts the ongoing costs in what is repaid.

Limits

This page adds up what the filings fix. It does not follow the payments on to the sellers of the gas, and it does not weigh bonds against other ways the costs could have been recovered, which the commissions' orders and staff reports do. It covers only the gas utilities' bond issues, and leaves out gas customers of Texas utilities that did not take part, customers who buy their gas from someone else and have it delivered (Texas's charge is on sales customers), the parts of Texas Gas Service's area its tariff excludes, Oklahoma utilities whose rates the Corporation Commission does not set, such as city-owned systems, which a separate law covers, and every class but homes in the totals. Payments are as scheduled; collections can run ahead or behind, and the true-ups catch them up.
